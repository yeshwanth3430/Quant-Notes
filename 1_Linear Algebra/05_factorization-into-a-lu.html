1. The lecture in five questions

Words used here

Inverse $A^{-1}$ = the matrix that undoes $A$. Transpose $A^T$ = rows turned into columns. $L$ = lower triangular (zeros above the diagonal) holding the multipliers. $U$ = upper triangular (zeros below the diagonal), the staircase left after elimination. Operation = one multiply-and-subtract. Permutation matrix = the identity with its rows reordered; it swaps rows.

#QuestionShort answer
1What is the inverse of $AB$?$B^{-1}A^{-1}$: the inverses in reverse order.
2What is the inverse of $A^T$?$(A^{-1})^T$: transpose the inverse.
3What is $A = LU$?Elimination as a formula: $L$ is lower triangular (holds the multipliers), $U$ is upper triangular (the staircase).
4How much work is elimination?About $\tfrac13 n^3$ steps for an $n \times n$ matrix, plus $n^2$ per right-hand side.
5What about row swaps?They are permutation matrices. A $3 \times 3$ has 6 of them, and $P^{-1} = P^T$.

2. Inverse of a product

Words used here

Product $AB$ = do $B$ first, then $A$ (when they act on a vector $x$: $ABx$ means $B$ acts, then $A$). Inverse $A^{-1}$ = the undo matrix: $AA^{-1} = I$. Identity $I$ = the "do nothing" matrix (1s on the diagonal, 0s elsewhere). Associative law = in a long product you may move the brackets, but never change the order.

Rule $$ (AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1} $$

Undo the steps in reverse order.

Story: socks and shoes

Getting dressed: first socks ($B$), then shoes ($A$). To undo it you take off the shoes first ($A^{-1}$), then the socks ($B^{-1}$). The last thing done is the first thing undone.

Why it works: multiply and let the middle cancel.

  1. What we do Multiply $AB$ by our guess $B^{-1}A^{-1}$.

    Why If the answer is $I$, the guess really is the undo of $AB$.

    How Write it out: $(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1})$. Move the brackets (associative law): $A\,(BB^{-1})\,A^{-1}$.

    What we have now $B$ and its undo $B^{-1}$ sit side by side in the middle.

  2. What we do Cancel the middle: $BB^{-1} = I$.

    How $A\,(BB^{-1})\,A^{-1} = A\,I\,A^{-1}$.

    What we have now $A\,I\,A^{-1}$.

  3. What we do Drop the $I$: doing nothing changes nothing.

    How $A\,I = A$, so $A\,I\,A^{-1} = AA^{-1}$.

  4. What we do Cancel again: $AA^{-1} = I$.

    What we have now $(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = I$. So $B^{-1}A^{-1}$ is the inverse of $AB$.

$$ (AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A\,(BB^{-1})\,A^{-1} = A\,I\,A^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I $$
Example: check with numbers $$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix},\ A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}; \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix},\ B^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Rule for multiplying: each row of the left matrix tells how much of each row of the right matrix to add up.

  1. What we do Find $AB$, one row at a time.

    Row 1 Row 1 of $A$ is $(1, 1)$: take $1 \times (1, 0)$ plus $1 \times (2, 1)$. That is $(1 + 2,\ 0 + 1) = (3, 1)$.

    Row 2 Row 2 of $A$ is $(0, 1)$: take $0 \times (1, 0)$ plus $1 \times (2, 1)$. That is $(0 + 2,\ 0 + 1) = (2, 1)$.

    What we have now $AB = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

  2. What we do Find $B^{-1}A^{-1}$ (the reverse order).

    Row 1 Row 1 of $B^{-1}$ is $(1, 0)$: $1 \times (1, -1) + 0 \times (0, 1) = (1, -1)$.

    Row 2 Row 2 of $B^{-1}$ is $(-2, 1)$: $-2 \times (1, -1) = (-2, 2)$. Add $1 \times (0, 1)$: $(-2 + 0,\ 2 + 1) = (-2, 3)$.

    What we have now $B^{-1}A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

  3. What we do For comparison, find the wrong order $A^{-1}B^{-1}$.

    Row 1 Row 1 of $A^{-1}$ is $(1, -1)$: $1 \times (1, 0) = (1, 0)$; minus $1 \times (-2, 1)$ adds $(2, -1)$; total $(1 + 2,\ 0 - 1) = (3, -1)$.

    Row 2 Row 2 of $A^{-1}$ is $(0, 1)$: $0 + 1 \times (-2, 1) = (-2, 1)$.

    What we have now The three products side by side:

    ProductRow 1Row 2Result
    $AB$$1 \times (1, 0) + 1 \times (2, 1) = (3, 1)$$0 \times (1, 0) + 1 \times (2, 1) = (2, 1)$$\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$
    $B^{-1}A^{-1}$ ✓$1 \times (1, -1) + 0 = (1, -1)$$-2 \times (1, -1) + 1 \times (0, 1) = (-2, 3)$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$
    $A^{-1}B^{-1}$ ✗$1 \times (1, 0) - 1 \times (-2, 1) = (3, -1)$$0 + 1 \times (-2, 1) = (-2, 1)$$\begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$

  4. Check $(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1})$, row 1: $3 \times (1, -1) = (3, -3)$; plus $1 \times (-2, 3)$: $(3 - 2,\ -3 + 3) = (1, 0)$ ✓.

    Check Row 2: $2 \times (1, -1) = (2, -2)$; plus $1 \times (-2, 3)$: $(2 - 2,\ -2 + 3) = (0, 1)$ ✓. That is $I$.

    Wrong order $(AB)(A^{-1}B^{-1})$, row 1: $3 \times (3, -1) = (9, -3)$; plus $1 \times (-2, 1)$: $(7, -2)$. Not $(1, 0)$ ✗.

    What we have now Only the reverse order $B^{-1}A^{-1}$ undoes $AB$.

3. Transpose, and its inverse

Words used here

Transpose $A^T$ = rows turned into columns (row 1 of $A$ becomes column 1 of $A^T$). Inverse $A^{-1}$ = the undo matrix, $AA^{-1} = I$. Identity $I$ = 1s on the diagonal, 0s elsewhere. Reverse order = in a product, the last factor comes first after flipping.

Definition: Transpose

The transpose $A^T$ turns rows into columns: row 1 of $A$ becomes column 1 of $A^T$, and so on.

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix} $$

A $2 \times 3$ matrix becomes $3 \times 2$. The identity stays the same: $I^T = I$.

Two rules

Transpose of a product also reverses the order: $(AB)^T = B^TA^T$.

Inverse of a transpose = transpose of the inverse: $(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T$. You can transpose and invert in either order.

Why the second rule holds:

  1. What we do Start from a fact we know: $AA^{-1} = I$.

    Why This is what "inverse" means: $A^{-1}$ undoes $A$.

  2. What we do Transpose both sides.

    Why If two matrices are equal, their transposes are equal too.

    How $(AA^{-1})^T = I^T$.

  3. Right side $I^T = I$: the identity's rows and columns are the same.

  4. Left side Transpose of a product reverses the order: $(AA^{-1})^T = (A^{-1})^TA^T$.

    What we have now $(A^{-1})^TA^T = I$. So $(A^{-1})^T$ is exactly what undoes $A^T$: $(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T$.

Example

From Lecture 04: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$ has $A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

  1. What we do Transpose $A$: rows become columns.

    How Row 1 $(1, 3)$ becomes column 1. Row 2 $(2, 7)$ becomes column 2.

    What we have now $A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$.

  2. What we do Transpose $A^{-1}$ the same way.

    How Row 1 $(7, -3)$ becomes column 1. Row 2 $(-2, 1)$ becomes column 2.

    What we have now The rule says $(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & -2 \\ -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

  3. Check $A^T$ times our answer, row 1: $1 \times (7, -2) = (7, -2)$; plus $2 \times (-3, 1) = (-6, 2)$; total $(7 - 6,\ -2 + 2) = (1, 0)$ ✓.

    Check Row 2: $3 \times (7, -2) = (21, -6)$; plus $7 \times (-3, 1) = (-21, 7)$; total $(21 - 21,\ -6 + 7) = (0, 1)$ ✓.

    What we have now That is exactly the inverse found for $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$ in Lecture 04, Example 7 ✓.

4. A = LU for a 2 × 2

Words used here

Pivot = the diagonal number used to clear the numbers under it. Multiplier = (number to remove) ÷ (pivot). Elimination matrix $E_{21}$ = identity with minus the multiplier in spot (row 2, column 1); it does "row 2 − multiplier × row 1". Its inverse $E_{21}^{-1}$ = the same with a plus sign; it adds the row back. $U$ = upper triangular (zeros below the diagonal). $L$ = lower triangular (zeros above the diagonal, 1s on it). $D$ = diagonal matrix (numbers only on the diagonal).

So far elimination has been written as $EA = U$ (step matrices on the left of $A$). Now we move them to the other side, to get $A$ alone.

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 8 & 7 \end{bmatrix} $$

  1. What we do Pick the pivot: the $2$ in row 1, column 1. Under it sits an $8$ that must become $0$.

    Why A zero under the pivot leaves row 2 with only one unknown: the staircase shape $U$.

  2. What we do Find the multiplier.

    Why this amount "How many 2's make 8?" $8 \div 2 = 4$. So we subtract 4 copies of row 1.

    What we have now Multiplier $\ell_{21} = 4$. Remember it: it goes into $L$ later.

  3. What we do Row 2 minus 4 × row 1.

    How 4 copies of row 1: $4 \times (2, 1) = (8, 4)$. Then subtract box by box: $8 - 8 = 0$ and $7 - 4 = 3$.

    col 1col 2
    Row 2$8$$7$
    minus 4 × row 1$-8$$-4$
    New row 2$0$$3$

    What we have now $U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. Pivots $2$ and $3$ on the diagonal.

  4. What we do Write that step as a matrix $E_{21}$.

    How Start from the identity and put $-4$ in spot (2, 1): row 2 of $E_{21}$ is $(-4, 1)$, which means "$-4 \times$ row 1 $+ 1 \times$ row 2".

    $$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{E_{21}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 8 & 7 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{U} $$

  5. What we do Move $E_{21}$ across to the other side.

    Why We want $A$ alone on one side, written from simple pieces.

    How The undo of "subtract 4 × row 1" is "add 4 × row 1", so $E_{21}^{-1}$ has $+4$ in spot (2, 1). Multiply both sides on the left by it: $E_{21}^{-1}E_{21}A = E_{21}^{-1}U$. On the left, $E_{21}^{-1}E_{21} = I$, leaving $A$.

    $$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 8 & 7 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{L = E_{21}^{-1}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{U} $$

    What we have now $A = LU$, and $L$ holds the multiplier $4$ with a plus sign.

  6. Check Rows of $L$ mix rows of $U$. Row 1 of $L$ is $(1, 0)$: $1 \times (2, 1) + 0 \times (0, 3) = (2, 1)$ ✓.

    Check Row 2 of $L$ is $(4, 1)$: $4 \times (2, 1) = (8, 4)$. Add $1 \times (0, 3)$: $(8 + 0,\ 4 + 3) = (8, 7)$ ✓.

Meet each factor on its own

L alone

$L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Lower triangular: the only non-zero numbers are on the diagonal or below it. The diagonal is all 1s. Below the diagonal sit the multipliers, each in the spot it cleared: $4$ in spot (2, 1) because we did "row 2 − 4 × row 1". In words: $L$ is the notebook of what we subtracted.

U alone

$U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. Upper triangular: zeros below the diagonal. It is simply the matrix left over when elimination finishes. The diagonal holds the pivots $2$ and $3$. In words: $U$ is the staircase left behind.

D alone

$D = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. Diagonal: numbers only on the diagonal, zeros everywhere else. It holds just the pivots. Multiplying by $D$ stretches row 1 by $2$ and row 2 by $3$, nothing else. In words: $D$ is the list of pivot sizes.

Splitting out the pivots: A = LDU

$U$ has pivots on its diagonal while $L$ has 1s. To balance them, pull the pivots out into a diagonal matrix $D$: divide each row of $U$ by its pivot.

  1. What we do Read the pivots off the diagonal of $U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$: they are $2$ and $3$.

    What we have now $D = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

  2. What we do Divide row 1 of $U$ by its pivot $2$.

    Why So that the new $U$ has a $1$ on the diagonal, like $L$.

    How $(2, 1) \div 2 = (2 \div 2,\ 1 \div 2) = (1, \tfrac12)$.

  3. What we do Divide row 2 of $U$ by its pivot $3$.

    How $(0, 3) \div 3 = (0 \div 3,\ 3 \div 3) = (0, 1)$.

    What we have now New $U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \tfrac12 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Both outer matrices have 1s on the diagonal.

    $$ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 8 & 7 \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{L} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{D \text{ (pivots)}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \tfrac12 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{new } U} $$

  4. Check $D$ times new $U$, row 1: $2 \times (1, \tfrac12) = (2, 1)$ ✓. Row 2: $3 \times (0, 1) = (0, 3)$ ✓. That is the old $U$ again, so $LDU = LU = A$.

5. A = LU for a 3 × 3

Words used here

$E_{21}$, $E_{31}$, $E_{32}$ = elimination matrices; $E_{ij}$ makes a zero in spot (row $i$, column $j$). Multiplier = (number to remove) ÷ (pivot). $U$ = upper triangular: what is left after elimination. $L$ = lower triangular: 1s on the diagonal, multipliers below. Inverse = undo. Reverse order = undo the last step first. $b$ = right-hand side (known), $x$ = unknown, $c$ = middle stop. Forward substitution = solve from the top row down. Back substitution = solve from the bottom row up.

With three rows there are up to three steps: clear (row 2, col 1), then (row 3, col 1), then (row 3, col 2):

$$ E_{32}\,E_{31}\,E_{21}\,A = U $$

Move them across one at a time. Their inverses come out in reverse order (socks and shoes):

  1. What we do Remove the outermost step $E_{32}$ first.

    Why It is the one furthest left, the last thing done to $A$.

    How Multiply both sides on the left by $E_{32}^{-1}$: $E_{31}E_{21}A = E_{32}^{-1}U$.

  2. What we do Now remove $E_{31}$.

    How Multiply both sides on the left by $E_{31}^{-1}$: $E_{21}A = E_{31}^{-1}E_{32}^{-1}U$.

  3. What we do Finally remove $E_{21}$.

    How Multiply both sides on the left by $E_{21}^{-1}$: $A = E_{21}^{-1}E_{31}^{-1}E_{32}^{-1}U$.

    What we have now The three inverses together make $L$:

    $$ A = \underbrace{E_{21}^{-1}\,E_{31}^{-1}\,E_{32}^{-1}}_{L}\,U $$

Worked example: the matrix from Lecture 03

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 8 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

We find the two matrices one at a time: first $U$, then $L$.

    How 3 copies of row 1: $3 \times (1, 2, 1) = (3, 6, 3)$. Subtract box by box: $3 - 3 = 0$, $8 - 6 = 2$, $1 - 3 = -2$.

    What we see It is already $0$.

    Why this amount $0 \div 1 = 0$. We subtract 0 × row 1, which changes nothing.

    How $3 \times (1, 2, 1) = (3, 6, 3)$. Add $(0, 2, -2)$: $3 + 0 = 3$, $6 + 2 = 8$, $3 - 2 = 1$.

    How $2 \times (0, 2, -2) = (0, 4, -4)$. Add $(0, 0, 5)$: $0 + 0 = 0$, $4 + 0 = 4$, $-4 + 5 = 1$.

    Check $(0, 4, 1)$ = row 3 of $A$ ✓.

  4. What we have now All three rows rebuilt:

Story: a shopping receipt

You start with a full bag of groceries ($A$). You take some items out ($U$ is what is left). The receipt of what you took out is $L$. With the leftover bag and the receipt, you can always rebuild the full bag. That is $A = LU$.

The big result

If no row swaps are needed:

$$ A = LU $$

where $U$ is the staircase from elimination and $L$ is lower triangular with 1s on the diagonal and each multiplier sitting in the spot it cleared. No extra work is needed to build $L$: just write down the multipliers.

Solving with L and U

The idea: two machines in a row

$A = LU$ means: doing $A$ to a vector is the same as doing $U$ first and then $L$. Give the "middle stop" a name, $c$:

Why bother? Each step uses a triangular matrix, and triangular systems are easy: the first (or last) equation has only one unknown, so you solve one line at a time.

Step 1: find c from Lc = b (top to bottom)

Here $L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $b = (2, 12, 2)$. Write $Lc = b$ as three equations, one per row of $L$:

Why this is useful

If tomorrow you get a new $b$ with the same $A$, you do not redo elimination. Keep $L$ and $U$, and just repeat step 1 and step 2 with the new $b$. That is the cheap part ($\approx n^2$ work, section 7).

6. Why L is so easy

Words used here

$E$ = the product of all elimination matrices; it goes forward: $EA = U$. $L$ = the lower triangular matrix that goes backward: $A = LU$. Multiplier = (number to remove) ÷ (pivot). $U$ = the upper triangular staircase. "Tangled" = a number in $E$ that mixes two multipliers together (like $10 = 5 \times 2$).

We can store the elimination steps in two ways:

FormulaDirection
$E$$EA = U$forward: turns $A$ into $U$
$L$$A = LU$backward: rebuilds $A$ from $U$

Both hold the same information. But $E$ gets messy numbers, while $L$ is just the multipliers. Let's see why on one concrete matrix.

The example

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 & 3 \\ 0 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} $$

Elimination on A:

  1. What we do Make a 0 in row 2, column 1.

    Why this amount Pivot $1$, number to remove $2$: $2 \div 1 = 2$. Subtract 2 × row 1.

    How $2 \times (1, 1, 1) = (2, 2, 2)$. Then $(2 - 2,\ 3 - 2,\ 3 - 2) = (0, 1, 1)$.

    What we have now New row 2 $= (0, 1, 1)$. Multiplier $2$.

  2. What we do Make a 0 in row 3, column 1.

    What we see Row 3 already has $0$ in column 1. $0 \div 1 = 0$: nothing to do.

    What we have now Multiplier $0$.

  3. What we do Make a 0 in row 3, column 2.

    Why this amount Pivot = the $1$ in new row 2, column 2. Number to remove = $5$. $5 \div 1 = 5$. Subtract 5 × (new row 2).

    How $5 \times (0, 1, 1) = (0, 5, 5)$. Then $(0 - 0,\ 5 - 5,\ 6 - 5) = (0, 0, 1)$.

    What we have now Multiplier $5$. All three steps together:

Rule

To get $L$: during elimination, write each multiplier in the spot it cleared, and put 1s on the diagonal. That's it.

Practical bonus

A computer doing elimination only needs to keep two things: the new rows (they become $U$) and the multipliers (they become $L$). Once row 2 is finished, the original row 2 of $A$ is never needed again, so the computer can overwrite $A$ with $L$ and $U$ and save memory.

7. How much work is elimination?

Words used here

Operation = one multiply-and-subtract (like $7 - 4 \times 1$). $n \times n$ = a square matrix with $n$ rows and $n$ columns. $n^2 = n \times n$; $n^3 = n \times n \times n$. Stage = clearing one column below its pivot. Right-hand side $b$ = the known numbers; each new $b$ needs its own forward and back substitution.

Count one "operation" as one multiply-and-subtract. Take an $n \times n$ matrix with no zeros.

  1. What we do Count stage 1: clear column 1.

    How There are $n - 1$ rows below row 1, and each has about $n$ numbers to change. That is $(n - 1) \times n$, roughly $n^2$.

    What we have now Cost $\approx n^2$. A smaller $(n - 1) \times (n - 1)$ block is left to clean.

  2. What we do Count stage 2: clear column 2.

    Why smaller Row 1 and column 1 are finished, so we only work in the $(n - 1) \times (n - 1)$ block.

    What we have now Cost $\approx (n - 1)^2$. Then stage 3 costs $(n - 2)^2$, and so on, down to $1$.

    StageWhat changesCost (about)
    Clear column 1all rows below row 1: roughly the whole $n \times n$ block$n^2$
    Clear column 2the remaining $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ block$(n-1)^2$
    Clear column 3the remaining $(n-2) \times (n-2)$ block$(n-2)^2$
    ………
    Last$1 \times 1$$1$

  3. What we do Add all stages.

    $$ \text{Total} \approx n^2 + (n-1)^2 + \dots + 2^2 + 1^2 \approx \tfrac13 n^3 $$

    Why a third $n$ stages, each up to $n^2$, would give $n^3$, but the blocks keep shrinking. It is like the calculus fact $\int_0^n x^2\,dx = \tfrac13 n^3$.

  4. Check Try $n = 10$ exactly: $1 + 4 = 5$, $5 + 9 = 14$, $14 + 16 = 30$, $30 + 25 = 55$, $55 + 36 = 91$, $91 + 49 = 140$, $140 + 64 = 204$, $204 + 81 = 285$, $285 + 100 = 385$.

    Compare $\tfrac13 \times 10^3 = 1000 \div 3 \approx 333$. Close, and it gets closer as $n$ grows:

    $n$Exact sum $1^2 + \dots + n^2$$\tfrac13 n^3$
    $10$$385$$\approx 333$
    $100$$338{,}350$$\approx 333{,}333$
    $1000$$333{,}833{,}500$$\approx 333{,}333{,}333$

  5. What we do Count the work for one right-hand side $b$.

    How Forward substitution: row $k$ of $L$ uses $k - 1$ numbers already found, so about $1 + 2 + \dots + n \approx \tfrac12 n^2$. Back substitution with $U$: the same, about $\tfrac12 n^2$. Together: $\tfrac12 n^2 + \tfrac12 n^2 = n^2$.

    What we have now Factoring $\approx \tfrac13 n^3$ (once). Each $b$ $\approx n^2$.

  6. What if n doubles $(2n)^3 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times n^3 = 8n^3$: factoring takes 8 times as long. $(2n)^2 = 2 \times 2 \times n^2 = 4n^2$: each $b$ takes 4 times as long.

8. Permutation matrices

Words used here

Row swap = two rows trade places (needed when a $0$ sits in a pivot spot). Permutation matrix $P$ = the identity with its rows reordered; multiplying $PA$ reorders the rows of $A$ the same way. Factorial $n! = n \times (n - 1) \times \dots \times 1$. Cycle = every row moves one place round. Transpose = rows become columns. Inverse = undo.

When a zero lands in a pivot spot we swap rows (Lecture 03). A permutation matrix $P$ does the swaps. It is the identity with its rows rearranged.

All six 3 × 3 permutation matrices

MatrixWhat it does to the rows
$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$nothing ($I$)
$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$swap rows 1 and 2
$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$swap rows 1 and 3
$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$swap rows 2 and 3
$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$cycle: row 2 → top, row 3 → middle, row 1 → bottom
$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$cycle the other way

Why exactly six?

  1. What we do Choose which row of $I$ goes on top.

    How Any of the 3 rows: 3 choices.

  2. What we do Choose the middle row.

    How One row is used, 2 are left: 2 choices. So far $3 \times 2 = 6$.

  3. What we do The bottom row.

    How Only 1 row is left: 1 choice. $6 \times 1 = 6$.

    What we have now $3! = 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 6$ permutation matrices.

Facts about permutation matrices
  • How many: $n \times n$ has $n! = n \times (n-1) \times \dots \times 1$ of them. $3! = 6$, $\ 4! = 24$.
  • Multiply two and you get another one: swaps followed by swaps are still swaps.
  • Inverse = transpose: $P^{-1} = P^T$. A single swap is its own inverse (swap again to undo). The two cycles are each other's inverse.
Example: check the inverse of a cycle

  1. What we do Transpose $P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$: rows become columns.

    How Row 1 $(0, 1, 0)$ becomes column 1. Row 2 $(0, 0, 1)$ becomes column 2. Row 3 $(1, 0, 0)$ becomes column 3.

    What we have now $P^T = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ (the other cycle).

  2. What we do Multiply $PP^T$ one row at a time.

    Why If we get $I$, then $P^T$ undoes $P$.

    How A row with a single 1 just picks one row of $P^T$. Row 1 of $P$, $(0, 1, 0)$, picks row 2 of $P^T$: $(1, 0, 0)$. Row 2, $(0, 0, 1)$, picks row 3: $(0, 1, 0)$. Row 3, $(1, 0, 0)$, picks row 1: $(0, 0, 1)$.

    Row of $P$Takes this row of $P^T$Row of $PP^T$
    $(0, 1, 0)$row 2: $(1, 0, 0)$$(1, 0, 0)$
    $(0, 0, 1)$row 3: $(0, 1, 0)$$(0, 1, 0)$
    $(1, 0, 0)$row 1: $(0, 0, 1)$$(0, 0, 1)$

  3. Check The rows are $(1, 0, 0)$, $(0, 1, 0)$, $(0, 0, 1)$: that is $I$. So $PP^T = I$ ✓, and $P^T$ undoes $P$.

In finance
  • A risk or pricing model often solves $Ax = b$ with the same $A$ and many different $b$'s (many payoffs, many scenarios). Build $L$ and $U$ once (about $\tfrac13 n^3$ work), then each new $b$ costs only $n^2$. Example 10 does this.
  • The cost count matters: a 1,000-asset model means about $3 \times 10^8$ operations to factor, which is quick for a computer. Doubling to 2,000 assets makes it 8 times as long.

1. The agenda: what question are we solving?

The question, as a story

Your desk trades three options. Nifty will expire at 19,900, 20,000 or 20,100 (payoffs in points ÷ 100, per 1 quantity):

ProductPays at 19,900at 20,000at 20,100
A = buy 20,100 PE210
B = buy 19,900 CE012
C = buy 20,000 PE100

Clients keep arriving: one wants 3 / 2 / 2, the next 2 / 0 / 2, the next 0 / 1 / 3. Each time you need the lots.

Lecture 03's way: redo the whole elimination for every client. Lecture 04's way: compute $A^{-1}$ (more work than needed).

Lecture 05's way: do the elimination once, and keep two things from it: $U$ (the staircase that's left) and $L$ (a diary of every row move). Then every client is two quick, easy passes: top down through $L$, bottom up through $U$.

7. How this is used with real options

  • Many payoffs, one factorization. A desk quoting lots of custom payoffs on the same set of listed options factors once and reuses $L$ and $U$ for every quote.
  • Option pricing by finite differences. Solving the Black–Scholes equation on a grid (implicit or Crank–Nicolson methods) needs a linear system at every time step with the same matrix. It is factored once with LU (for these tridiagonal matrices, the "Thomas algorithm") and reused at every step.
  • Correlated scenarios. Monte Carlo for baskets uses the Cholesky factorization, a close cousin of LU for symmetric matrices ($A = LL^T$), to turn independent random numbers into correlated ones.
  • Speed. Factoring costs about $n^3/3$ steps once; each new right side costs about $n^2$. For big systems this is the difference between seconds and hours.

Example 2: A = LU for a 2 × 2

Problem

Factor $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 8 \end{bmatrix}$ into $LU$, and then into $LDU$.

Words used here

Pivot = the diagonal number used to clear below it. Multiplier = (number to remove) ÷ (pivot). $U$ = upper triangular, what is left after elimination. $L$ = lower triangular with 1s on the diagonal and the multiplier below. $D$ = diagonal matrix of the pivots.

  1. What we do Clear the $3$ under the pivot $1$.

    Why this amount $3 \div 1 = 3$: subtract 3 × row 1.

    How $3 \times (1, 2) = (3, 6)$. Then $(3 - 3,\ 8 - 6) = (0, 2)$.

    What we have now $U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, multiplier $3$.

  2. What we do Write $L$.

    How 1s on the diagonal, the multiplier $3$ in spot (2, 1) (the spot it cleared).

    $$ A = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{L}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}}_{U} $$

  3. Check Row 1 of $LU$: $1 \times (1, 2) = (1, 2)$ ✓. Row 2: $3 \times (1, 2) = (3, 6)$; add $(0, 2)$: $(3, 8)$ ✓.

  4. What we do Split out the pivots for $LDU$.

    How Pivots are $1$ and $2$, so $D = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. Row 1 of $U$ ÷ 1: $(1, 2)$. Row 2 ÷ 2: $(0 \div 2,\ 2 \div 2) = (0, 1)$.

    $$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

    Check $D$ × new $U$: row 1 $1 \times (1, 2) = (1, 2)$, row 2 $2 \times (0, 1) = (0, 2)$. That is the old $U$ ✓.

Example 5: E has tangled numbers, L does not

Problem

Elimination did "row 2 − 3 × row 1" and then "row 3 − 4 × row 2" (with (3, 1) already zero). Find $E = E_{32}E_{21}$ and $L$.

Words used here

$E_{21}$ = identity with $-3$ in spot (2, 1): it does "row 2 − 3 × row 1". $E_{32}$ = identity with $-4$ in spot (3, 2): it does "row 3 − 4 × row 2". $E = E_{32}E_{21}$ = both steps in one matrix (forward). $L$ = the undo (backward): 1s on the diagonal, multipliers in their spots. Tangled = a number in $E$ made by mixing two multipliers.

  1. What we do Write the two step matrices.

    How $E_{21}$ rows: $(1, 0, 0)$, $(-3, 1, 0)$, $(0, 0, 1)$. $E_{32}$ rows: $(1, 0, 0)$, $(0, 1, 0)$, $(0, -4, 1)$.

  2. What we do Multiply $E = E_{32}E_{21}$ row by row (rows of $E_{32}$ mix rows of $E_{21}$).

    Rows 1, 2 Row 1 picks $(1, 0, 0)$. Row 2 picks $(-3, 1, 0)$.

    Row 3 $-4 \times (-3, 1, 0) = (12, -4, 0)$. Add row 3 $(0, 0, 1)$: $(12, -4, 1)$.

  3. What we do Write $L$: multiplier 3 in spot (2, 1), 0 in spot (3, 1), 4 in spot (3, 2).

    $$ E = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 & 0 \\ 12 & -4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

  4. Check $L$ undoes $E$, so $LE$ should be $I$. Row 2 of $L$, $(3, 1, 0)$: $3 \times (1, 0, 0) + (-3, 1, 0) = (3 - 3, 1, 0) = (0, 1, 0)$ ✓.

    Check Row 3 of $L$, $(0, 4, 1)$: $4 \times (-3, 1, 0) = (-12, 4, 0)$; add $(12, -4, 1)$: $(-12 + 12,\ 4 - 4,\ 0 + 1) = (0, 0, 1)$ ✓.

$E$ has a tangled $12 = 3 \times 4$. $L$ just holds the multipliers 3 and 4.

Example 6: Inverse of a product

Problem

If $A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $B^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, find $(AB)^{-1}$.

Words used here

Inverse = the undo matrix. Reverse order rule: $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$ (undo the last step first). Product: each row of the left matrix tells how much of each row of the right matrix to add.

  1. What we do Use the reverse order: $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$.

    Why $B$ acted first, so it is undone last (socks and shoes).

  2. Row 1 Row 1 of $B^{-1}$ is $(1, 0)$: $1 \times (1, 2) + 0 \times (0, 1) = (1, 2)$.

  3. Row 2 Row 2 of $B^{-1}$ is $(3, 1)$: $3 \times (1, 2) = (3, 6)$. Add $1 \times (0, 1)$: $(3 + 0,\ 6 + 1) = (3, 7)$.

    Row of $B^{-1}$MixResult
    $(1, 0)$$1 \times (1, 2)$$(1, 2)$
    $(3, 1)$$3 \times (1, 2) + 1 \times (0, 1)$$(3, 7)$

  4. Check Undo the given inverses: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Row 1 of $AB$: $1 \times (1, 0) - 2 \times (-3, 1) = (1 + 6,\ 0 - 2) = (7, -2)$. Row 2: $(-3, 1)$.

    Check $AB$ times our answer, row 1: $7 \times (1, 2) - 2 \times (3, 7) = (7 - 6,\ 14 - 14) = (1, 0)$ ✓. Row 2: $-3 \times (1, 2) + 1 \times (3, 7) = (-3 + 3,\ -6 + 7) = (0, 1)$ ✓.

Example 7: Transposes

Problem

Find $A^T$ for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$, and $(A^T)^{-1}$ for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 5 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

Words used here

Transpose = rows become columns. Inverse = undo. For a $2 \times 2$ matrix $\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$ the shortcut inverse is: swap $a$ and $d$, change the signs of $b$ and $c$, divide by $ad - bc$. Rule: $(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T$.

  1. What we do First part: turn rows into columns.

    How Row 1 $(1, 0, 2)$ becomes column 1. Row 2 $(3, 4, 5)$ becomes column 2.

    What we have now $A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$ ($2 \times 3 \to 3 \times 2$).

  2. What we do Second part: find $ad - bc$ for $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 5 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

    How $a \times d = 2 \times 3 = 6$. $b \times c = 1 \times 5 = 5$. $6 - 5 = 1$.

  3. What we do Build $A^{-1}$ with the shortcut.

    How Swap $2$ and $3$; make $1 \to -1$ and $5 \to -5$; divide by 1 (no change).

    What we have now $A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -5 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.

  4. What we do Transpose it: row 1 $(3, -1)$ becomes column 1, row 2 $(-5, 2)$ becomes column 2.

    What we have now $(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -5 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.

  5. Check $A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. Row 1 of $A^T \times$ our answer: $(2 \times 3 + 5 \times (-1),\ 2 \times (-5) + 5 \times 2) = (6 - 5,\ -10 + 10) = (1, 0)$ ✓.

    Check Row 2: $(1 \times 3 + 3 \times (-1),\ 1 \times (-5) + 3 \times 2) = (3 - 3,\ -5 + 6) = (0, 1)$ ✓.

$(A^T)^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -5 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$

Example 8: How long will it take?

Problem

Factoring a $500 \times 500$ matrix takes 1 second. About how long for $1000 \times 1000$? And for one extra right-hand side at size 1000, compared with size 500?

Words used here

Factoring = finding $L$ and $U$; it costs about $\tfrac13 n^3$ operations. Right-hand side $b$ = one new set of known numbers; solving for it costs about $n^2$. Doubling $n$ = going from 500 to 1000.

  1. What we do See what doubling does to $n^3$.

    How $(2n)^3 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times n^3$. $2 \times 2 = 4$, $4 \times 2 = 8$. So 8 times the work.

    What we have now $1 \text{ second} \times 8 = $ about 8 seconds to factor.

  2. What we do See what doubling does to $n^2$.

    How $(2n)^2 = 2 \times 2 \times n^2 = 4n^2$.

    What we have now Each $b$ takes 4 times as long as at size 500.

About 8 seconds to factor; each $b$ takes 4 times as long as before (still tiny).

Example 9: Permutations

Problem

(a) How many $4 \times 4$ permutation matrices are there? (b) What is the inverse of $P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$?

Words used here

Permutation matrix = identity with rows reordered. $n!$ = number of ways to order $n$ rows. Inverse = undo. Transpose = rows become columns. Symmetric = equal to its own transpose.

  1. (a) What we do Count the ways to order 4 rows.

    How Top row: 4 choices. Next: 3. Next: 2. Last: 1. $4 \times 3 = 12$, $12 \times 2 = 24$, $24 \times 1 = 24$.

    What we have now $4! = 24$.

  2. (b) What we do Read what $P$ does.

    How Row 1 of $P$ is $(0, 0, 1)$: it picks row 3 to go on top. Row 3 is $(1, 0, 0)$: it picks row 1 to go at the bottom. Row 2 stays. So $P$ swaps rows 1 and 3.

  3. What we do Undo it.

    Why Swapping rows 1 and 3 a second time puts them back. So $P^{-1} = P$.

    Check $PP$: row 1 $(0, 0, 1)$ picks row 3 of $P$ $= (1, 0, 0)$; row 2 picks $(0, 1, 0)$; row 3 picks row 1 $= (0, 0, 1)$. That is $I$ ✓.

    Also Transposing $P$ gives $P$ again (it is symmetric), which agrees with $P^{-1} = P^T$.

Story first

The funds (the "tickets") never change, only the orders do. So do the expensive work once, splitting the fund matrix into $L$ and $U$. Then each new order is two quick triangular solves.

Words used here

Fund matrix $A$ = rows are the states (down, flat, up), columns are the funds; entry = what that fund pays in that state. $b$ = the payout a client wants in each state. $x$ = how many units of each fund to buy (the recipe). $L$, $U$ = the two triangular factors, found once. $c$ = the middle stop: $Lc = b$, then $Ux = c$. Cost = units × price today, added up.

  1. What we do Factor once.

    Why The fund matrix (rows = states, columns = funds) has rows $(1, 1, 1)$, $(2, 3, 1)$, $(1, 3, 4)$: exactly the matrix of Example 3. So we already have its factors.

    $$ L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}. $$
