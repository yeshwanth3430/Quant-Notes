2. Row swaps: PA = LU

Lecture 05 gave $A = LU$ when no row swaps are needed. If a $0$ lands in a pivot spot, we must swap rows. The fix: do all the swaps first (with a permutation matrix $P$), then factor as usual.

Elimination with row swaps $$ PA = LU $$

$P$ puts the rows of $A$ in a good order. Then elimination runs with no trouble, and $L$ and $U$ come out exactly as in Lecture 05. Every invertible $A$ can be written this way. (Most need no swaps, so $P = I$.)

Example: one swap, then LU $$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 7 & 9 \end{bmatrix} $$

Problem: the first pivot spot holds $0$. Row 2 has a $1$ there, so swap rows 1 and 2:

$$ P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad PA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 7 & 9 \end{bmatrix} $$

Now eliminate $PA$ (Part 1 of Lecture 05: find $U$, write down the multipliers):

StepWorkMultiplier
(row 2, col 1)already $0$$0$
(row 3, col 1): row 3 − 2 × row 1$(2, 7, 9) - (2, 4, 2) = (0, 3, 7)$$2$
(row 3, col 2): row 3 − 3 × row 2$(0, 3, 7) - (0, 3, 3) = (0, 0, 4)$$3$
$$ L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check row 3 of $LU$: $2 \times (1, 2, 1) + 3 \times (0, 1, 1) + 1 \times (0, 0, 4) = (2,\ 4 + 3,\ 2 + 3 + 4) = (2, 7, 9)$ ✓, row 3 of $PA$.

Real computers swap even more

MATLAB and Python don't only swap when a pivot is exactly $0$. They also swap when a pivot is very small, because dividing by tiny numbers causes big rounding errors. So in practice $P$ is often not $I$. The algebra is the same.

Permutation matrices: a quick recap

FactMeaning
$P$ = rows of $I$ reorderedSwap the rows of $I$ the way you want to swap the rows of $A$.
There are $n!$ of them$3! = 6$, $\ 4! = 24$, $\ 5! = 120$.
$P^{-1} = P^T$Equivalently $P^TP = I$: transposing a permutation undoes it.

3. Transpose, symmetric matrices

Definition: Transpose

Flip the matrix across its main diagonal: rows become columns. The entry in row $i$, column $j$ of $A^T$ is the entry in row $j$, column $i$ of $A$:

$$ (A^T)_{ij} = A_{ji} $$
$$ R = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \ (3 \times 2) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \ (2 \times 3) $$

Column 1 of $R$, $(1, 2, 4)$, became row 1 of $R^T$. A tall matrix lies down on its side.

Definition: Symmetric

A matrix is symmetric if transposing does not change it: $A^T = A$. The entry at (row $i$, col $j$) equals the entry at (row $j$, col $i$). It is a mirror image across the diagonal.

$$ S = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & \color{#2e7d4f}{1} & \color{#2f5d8a}{7} \\ \color{#2e7d4f}{1} & 2 & \color{#b42318}{9} \\ \color{#2f5d8a}{7} & \color{#b42318}{9} & 4 \end{bmatrix} $$

Matching colours are mirror pairs. The diagonal ($3, 2, 4$) can be anything.

Matrix$A^T$ givesSpecial property
Symmetricthe same matrix$A^T = A$ (easy to spot: mirror across the diagonal)
Permutationthe inverse$P^T = P^{-1}$

4. RᵀR is always symmetric

$R$ above is rectangular and nowhere near symmetric. But multiply it by its transpose:

$$ R^TR = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Each entry is (row of $R^T$) · (column of $R$), which is (column of $R$) · (column of $R$):

EntryWhich columns of $R$WorkValue
(1, 1)col 1 · col 1$1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 4$$21$
(1, 2)col 1 · col 2$1 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 3 + 4 \cdot 1$$13$
(2, 1)col 2 · col 1$3 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 4$$13$
(2, 2)col 2 · col 2$3 \cdot 3 + 3 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 1$$19$
$$ R^TR = \begin{bmatrix} 21 & \color{#2e7d4f}{13} \\ \color{#2e7d4f}{13} & 19 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{symmetric ✓} $$

The two 13s match because (col 1 · col 2) and (col 2 · col 1) are the same dot product, just in the other order.

Proof in two lines

Transpose $R^TR$ and use the "reverse the order" rule:

$$ (R^TR)^T = R^T\,(R^T)^T = R^T R $$

Transposing twice gives back $R$. So $R^TR$ equals its own transpose: it is symmetric.

The same goes for $RR^T$ (here $3 \times 3$): $\begin{bmatrix} 10 & 11 & 7 \\ 11 & 13 & 11 \\ 7 & 11 & 17 \end{bmatrix}$, also a mirror image.

5. Vector spaces

Now the big step: from single vectors to spaces of vectors.

Definition: Vector space

A vector space is a collection of vectors where you can always:

  1. add any two of them, and
  2. multiply any of them by any number,

and the answer is still in the collection. In short: you cannot escape it by taking linear combinations. (There are also 8 simple rules, like $v + w = w + v$, which normal vectors always follow.)

The main examples

SpaceContainsPictureExample vectors
$\mathbb{R}^2$all vectors with 2 real componentsthe whole flat $xy$-plane$(3, 2)$, $(0, 0)$, $(\pi, e)$
$\mathbb{R}^3$all vectors with 3 real componentsall of 3D space$(3, 2, 0)$
$\mathbb{R}^n$all vectors with $n$ real components$n$-dimensional space$(v_1, \dots, v_n)$
Watch out

$(3, 2, 0)$ is in $\mathbb{R}^3$, not $\mathbb{R}^2$. It has three components; one just happens to be $0$.

Every vector space contains zero

Take any vector $v$ in the space. Multiplying by $0$ is allowed, so $0 \times v = 0$ must be in the space. (Or add $v$ and $-v$.) A space with the origin removed is not a vector space.

Something that is NOT a vector space

Take only the top-right quarter of the plane: vectors with both components $\ge 0$.

TestExampleStays inside?
Add two of them$(3, 2) + (5, 6) = (8, 8)$yes ✓
Multiply by a number$-5 \times (3, 2) = (-15, -10)$no ✗ (it lands in the bottom-left)

Multiplying by a negative number escapes, so the quarter plane is not closed and not a vector space.

6. Subspaces

Definition: Subspace

A subspace is a vector space that sits inside a bigger one. It must pass the same test: adding or multiplying by any number never leads outside it.

A line through the origin is a subspace

Put one vector $v$ in. Then all its multiples ($2v$, $\tfrac12 v$, $-v$, $0v$, …) must come in too. That fills a whole line through the origin. Adding two points on that line stays on the line ✓.

A line that misses the origin is not a subspace: $0 \times v = 0$ would be off the line.

All subspaces of R² and R³

Subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^2$Subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^3$

  • The zero vector alone is a subspace: $0 + 0 = 0$ and $17 \times 0 = 0$, so you never leave it.
  • A line in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is not $\mathbb{R}^1$. It looks like a line, but its vectors have 2 components.

7. The column space

The most important way to get a subspace out of a matrix: take its columns and all their combinations.

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \text{column 1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \text{column 2} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
Definition: Column space C(A)

The column space of $A$, written $C(A)$, is the set of all linear combinations of the columns of $A$:

$$ C(A) = \{\, x_1(\text{col } 1) + x_2(\text{col } 2) \ :\ \text{any numbers } x_1, x_2 \,\} $$

It is automatically a subspace, because a mix of mixes is still a mix.

Some vectors in C(A)

$x_1$$x_2$$x_1(1, 2, 4) + x_2(3, 3, 1)$
$1$$0$$(1, 2, 4)$
$0$$1$$(3, 3, 1)$
$1$$1$$(4, 5, 5)$
$0$$0$$(0, 0, 0)$
$2$$-1$$(2 - 3,\ 4 - 3,\ 8 - 1) = (-1, 1, 7)$

What shape is it?

The two columns point in different directions (neither is a multiple of the other). All their combinations fill a plane through the origin inside $\mathbb{R}^3$. It is not all of $\mathbb{R}^3$: two vectors can only make a flat sheet.

Why the column space matters

$Ax$ is always a mix of the columns of $A$. So

$$ Ax = b \text{ has a solution} \iff b \text{ is in the column space } C(A). $$

This links solving equations to the geometry of subspaces. It is the starting point of the next lecture.

If the columns…their column space is
point in different directions (2 columns in $\mathbb{R}^3$)a plane
lie on the same lineonly a line
are 3 independent columns in $\mathbb{R}^3$all of $\mathbb{R}^3$

Bigger example: 5 vectors with 10 components each give a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{10}$. It is at most 5-dimensional (less if some vectors are mixes of others), and never $\mathbb{R}^5$, because the vectors have 10 components.

In finance
  • Symmetric matrices everywhere: put daily returns in a matrix $R$ (rows = days, columns = assets). Then $R^TR$ is symmetric. Its diagonal measures how much each asset moves; its off-diagonal measures how two assets move together. This is the idea behind the covariance matrix (Example 7).
  • Column space = what you can build: the columns of a payoff matrix are the assets. Every payoff you can create by buying them is in the column space. If a payoff is outside it, you cannot replicate it: the market is incomplete (Example 8).

Worked examples

Example 1: PA = LU with a 2 × 2

Problem

Factor $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ as $PA = LU$.

Problem: the first pivot spot is $0$. Row 2 has a $3$ there, so swap the rows:

$$ P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad PA = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} $$

Eliminate $PA$: it is already a staircase (the spot below the pivot is $0$), so the multiplier is $0$.

$$ L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad U = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} $$

$PA = LU$ with $L = I$ and $U = PA$.

Example 2: Transpose

Problem

Find the transpose of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 5 \\ 2 & 7 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. What is the entry in row 3, column 2 of $A^T$?

Rows become columns: row 1 $(1, 0, 5)$ becomes column 1; row 2 $(2, 7, 3)$ becomes column 2.

$$ A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 7 \\ 5 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \quad (2 \times 3 \to 3 \times 2) $$

Entry (3, 2) of $A^T$ $=$ entry (2, 3) of $A = 3$ ✓.

$A^T$ as above; $(A^T)_{32} = 3$.

Example 6: Is b in the column space?

Problem

$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Is (a) $b = (4, 5, 5)$ and (b) $b = (1, 0, 0)$ in $C(A)$?

Question: can we find $x_1, x_2$ with $x_1(1, 2, 4) + x_2(3, 3, 1) = b$? That is three equations:

$$ x_1 + 3x_2 = b_1, \qquad 2x_1 + 3x_2 = b_2, \qquad 4x_1 + x_2 = b_3 $$

Use the first two to find $x_1, x_2$, then check the third.

(a) $b = (4, 5, 5)$

StepWorkResult
eq 2 − eq 1$(2x_1 + 3x_2) - (x_1 + 3x_2) = 5 - 4$$x_1 = 1$
eq 1$1 + 3x_2 = 4$$x_2 = 1$
check eq 3$4(1) + 1 = 5$$5 = 5$ ✓

(a) Yes: $b$ = col 1 + col 2.

(b) $b = (1, 0, 0)$

StepWorkResult
eq 2 − eq 1$x_1 = 0 - 1$$x_1 = -1$
eq 1$-1 + 3x_2 = 1 \Rightarrow 3x_2 = 2$$x_2 = \tfrac23$
check eq 3$4(-1) + \tfrac23 = -\tfrac{10}{3}$$-\tfrac{10}{3} \ne 0$ ✗

(b) No: $(1, 0, 0)$ is off the plane, so $Ax = (1, 0, 0)$ has no solution.

Example 7: Returns matrix → a symmetric "co-movement" matrix (finance)

Problem

Two stocks, three days. Daily returns (in %):

DayStock AStock B
1$1$$2$
2$0$$1$
3$1$$0$

Put them in $R$ (rows = days, columns = stocks) and compute $R^TR$. What do its entries mean?

Story first

Each stock's column is its "diary" of daily moves. Dotting a diary with itself measures how much that stock moves. Dotting two different diaries measures how much they move together. Stock A with B and B with A give the same answer, so the table is a mirror image.

$R = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, the same as Example 4, so

$$ R^TR = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix} $$
EntryWorkMeaning
(A, A) $= 2$$1^2 + 0^2 + 1^2$how much A moves
(B, B) $= 5$$2^2 + 1^2 + 0^2$how much B moves (more than A)
(A, B) $=$ (B, A) $= 2$$1 \cdot 2 + 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0$how much they move together

$R^TR$ is symmetric. With averages subtracted and scaled, this becomes the covariance matrix used in portfolio risk.

Example 8: Can the market build this payoff? (finance)

Problem

Tomorrow the stock ends at 1 (low), 2 (middle) or 3 (high). Only two assets exist:

AssetPays if lowPays if middlePays if high
Bond$1$$1$$1$
Stock$1$$2$$3$

Can you build (a) the payoff $(3, 5, 7)$, and (b) a call option with strike 2, paying $(0, 0, 1)$?

Story first

Two kinds of ticket, three kinds of weather. Mixing two tickets can only reach a flat sheet (a plane) of payoffs inside 3D. Some payoffs are on the sheet, some are not.

Question: is the payoff in the column space of $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$? Solve $x(1, 1, 1) + y(1, 2, 3) =$ payoff, using the first two rows, then check the third.

(a) $(3, 5, 7)$

Middle − low: $y = 5 - 3 = 2$. Low: $x + 2 = 3$, so $x = 1$. Check high: $1 + 3(2) = 7$ ✓.

(a) Yes: 1 bond + 2 stocks.

(b) Call $(0, 0, 1)$

Middle − low: $y = 0$. Low: $x + 0 = 0$, so $x = 0$. Check high: $0 + 0 = 0 \ne 1$ ✗.

(b) No: the call is outside the column space. This market is incomplete; you would need a third asset to build it.

5. What is the column space of $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$?

Column 2 $= 2 \times$ column 1, so all combinations are multiples of $(1, 2)$: the line through $(1, 2)$.
