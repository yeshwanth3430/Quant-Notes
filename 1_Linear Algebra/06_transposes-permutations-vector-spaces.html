2. Row swaps: PA = LU

Words used here

Pivot (leader) = the number we use to clear the entries under it; it must not be $0$, because we divide by it. Multiplier = (number to remove) ÷ (pivot). Row swap = trade the places of two rows. Identity $I$ = 1s on the diagonal, 0s elsewhere. Permutation matrix $P$ = $I$ with its rows reordered; $PA$ reorders the rows of $A$ the same way. $L$ = lower triangular (1s on the diagonal, multipliers below). $U$ = upper triangular staircase left after elimination. $n!$ = $n \times (n - 1) \times \dots \times 1$. Transpose $P^T$ = rows of $P$ become columns. Inverse $P^{-1}$ = the matrix that undoes $P$.

Lecture 05 gave $A = LU$ when no row swaps are needed. If a $0$ lands in a pivot spot, we must swap rows. The fix: do all the swaps first (with a permutation matrix $P$), then factor as usual.

Elimination with row swaps $$ PA = LU $$

$P$ puts the rows of $A$ in a good order. Then elimination runs with no trouble, and $L$ and $U$ come out exactly as in Lecture 05. Every invertible $A$ can be written this way. (Most need no swaps, so $P = I$.)

Example: one swap, then LU $$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 7 & 9 \end{bmatrix} $$

  1. What we do Look at the first pivot spot: row 1, column 1.

    Why Elimination divides by the pivot to find each multiplier. Dividing by $0$ is impossible.

    How The number there is $0$. Look down column 1: row 2 has a $1$, row 3 has a $2$. The first non-zero one below is the $1$ in row 2.

    What we have now Plan: swap rows 1 and 2, so the $1$ becomes the pivot.

  2. What we do Build $P$, the matrix that does this swap.

    Why Whatever we do to the rows of $I$, multiplying by the result does to the rows of $A$.

    How Start from $I$ with rows $(1, 0, 0)$, $(0, 1, 0)$, $(0, 0, 1)$. Swap its rows 1 and 2: rows become $(0, 1, 0)$, $(1, 0, 0)$, $(0, 0, 1)$.

    What we have now The permutation matrix below.

    $$ P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

  3. What we do Compute $PA$.

    Why This is the matrix we will actually eliminate.

    How Row 1 of $P$ is $(0, 1, 0)$: "take 1 of row 2 of $A$" $= (1, 2, 1)$. Row 2 of $P$ is $(1, 0, 0)$: "take row 1 of $A$" $= (0, 1, 1)$. Row 3 of $P$ is $(0, 0, 1)$: "take row 3 of $A$" $= (2, 7, 9)$.

    What we have now Rows 1 and 2 of $A$ have traded places.

    $$ PA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 7 & 9 \end{bmatrix} $$

  4. What we do Pivot = the $1$ at the top-left of $PA$. Clear column 1 below it.

    Why We want zeros under every pivot: that makes the staircase $U$.

    How Row 2 already has $0$ in column 1: multiplier $0 \div 1 = 0$, nothing to do. Row 3 has $2$: multiplier $2 \div 1 = 2$. Two copies of row 1: $2 \times (1, 2, 1) = (2, 4, 2)$. Take them away: $(2 - 2,\ 7 - 4,\ 9 - 2) = (0, 3, 7)$.

    What we have now Rows $(1, 2, 1)$, $(0, 1, 1)$, $(0, 3, 7)$. Multipliers so far: $\ell_{21} = 0$, $\ell_{31} = 2$.

  5. What we do Pivot = the $1$ in row 2, column 2. Clear the $3$ under it.

    Why Column 2 also needs a zero below its pivot.

    How Multiplier $3 \div 1 = 3$. Three copies of row 2: $3 \times (0, 1, 1) = (0, 3, 3)$. Take them away: $(0 - 0,\ 3 - 3,\ 7 - 3) = (0, 0, 4)$.

    What we have now The staircase is done. Multiplier $\ell_{32} = 3$. All the work in one table:

    StepWorkMultiplier
    (row 2, col 1)already $0$$0$
    (row 3, col 1): row 3 − 2 × row 1$(2, 7, 9) - (2, 4, 2) = (0, 3, 7)$$2$
    (row 3, col 2): row 3 − 3 × row 2$(0, 3, 7) - (0, 3, 3) = (0, 0, 4)$$3$

  6. What we do Write down $L$ and $U$.

    Why $U$ is the staircase; $L$ remembers the multipliers, each in the spot it cleared.

    How $L$: 1s on the diagonal, $0$ at (2, 1), $2$ at (3, 1), $3$ at (3, 2), 0s above. $U$: the three final rows.

    What we have now The two factors:

    $$ L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix} $$

  7. Check Row 3 of $LU$ = $2 \times$ (row 1 of $U$) $+ 3 \times$ (row 2 of $U$) $+ 1 \times$ (row 3 of $U$).

    How $2 \times (1, 2, 1) = (2, 4, 2)$. $3 \times (0, 1, 1) = (0, 3, 3)$. $1 \times (0, 0, 4) = (0, 0, 4)$. Add: $(2 + 0 + 0,\ 4 + 3 + 0,\ 2 + 3 + 4) = (2, 7, 9)$.

    What we have now $(2, 7, 9)$ is row 3 of $PA$ ✓. Rows 1 and 2 of $LU$ are just rows 1 and 2 of $U$: $(1, 2, 1)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$, matching $PA$ ✓. So $PA = LU$.

Real computers swap even more

MATLAB and Python don't only swap when a pivot is exactly $0$. They also swap when a pivot is very small, because dividing by tiny numbers causes big rounding errors. So in practice $P$ is often not $I$. The algebra is the same.

Counting the 3 × 3 permutation matrices

  1. What we do Choose which row of $I$ goes on top.

    Why A permutation matrix is just the 3 rows of $I$ in some order, so counting orders counts matrices.

    How Any of the 3 rows can go first: 3 choices.

    What we have now 3 ways to fill the top.

  2. What we do Choose the middle row.

    Why One row is already used, so fewer are left.

    How 2 rows are left: 2 choices. Each of the 3 tops pairs with each of these 2: $3 \times 2 = 6$.

    What we have now 6 ways to fill top and middle.

  3. What we do Put in the bottom row.

    Why Only one row is left, so there is no choice.

    How 1 choice: $3 \times 2 \times 1 = 6 = 3!$.

    What we have now Six permutation matrices. Listed by the order of the rows of $I$: (1,2,3) = $I$, (2,1,3), (3,2,1), (1,3,2), (2,3,1), (3,1,2).

  4. Check Same rule for 4 rows: $4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 24 = 4!$ ✓, matching the table.

Checking $P^TP = I$ for the swap above

  1. What we do Transpose the $P$ from the example.

    Why We want to see that $P^T$ undoes $P$.

    How Rows of $P$: $(0, 1, 0)$, $(1, 0, 0)$, $(0, 0, 1)$. They become columns: column 1 $= (0, 1, 0)$, column 2 $= (1, 0, 0)$, column 3 $= (0, 0, 1)$. Read across: row 1 of $P^T$ $= (0, 1, 0)$, row 2 $= (1, 0, 0)$, row 3 $= (0, 0, 1)$.

    What we have now $P^T = P$ here (one swap is its own mirror image).

  2. What we do Multiply $P^TP$, i.e. apply the swap twice.

    Why If the answer is $I$, then $P^T$ is the inverse.

    How $P$ swaps rows 1 and 2 of whatever comes after it. Apply it to $P$ itself: rows $(0, 1, 0)$ and $(1, 0, 0)$ trade places, giving $(1, 0, 0)$, $(0, 1, 0)$, $(0, 0, 1)$.

    What we have now That is $I$ ✓. Swapping twice puts everything back.

3. Transpose, symmetric matrices

Words used here

Matrix = a table of numbers; $3 \times 2$ means 3 rows and 2 columns. Entry $A_{ij}$ = the number in row $i$, column $j$. Main diagonal = the entries $A_{11}, A_{22}, \dots$ from top-left going down-right. Transpose $A^T$ = flip across the main diagonal, so row $i$ becomes column $i$. Symmetric = transposing changes nothing ($A^T = A$). Mirror pair = the two entries $A_{ij}$ and $A_{ji}$, which sit opposite each other across the diagonal. Permutation $P$ = $I$ with rows reordered; inverse = the matrix that undoes it.

Definition: Transpose

Flip the matrix across its main diagonal: rows become columns. The entry in row $i$, column $j$ of $A^T$ is the entry in row $j$, column $i$ of $A$:

$$ (A^T)_{ij} = A_{ji} $$
$$ R = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \ (3 \times 2) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \ (2 \times 3) $$

Column 1 of $R$, $(1, 2, 4)$, became row 1 of $R^T$. A tall matrix lies down on its side.

Transposing R entry by entry

  1. What we do Find the shape of $R^T$.

    Why Rows become columns, so the two numbers in the shape trade places.

    How $R$ is $3 \times 2$ (3 rows, 2 columns). Swap: $2 \times 3$.

    What we have now An empty table with 2 rows and 3 columns to fill.

  2. What we do Fill row 1 of $R^T$.

    Why The rule $(R^T)_{1j} = R_{j1}$: row 1 of $R^T$ reads down column 1 of $R$.

    How One entry at a time, using the table below.

    Entry of $R^T$RuleLook up in $R$Value
    (1, 1)$R_{11}$row 1, col 1$1$
    (1, 2)$R_{21}$row 2, col 1$2$
    (1, 3)$R_{31}$row 3, col 1$4$

    What we have now Row 1 of $R^T$ $= (1, 2, 4)$.

  3. What we do Fill row 2 of $R^T$.

    Why $(R^T)_{2j} = R_{j2}$: row 2 of $R^T$ reads down column 2 of $R$.

    How Same lookup, column 2 this time.

    Entry of $R^T$RuleLook up in $R$Value
    (2, 1)$R_{12}$row 1, col 2$3$
    (2, 2)$R_{22}$row 2, col 2$3$

    What we have now Row 2 of $R^T$ $= (3, 3, 1)$.

  4. Check Transpose back: columns of $R^T$ are $(1, 3)$, $(2, 3)$, $(4, 1)$, which are exactly the rows of $R$ ✓. Flipping twice gives the start: $(R^T)^T = R$.

Definition: Symmetric

A matrix is symmetric if transposing does not change it: $A^T = A$. The entry at (row $i$, col $j$) equals the entry at (row $j$, col $i$). It is a mirror image across the diagonal.

$$ S = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & \color{#2e7d4f}{1} & \color{#2f5d8a}{7} \\ \color{#2e7d4f}{1} & 2 & \color{#b42318}{9} \\ \color{#2f5d8a}{7} & \color{#b42318}{9} & 4 \end{bmatrix} $$

Matching colours are mirror pairs. The diagonal ($3, 2, 4$) can be anything.

Checking that S is symmetric

  1. What we do List the entries above the diagonal.

    Why Each has one mirror partner below the diagonal. Diagonal entries are their own mirror, so they never need checking.

    How In a $3 \times 3$ matrix they are (1, 2), (1, 3), (2, 3).

    What we have now Three pairs to compare.

  2. What we do Compare each with its partner, one pair at a time.

    Why Symmetric means $S_{ij} = S_{ji}$ for every pair.

    How See the table.

    AboveValueMirror belowValueEqual?

    What we have now All three pairs match.

  3. Check Transpose $S$: row 1 becomes column 1, $(3, 1, 7)$, which is already column 1 of $S$. Same for rows 2 and 3. So $S^T = S$: symmetric ✓.

4. RᵀR is always symmetric

Words used here

Rectangular = rows and columns are different in number. Transpose $R^T$ = rows of $R$ become columns. Dot product = multiply matching numbers, then add: $(1, 2) \cdot (3, 4) = 3 + 8 = 11$. Matrix product: entry $(i, j)$ of $AB$ = (row $i$ of $A$) · (column $j$ of $B$). Symmetric = equal to its own transpose, so each entry equals its mirror across the diagonal. "Reverse the order" rule = $(AB)^T = B^TA^T$.

$R$ above is rectangular and nowhere near symmetric. But multiply it by its transpose:

$$ R^TR = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Each entry is (row of $R^T$) · (column of $R$), which is (column of $R$) · (column of $R$):

  1. What we do Find the shape of the answer.

    Why A $(2 \times 3)$ times a $(3 \times 2)$ gives (outer numbers) $2 \times 2$; the inner 3s must match, and they do.

    How $R^T$ is $2 \times 3$, $R$ is $3 \times 2$. Outer numbers: $2$ and $2$.

    What we have now Four entries to find: (1, 1), (1, 2), (2, 1), (2, 2).

  2. What we do Entry (1, 1) = (row 1 of $R^T$) · (column 1 of $R$).

    Why Row 1 of $R^T$ is column 1 of $R$, so this is col 1 · col 1.

    How $(1, 2, 4) \cdot (1, 2, 4)$: $1 \cdot 1 = 1$, $2 \cdot 2 = 4$, $4 \cdot 4 = 16$. Add: $1 + 4 + 16 = 21$.

    What we have now Top-left $= 21$.

  3. What we do Entry (1, 2) = col 1 · col 2.

    Why Row 1 of $R^T$ is col 1 of $R$; the second column of $R$ is col 2.

    How $(1, 2, 4) \cdot (3, 3, 1)$: $1 \cdot 3 = 3$, $2 \cdot 3 = 6$, $4 \cdot 1 = 4$. Add: $3 + 6 + 4 = 13$.

    What we have now Top-right $= 13$.

  4. What we do Entry (2, 1) = col 2 · col 1.

    Why Row 2 of $R^T$ is col 2 of $R$; then column 1 of $R$.

    How $(3, 3, 1) \cdot (1, 2, 4)$: $3 \cdot 1 = 3$, $3 \cdot 2 = 6$, $1 \cdot 4 = 4$. Add: $3 + 6 + 4 = 13$.

    What we have now Bottom-left $= 13$: the same three products as step 3, just multiplied the other way round.

  5. What we do Entry (2, 2) = col 2 · col 2.

    Why Row 2 of $R^T$ with column 2 of $R$.

    How $(3, 3, 1) \cdot (3, 3, 1)$: $3 \cdot 3 = 9$, $3 \cdot 3 = 9$, $1 \cdot 1 = 1$. Add: $9 + 9 + 1 = 19$.

    What we have now Bottom-right $= 19$. All four entries together:

    EntryWhich columns of $R$WorkValue
    (1, 1)col 1 · col 1$1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 4$$21$
    (1, 2)col 1 · col 2$1 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 3 + 4 \cdot 1$$13$
    (2, 1)col 2 · col 1$3 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 4$$13$
    (2, 2)col 2 · col 2$3 \cdot 3 + 3 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 1$$19$
    $$ R^TR = \begin{bmatrix} 21 & \color{#2e7d4f}{13} \\ \color{#2e7d4f}{13} & 19 \end{bmatrix} $$

  6. Check The only mirror pair is (1, 2) and (2, 1): $13$ and $13$, equal. So $R^TR$ is symmetric ✓.

The two 13s match because (col 1 · col 2) and (col 2 · col 1) are the same dot product, just in the other order.

Proof in two lines

Transpose $R^TR$ and use the "reverse the order" rule:

$$ (R^TR)^T = R^T\,(R^T)^T = R^T R $$

Transposing twice gives back $R$. So $R^TR$ equals its own transpose: it is symmetric.

  1. What we do Transpose the product $R^TR$.

    Why "Symmetric" means "equal to its own transpose", so we compute the transpose and compare.

    How Reverse-the-order rule with $A = R^T$ and $B = R$: $(AB)^T = B^TA^T = R^T\,(R^T)^T$.

    What we have now $(R^TR)^T = R^T\,(R^T)^T$.

  2. What we do Simplify $(R^T)^T$.

    Why Flipping across the diagonal twice puts every entry back where it started.

    How $(R^T)^T = R$, so $R^T\,(R^T)^T = R^TR$.

    What we have now $(R^TR)^T = R^TR$: symmetric, for any $R$.

  3. Check With our numbers: the transpose of $\begin{bmatrix} 21 & 13 \\ 13 & 19 \end{bmatrix}$ swaps the two 13s, which changes nothing ✓.

The same goes for $RR^T$ (here $3 \times 3$): $\begin{bmatrix} 10 & 11 & 7 \\ 11 & 13 & 11 \\ 7 & 11 & 17 \end{bmatrix}$, also a mirror image.

  1. What we do Compute $RR^T$ entry by entry.

    Why Entry $(i, j)$ of $RR^T$ = (row $i$ of $R$) · (column $j$ of $R^T$) = (row $i$ of $R$) · (row $j$ of $R$).

    How Rows of $R$: $(1, 3)$, $(2, 3)$, $(4, 1)$. Six different pairs:

    EntryRows of $R$WorkValue

    What we have now The $3 \times 3$ matrix above.

  2. Check Mirror pairs: (1, 2) and (2, 1) both $11$; (1, 3) and (3, 1) both $7$; (2, 3) and (3, 2) both $11$ ✓. Symmetric.

5. Vector spaces

Words used here

Vector = a list of numbers. Component = one number in that list. Adding vectors = add matching components: $(3, 2) + (5, 6) = (8, 8)$. Scaling (multiply by a number) = multiply every component: $-5 \times (3, 2) = (-15, -10)$. Linear combination = scale some vectors, then add. Closed = you can never leave the collection by doing that operation. $\mathbb{R}^n$ = all vectors with $n$ real numbers. Zero vector = all components 0. Origin = the point $(0, 0, \dots)$.

Now the big step: from single vectors to spaces of vectors.

Definition: Vector space

A vector space is a collection of vectors where you can always:

  1. add any two of them, and
  2. multiply any of them by any number,

and the answer is still in the collection. In short: you cannot escape it by taking linear combinations. (There are also 8 simple rules, like $v + w = w + v$, which normal vectors always follow.)

6. Subspaces

Words used here

Vector space = a collection of vectors closed under adding and scaling (you can't leave it). Subspace = a vector space sitting inside a bigger one. Multiple of $v$ = $c \times v$ for some number $c$. Origin = the zero point $(0, 0)$. Line through the origin = all multiples of one non-zero vector. Plane through the origin = all mixes of two vectors pointing in different directions. $\{0\}$ = the collection holding only the zero vector. $\mathbb{R}^1$ = numbers on a single axis (vectors with one component).

Definition: Subspace

A subspace is a vector space that sits inside a bigger one. It must pass the same test: adding or multiplying by any number never leads outside it.

A line through the origin is a subspace

Put one vector $v$ in. Then all its multiples ($2v$, $\tfrac12 v$, $-v$, $0v$, …) must come in too. That fills a whole line through the origin. Adding two points on that line stays on the line ✓.

A line that misses the origin is not a subspace: $0 \times v = 0$ would be off the line.

Checking with numbers: the line y = 2x, and the line y = 2x + 1

  1. What we do Pick two points on the line $y = 2x$: $(1, 2)$ and $(3, 6)$.

    Why To test adding we need two members.

    How $(1, 2)$: is $2 = 2 \times 1$? Yes. $(3, 6)$: is $6 = 2 \times 3$? Yes.

    What we have now Two members of the line.

  2. What we do Add them.

    Why Rule 1: the sum must stay on the line.

    How $(1 + 3,\ 2 + 6) = (4, 8)$. Is $8 = 2 \times 4$? Yes.

    What we have now The sum is on the line ✓.

  3. What we do Scale $(1, 2)$ by $-3$.

    Why Rule 2: every multiple must stay on the line, negatives too.

    How $-3 \times (1, 2) = (-3, -6)$. Is $-6 = 2 \times (-3)$? Yes.

    What we have now On the line ✓. (Scale by $0$: $(0, 0)$, and $0 = 2 \times 0$ ✓.) The line $y = 2x$ is a subspace.

  4. What we do Now test the shifted line $y = 2x + 1$ with the zero vector.

    Why Every subspace must contain $(0, 0)$; this is the quickest test.

    How Put $x = 0$: the line needs $y = 2 \times 0 + 1 = 1$. But the zero vector has $y = 0$, and $0 \ne 1$.

    What we have now $(0, 0)$ is not on it ✗.

  5. Check Adding fails too: $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 3)$ are on $y = 2x + 1$ ($1 = 0 + 1$, $3 = 2 + 1$). Their sum $(1, 4)$ needs $4 = 2 \times 1 + 1 = 3$: no ✗. Not a subspace.

7. The column space

Words used here

Column of $A$ = one vertical list of numbers in the matrix. Linear combination (mix) = $x_1 \times$ (col 1) $+ x_2 \times$ (col 2), for any numbers $x_1, x_2$. Column space $C(A)$ = every mix of the columns. Subspace = a vector space inside a bigger one (closed under adding and scaling). Plane through the origin = the flat sheet made by mixing two vectors that point in different directions. $Ax$ = the mix of the columns of $A$ with amounts $x$. $Ax = b$ has a solution = some amounts $x$ build exactly $b$. Independent columns = no column is a mix of the others.

The most important way to get a subspace out of a matrix: take its columns and all their combinations.

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \text{column 1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \text{column 2} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
Definition: Column space C(A)

The column space of $A$, written $C(A)$, is the set of all linear combinations of the columns of $A$:

$$ C(A) = \{\, x_1(\text{col } 1) + x_2(\text{col } 2) \ :\ \text{any numbers } x_1, x_2 \,\} $$

It is automatically a subspace, because a mix of mixes is still a mix.

  1. What we do Build the mix with $x_1 = 1$, $x_2 = 1$.

    Why Every such mix is, by definition, a member of $C(A)$.

    How $1 \times (1, 2, 4) = (1, 2, 4)$. $1 \times (3, 3, 1) = (3, 3, 1)$. Add component by component: $(1 + 3,\ 2 + 3,\ 4 + 1) = (4, 5, 5)$.

    What we have now $(4, 5, 5)$ is in $C(A)$.

  2. What we do Build the mix with $x_1 = 2$, $x_2 = -1$.

    Why Negative amounts are allowed too ("sell" column 2).

    How $2 \times (1, 2, 4) = (2, 4, 8)$. $-1 \times (3, 3, 1) = (-3, -3, -1)$. Add: $(2 - 3,\ 4 - 3,\ 8 - 1) = (-1, 1, 7)$.

    What we have now $(-1, 1, 7)$ is in $C(A)$.

  3. What we do Add these two members: $(4, 5, 5) + (-1, 1, 7)$.

    Why To see "a mix of mixes is still a mix", i.e. $C(A)$ is closed under adding.

    How $(4 - 1,\ 5 + 1,\ 5 + 7) = (3, 6, 12)$. The amounts add too: $x_1 = 1 + 2 = 3$, $x_2 = 1 + (-1) = 0$.

    What we have now Prediction: $(3, 6, 12) = 3 \times$ col 1 $+ 0 \times$ col 2.

  4. Check $3 \times (1, 2, 4) = (3, 6, 12)$ ✓. The sum is again a mix of the columns, so it stays in $C(A)$.

What shape is it?

The two columns point in different directions (neither is a multiple of the other). All their combinations fill a plane through the origin inside $\mathbb{R}^3$. It is not all of $\mathbb{R}^3$: two vectors can only make a flat sheet.

  1. What we do Test whether col 2 is a multiple of col 1.

    Why If it were, all mixes would lie on one line. If not, they fill a plane.

    How To turn col 1's first number $1$ into col 2's first number $3$, multiply by $3$. Try that on the second numbers: $3 \times 2 = 6$, but col 2 has $3$. $6 \ne 3$.

    What we have now No single number works, so the columns point in different directions.

  2. Check Two different directions through the origin make a flat sheet: $C(A)$ is a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (the purple sheet below).

Why the column space matters

$Ax$ is always a mix of the columns of $A$. So

$$ Ax = b \text{ has a solution} \iff b \text{ is in the column space } C(A). $$

This links solving equations to the geometry of subspaces. It is the starting point of the next lecture.

If the columns…their column space is

Bigger example: 5 vectors with 10 components each give a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{10}$. It is at most 5-dimensional (less if some vectors are mixes of others), and never $\mathbb{R}^5$, because the vectors have 10 components.

In finance
  • Symmetric matrices everywhere: put daily returns in a matrix $R$ (rows = days, columns = assets). Then $R^TR$ is symmetric. Its diagonal measures how much each asset moves; its off-diagonal measures how two assets move together. This is the idea behind the covariance matrix (Example 9).
  • Column space = what you can build: the columns of a payoff matrix are the assets. Every payoff you can create by buying them is in the column space. If a payoff is outside it, you cannot replicate it: the market is incomplete (Example 10).

7. How this is used with real options

  • Permutations: an option chain can be sorted by strike, by expiry or by volume; risk numbers must not depend on that order. Solvers also swap rows on purpose (partial pivoting) to avoid dividing by tiny numbers.
  • Transpose: the same data is used "per product" (the option chain) and "per scenario" (a risk or P&L grid). Pricing from state prices is $A^Tq$.
  • Symmetric matrices: covariance and correlation matrices are symmetric, like $A^TA$. $A^TA$ is exactly what least-squares hedging (the closest hedge) uses.
  • Vector spaces: the payoffs you can hedge are the column space of your instruments. Short-selling limits (long-only funds, margin rules) break the "subspace" property.

Example 3: PA = LU with a 2 × 2

Problem

Factor $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ as $PA = LU$.

Words used here

Pivot = the number used to clear below it; it can't be $0$. Row swap = trade two rows. $P$ = permutation matrix ($I$ with rows reordered). Multiplier = (number to remove) ÷ (pivot). $L$ = lower triangular, 1s on the diagonal and multipliers below. $U$ = upper triangular staircase. $I$ = identity, "do nothing".

  1. What we do Look at the first pivot spot (row 1, column 1).

    Why We will divide by it; it must not be $0$.

    How It holds $0$. Below it, row 2 has a $3$.

    What we have now Plan: swap rows 1 and 2.

  2. What we do Build $P$ and compute $PA$.

    Why $P$ does the swap for us: swap the rows of $I$.

    How Rows of $I$: $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$. Swapped: $(0, 1)$, $(1, 0)$. Then $PA$: row 1 = "take row 2 of $A$" $= (3, 4)$; row 2 = "take row 1 of $A$" $= (0, 2)$.

    What we have now The swapped matrix:

    $$ P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad PA = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} $$

  3. What we do Eliminate $PA$: clear under the pivot $3$.

    Why We need zeros under each pivot to get $U$.

    How The number under it is already $0$. Multiplier $= 0 \div 3 = 0$. Nothing to subtract.

    What we have now $U = PA$, and $L$ has $0$ below its diagonal, so $L = I$:

    $$ L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad U = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} $$

  4. Check $LU = IU = U = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = PA$ ✓.

$PA = LU$ with $L = I$ and $U = PA$.

Example 4: Transpose

Problem

Find the transpose of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 5 \\ 2 & 7 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. What is the entry in row 3, column 2 of $A^T$?

Words used here

Transpose $A^T$ = flip across the main diagonal: row $i$ of $A$ becomes column $i$ of $A^T$. Entry $(i, j)$ = the number in row $i$, column $j$. The rule $(A^T)_{ij} = A_{ji}$ says: to read $A^T$ at (row $i$, col $j$), look in $A$ at (row $j$, col $i$). $2 \times 3$ = 2 rows, 3 columns.

Rows become columns: row 1 $(1, 0, 5)$ becomes column 1; row 2 $(2, 7, 3)$ becomes column 2.

  1. What we do Find the shape of $A^T$.

    Why Rows and columns trade places, so the shape numbers swap.

    How $A$ is $2 \times 3$, so $A^T$ is $3 \times 2$.

    What we have now A table with 3 rows and 2 columns to fill.

  2. What we do Fill column 1 of $A^T$ with row 1 of $A$.

    Why Row 1 becomes column 1.

    How Row 1 of $A$ is $(1, 0, 5)$. Write it downwards: $(A^T)_{11} = 1$, $(A^T)_{21} = 0$, $(A^T)_{31} = 5$.

    What we have now Column 1 $= (1, 0, 5)$.

  3. What we do Fill column 2 of $A^T$ with row 2 of $A$.

    Why Row 2 becomes column 2.

    How Row 2 of $A$ is $(2, 7, 3)$. Downwards: $(A^T)_{12} = 2$, $(A^T)_{22} = 7$, $(A^T)_{32} = 3$.

    What we have now The full transpose:

    $$ A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 7 \\ 5 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \quad (2 \times 3 \to 3 \times 2) $$

  4. What we do Read entry (3, 2) of $A^T$.

    Why The question asks for it.

    How Row 3 of $A^T$ is $(5, 3)$; its column-2 number is $3$.

    What we have now $(A^T)_{32} = 3$.

Example 7: Subspace or not?

Problem

Which are subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^2$? (a) the line $y = 3x$   (b) the line $y = 3x + 1$   (c) the quarter plane $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$   (d) just $(0, 0)$

Words used here

Subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$ = a collection of 2-number vectors that contains $(0, 0)$ and is closed: adding two members, or multiplying a member by any number, gives a member. Line $y = 3x$ = all points whose second number is 3 times the first. Quarter plane = points with both numbers $\ge 0$. One failure of any test is enough to say "not a subspace".

  1. What we do (a) $y = 3x$: test zero, adding, scaling.

    Why Those are the three things a subspace needs.

    How Zero: $0 = 3 \times 0$ ✓. Add $(1, 3) + (2, 6) = (3, 9)$; $9 = 3 \times 3$ ✓. Scale $-2 \times (1, 3) = (-2, -6)$; $-6 = 3 \times (-2)$ ✓.

    What we have now (a) passes: subspace.

  2. What we do (b) $y = 3x + 1$: test zero first.

    Why Quickest test; if it fails we are done.

    How At $x = 0$ the line has $y = 3 \times 0 + 1 = 1$. The zero vector has $y = 0$. $0 \ne 1$.

    What we have now (b) fails: not a subspace.

  3. What we do (c) quarter plane: zero, then scaling by $-1$.

    Why Zero is in, so we need a harder test; negatives are the weak spot.

    How $(0, 0)$: both $\ge 0$ ✓. $(1, 1)$ is inside. $-1 \times (1, 1) = (-1, -1)$; is $-1 \ge 0$? No.

    What we have now (c) fails: not a subspace.

Example 9: Returns matrix → a symmetric "co-movement" matrix (finance)

Problem

Two stocks, three days. Daily returns (in %):

DayStock AStock B
1$1$$2$
2$0$$1$
3$1$$0$

Put them in $R$ (rows = days, columns = stocks) and compute $R^TR$. What do its entries mean?

Story first

Each stock's column is its "diary" of daily moves. Dotting a diary with itself measures how much that stock moves. Dotting two different diaries measures how much they move together. Stock A with B and B with A give the same answer, so the table is a mirror image.

Words used here

Return = how much a stock went up that day, in %. $R$ = the returns table: rows = days, columns = stocks. $R^T$ = its transpose. $R^TR$ entry (A, B) = (column A) · (column B), a dot product: multiply day by day, then add. Symmetric = mirror entries are equal. Covariance matrix = the same idea after subtracting each stock's average and dividing by the number of days; it measures risk.

$R = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, the same as Example 6, so

$$ R^TR = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix} $$

  1. What we do Build $R$ from the table.

    Why Each stock's diary of daily moves becomes one column.

    How Column A = days 1, 2, 3 of stock A = $(1, 0, 1)$. Column B = $(2, 1, 0)$.

    What we have now The $3 \times 2$ matrix $R$ above.

  2. What we do Entry (A, A) = column A · column A.

    Why A diary dotted with itself adds up the squared moves: how much A moves.

    How $1 \times 1 = 1$, $0 \times 0 = 0$, $1 \times 1 = 1$. Add: $1 + 0 + 1 = 2$.

    What we have now (A, A) $= 2$.

  3. What we do Entry (B, B) = column B · column B.

    Why How much B moves.

    How $2 \times 2 = 4$, $1 \times 1 = 1$, $0 \times 0 = 0$. Add: $4 + 1 + 0 = 5$.

    What we have now (B, B) $= 5$: B moves more than A.

  4. What we do Entry (A, B) = column A · column B.

    Why Big when both move up on the same days: how much they move together.

    How Day 1: $1 \times 2 = 2$. Day 2: $0 \times 1 = 0$. Day 3: $1 \times 0 = 0$. Add: $2 + 0 + 0 = 2$.

    What we have now (A, B) $= 2$. (B, A) multiplies the same pairs in the other order, so it is also $2$.

    EntryWorkMeaning
    (A, A) $= 2$$1^2 + 0^2 + 1^2$how much A moves
    (B, B) $= 5$$2^2 + 1^2 + 0^2$how much B moves (more than A)
    (A, B) $=$ (B, A) $= 2$$1 \cdot 2 + 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0$how much they move together

  5. Check Same numbers as Example 6 ($2, 2, 2, 5$) ✓, and the mirror pair (A, B) = (B, A) = 2 ✓: symmetric.

$R^TR$ is symmetric. With averages subtracted and scaled, this becomes the covariance matrix used in portfolio risk.

Two kinds of ticket, three kinds of weather. Mixing two tickets can only reach a flat sheet (a plane) of payoffs inside 3D. Some payoffs are on the sheet, some are not.

Payoff vector = (pays if low, pays if middle, pays if high). Bond = pays 1 always. $x$ bonds and $y$ stocks = the amounts we choose. Column space = every payoff $x \times$ bond $+ y \times$ stock.     Why In each scenario, the portfolio must pay exactly the target.

    What we have now 1 bond, 2 stocks.
