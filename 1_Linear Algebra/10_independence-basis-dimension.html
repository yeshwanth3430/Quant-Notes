1. The lecture in four words

Words used here

Vector = a list of numbers. Combination = multiply each vector by a number, then add. Space = a collection of vectors closed under adding and scaling.

WordQuestion it answersLooks atSaid about
IndependentCan a non-zero combination give $0$?combinations that give zeroa bunch of vectors
SpanWhat do all combinations fill?all combinationsa bunch of vectors
BasisIndependent and spanning?botha bunch of vectors
DimensionHow many vectors in a basis?a numbera space
Use the words correctly
  • Vectors are independent, span, or form a basis. (Not "the matrix is independent".)
  • A matrix has a rank. (Not "the rank of a subspace".)
  • A space has a dimension. (Not "the dimension of $A$", but "the dimension of the column space of $A$".)

These meet in one sentence: the rank of a matrix = the dimension of its column space.

The trading question behind this lecture

Products = vectors (their payoffs in each Nifty scenario). Independent: no product can be copied from the others. Span: all payoffs you can build. Basis: the smallest set of products that still builds everything. Dimension: how many truly different products the market really has.

2. Key fact: more unknowns than equations

Words used here

$m$ = number of equations (rows). $n$ = number of unknowns (columns). Pivot = leader of a row. Free variable = unknown whose column has no pivot; you may choose it. Nullspace = all solutions of $Ax = 0$.

Key fact

If $n > m$ (more unknowns than equations), then $Ax = 0$ has a solution other than $x = 0$.

Why: each pivot needs its own row, so there are at most $m$ pivots. There are $n$ columns, so at least $n - m$ columns have no pivot. Those are free variables. Set one of them to $1$, solve for the pivots, and you get a non-zero solution.

Example: 2 equations, 3 unknowns. $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$. Find a non-zero $c$ with $Ac = 0$.

  1. What we do Leader = the $2$ at the top of column 1. Clear the $1$ under it.

    Why this amount "How many 2's make 1?" $1 \div 2 = \tfrac12$. Take away half of row 1: $\tfrac12(2, 1, 1) = (1, \tfrac12, \tfrac12)$.

    How $(1 - 1,\ 2 - \tfrac12,\ -1 - \tfrac12) = (0, \tfrac32, -\tfrac32)$.

    What we have now Leaders in columns 1 and 2. Column 3 has no leader, so $c_3$ is free.

  2. What we do Set the free one to $1$: $c_3 = 1$.

    Why not 0 If we set it to 0, everything becomes 0 and we learn nothing. $1$ gives a non-zero answer.

  3. What we do Bottom row: $\tfrac32 c_2 - \tfrac32 \times 1 = 0$.

    How Move $-\tfrac32$ across: $\tfrac32 c_2 = \tfrac32$, so $c_2 = 1$.

  4. What we do Top row: $2c_1 + 1 \times 1 + 1 \times 1 = 0$.

    How $2c_1 + 2 = 0$, so $2c_1 = -2$, so $c_1 = -1$.

    What we have now $c = (-1, 1, 1)$, not all zeros.

  5. Check $-1 \times (2, 1) + 1 \times (1, 2) + 1 \times (1, -1) = (-2 + 1 + 1,\ -1 + 2 - 1) = (0, 0)$ ✓.

Market meaning

More products than scenarios ($n > m$) always means a zero-payoff combination exists. With 3 Nifty levels and 5 products, at least $5 - 3 = 2$ products are copies of the others.

3. Independence

Words used here

Combination = $c_1v_1 + c_2v_2 + \dots + c_nv_n$. Coefficients = the numbers $c_1, \dots, c_n$. Zero combination = all $c$'s are 0 (this always gives the zero vector, so it proves nothing).

Definition

Vectors $v_1, \dots, v_n$ are independent if

$$ c_1v_1 + c_2v_2 + \dots + c_nv_n = 0 \quad \text{only when} \quad c_1 = c_2 = \dots = c_n = 0. $$

They are dependent (the opposite) if some combination with at least one $c \ne 0$ gives the zero vector.

In child words: can you walk some steps along the vectors (forward or backward) and end up back home, without standing still? If yes: dependent. If the only way home is to not move at all: independent.

4. The independence test with a matrix

Words used here

Put the vectors as columns of a matrix $A$. Then a combination $c_1v_1 + \dots + c_nv_n$ is exactly $Ac$ (matrix times the list of coefficients). Nullspace $N(A)$ = all $c$ with $Ac = 0$. Rank $r$ = number of pivots.

The test
Columns independentColumns dependent
Nullspaceonly $\{0\}$contains some $c \ne 0$
Free variablesnoneat least one
Rank$r = n$ (every column a pivot column)$r < n$

Why this works: $Ac = 0$ says "$c_1$ of column 1 + $c_2$ of column 2 + … = zero column". So a non-zero $c$ in the nullspace is exactly a non-zero combination giving 0: dependence. And a free column is always a combination of the earlier pivot columns; that's what its special solution says.

Market meaning

Put each product's payoff vector as a column. A non-zero vector in the nullspace is a list of lots that pays zero in every scenario. If such a trade exists, some product is redundant, and its price must match the price of its copy, or there is arbitrage.

5. Span

Words used here

Combination = multiply and add. Space = closed under adding and scaling. Column space $C(A)$ = all combinations of the columns of $A$.

Definition

Vectors $v_1, \dots, v_\ell$ span a space when the space consists of all their combinations.

  • The columns of $A$ span the column space $C(A)$. That's how $C(A)$ was built.
  • The space they span is the smallest space containing them: any space holding the vectors must also hold all their combinations.
  • Spanning says nothing about extras: $(1,0), (0,1), (5,5)$ span $\mathbb{R}^2$, but $(5,5)$ is not needed.
VectorsWhat they span
$(1, 0)$the x-axis (a line)
$(1, 0), (2, 0)$still the x-axis: the second adds nothing
$(1, 0), (0, 1)$all of $\mathbb{R}^2$
$(1, 1, 2), (2, 2, 5)$a plane inside $\mathbb{R}^3$ (section 7)

6. Basis

Words used here

Independent = no non-zero combination gives 0 (nothing extra). Span = all combinations fill the space (nothing missing).

Definition

A basis for a space is a list of vectors with two properties:

  1. they are independent (not too many);
  2. they span the space (enough).

Why it matters: give me a basis, and you've told me the whole space: it is exactly all their combinations, and each vector in it is built in only one way (independence means no two recipes differ by a zero combination).

TryIndependent?Span $\mathbb{R}^3$?Basis for $\mathbb{R}^3$?
$(1,0,0), (0,1,0), (0,0,1)$yesyesyes (the standard basis)

$(1,1,2), (2,2,5), (3,3,7)$no: $(3,3,7) = (1,1,2) + (2,2,5)$no: still the planeno

Standard basis, checked like a child: $c_1(1,0,0) + c_2(0,1,0) + c_3(0,0,1) = (c_1, c_2, c_3)$. For this to be $(0,0,0)$ each $c$ must be 0. Independent ✓. And any $(x, y, z) = x(1,0,0) + y(0,1,0) + z(0,0,1)$. Spans ✓. With a matrix: the columns form $I$, and $N(I) = \{0\}$.

7. Testing a basis for R³

Words used here

Invertible = square matrix with a pivot in every row and column. Row exchange = swapping two rows when a leader spot holds a 0. Plane = a flat 2-direction piece.

Test for Rⁿ

$n$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ form a basis exactly when the $n \times n$ matrix with those columns is invertible.

First try: (1,1,2), (2,2,5), (3,3,8)

In the lecture, Strang adds $(3,3,8)$, hoping it leaves the plane. Let's check it slowly.

  1. What we do Look for a pattern. All three vectors have first number = second number: $(1,1,\cdot), (2,2,\cdot), (3,3,\cdot)$.

    Why it matters Adding or scaling such vectors keeps first = second. So every combination has first = second. $(1, 0, 0)$ can never be built. They can't span $\mathbb{R}^3$.

  2. What we do Find the combination. Want $a(1,1,2) + b(2,2,5) = (3,3,8)$.

    How First entry: $a + 2b = 3$, so $a = 3 - 2b$. Third entry: $2a + 5b = 8$. Put $a$ in: $2(3 - 2b) + 5b = 8$, so $6 - 4b + 5b = 8$, so $6 + b = 8$, so $b = 2$. Then $a = 3 - 4 = -1$.

    Check $-1(1,1,2) + 2(2,2,5) = (-1 + 4,\ -1 + 4,\ -2 + 10) = (3, 3, 8)$ ✓.

  3. What we have now $(3,3,8)$ lies in the plane of the first two. Dependent. Not a basis. (A small slip in the lecture; the plot below shows it.)

Second try: (1,1,2), (2,2,5), (3,4,8)

Break the pattern: second number $4 \ne 3$. Put them as columns and eliminate.

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 & 8 \end{bmatrix} $$

  1. What we do Leader = the $1$ at the top-left. Clear the $1$ and the $2$ under it.

    How Row 2 − 1 × row 1: $(1-1,\ 2-2,\ 4-3) = (0, 0, 1)$. Row 3 − 2 × row 1: $(2-2,\ 5-4,\ 8-6) = (0, 1, 2)$.

  2. What we see Row 2's leader spot (column 2) holds a $0$. But row 3 has a $1$ there.

    What we do Swap rows 2 and 3. Order of equations doesn't change the answers.

    What we have now $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$: leaders $1, 1, 1$, one in every row and column.

  3. Conclusion 3 pivots in a $3 \times 3$ matrix: invertible. Nullspace $= \{0\}$: independent. Rank 3 = number of rows: they span $\mathbb{R}^3$. Basis ✓.

8. Dimension

Words used here

Basis = independent + spanning. "Not unique" = there are many different ones. Dimension = a single number attached to a space.

The great fact

A space has many bases (any invertible $3 \times 3$ matrix gives a basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$ from its columns). But every basis has the same number of vectors. That number is the dimension of the space.

SpaceOne basisDimension
$\mathbb{R}^3$$(1,0,0), (0,1,0), (0,0,1)$3
$\mathbb{R}^n$columns of $I_n$$n$
plane $x = y$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$$(1,1,2), (2,2,5)$2
a line through 0one non-zero vector on it1
$\{0\}$ aloneempty list0
If you know the dimension d
  • More than $d$ vectors: must be dependent.
  • Fewer than $d$ vectors: can't span.
  • Exactly $d$ vectors: independent ⇔ they span ⇔ basis. Check one, you get the other free. (If $d$ independent vectors didn't span, a missing vector could be added and stay independent, giving $d + 1$ independent vectors: impossible.)

9. The big example: C(A) and N(A)

Words used here

Column space $C(A)$ = all combinations of the columns. Nullspace $N(A)$ = all $x$ with $Ax = 0$. Pivot column = column with a leader. Special solution = one free variable 1, other free variables 0, then solve. Rank $r$ = number of pivots.

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \text{columns: } (1,1,1),\ (2,1,2),\ (3,2,3),\ (1,1,1) $$

Notice: column 3 = column 1 + column 2, and column 4 = column 1.

  1. What we do Leader = the $1$ at the top of column 1. Clear the two $1$'s under it.

    How Row 2 − row 1: $(1-1,\ 1-2,\ 2-3,\ 1-1) = (0, -1, -1, 0)$. Row 3 − row 1: $(1-1,\ 2-2,\ 3-3,\ 1-1) = (0, 0, 0, 0)$.

    What we have now $U = \begin{bmatrix} \boxed{1} & 2 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & \boxed{-1} & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Leaders in columns 1 and 2. Rank $r = 2$.

  2. Column space The pivot columns of the original $A$, $(1,1,1)$ and $(2,1,2)$, are a basis for $C(A)$. So $\dim C(A) = 2 = r$.

    Why original A Elimination changes the columns themselves (the zero row of $U$ is not in $C(A)$). It keeps which columns are pivot columns.

  3. What we do First special solution: $x_3 = 1$, $x_4 = 0$.

    How Row 2 of $U$: $-x_2 - 1 = 0$, so $x_2 = -1$. Row 1: $x_1 + 2(-1) + 3(1) + 1(0) = 0$, so $x_1 - 2 + 3 = 0$, so $x_1 + 1 = 0$, so $x_1 = -1$.

    What we have now $s_1 = (-1, -1, 1, 0)$: "−col 1 − col 2 + col 3 = 0", i.e. col 3 = col 1 + col 2 ✓.

  4. What we do Second special solution: $x_3 = 0$, $x_4 = 1$.

    How Row 2: $-x_2 - 0 = 0$, so $x_2 = 0$. Row 1: $x_1 + 0 + 0 + 1 = 0$, so $x_1 = -1$.

    What we have now $s_2 = (-1, 0, 0, 1)$: "−col 1 + col 4 = 0", i.e. col 4 = col 1 ✓.

  5. Nullspace $s_1, s_2$ are independent (look at entries 3, 4: $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$) and every nullspace vector is a combination of them. So they are a basis for $N(A)$, and $\dim N(A) = 2$ = number of free variables $= n - r = 4 - 2$.

Other bases for $C(A)$?VerdictWhy
cols 1 and 3: $(1,1,1), (3,2,3)$yes2 vectors, not multiples, in a space of dimension 2
cols 2 and 3, cols 2 and 4, cols 3 and 4yessame reason
cols 1 and 4nothey are the same vector: dependent
$(2,2,2)$ and $(7,5,7)$yes$(2,2,2) = 2 \times$ col 1; $(7,5,7)$ = sum of all columns; not multiples of each other
The two dimension formulas $$ \dim C(A) = r \qquad\qquad \dim N(A) = n - r $$

Pivot columns count the column space; free columns count the nullspace. Together: $r + (n - r) = n$ = number of columns.

In finance
  • dim C(A) = rank = number of truly different products = number of scenarios you can control independently. Rank = number of scenarios: complete market.
  • dim N(A) = n − r = number of independent zero-payoff trades (redundancies). Each one is a price relation that must hold (e.g. put–call parity), or arbitrage appears.
  • A basis of products = a minimal set of instruments. Everything else in the market is a fixed recipe of them.

Worked examples

Example 1: How many truly different products does this Nifty market have? (finance, 3D)

Story

A trader's screen shows five products on Nifty, all expiring tomorrow. Nifty ends at 19,900, 20,000 or 20,100. The trader asks: are these five really different, or are some just copies of others? Which ones do I actually need? And are the prices fair?

Problem

Payoff tomorrow in points ÷ 100 (so 100 points = 1 unit):

ProductHow the payoff is computed19,90020,00020,100Price today
1. 20,000 PE$\max(20{,}000 - S, 0) \div 100$$1$$0$$0$$40$
2. Bondpays 1 always$1$$1$$1$$99$
3. 20,000 CE$\max(S - 20{,}000, 0) \div 100$$0$$0$$1$$40$
4. Future @ 20,000$(S - 20{,}000) \div 100$$-1$$0$$1$$0$
5. 20,000 straddlePE + CE in one ticket$1$$0$$1$$85$

(a) Are the five payoff vectors independent? (b) Find every dependency. (c) Find a basis and the dimension of what they span. Is the market complete? (d) Are the prices consistent?

Words used here

Each product = a vector of 3 payoffs (one per Nifty level) = one column. Independent = no product is a copy of the others. Dependency = a list of lots that pays zero at every level (a nullspace vector). Span = all payoffs you can build. Basis = the fewest products that still build everything. Dimension = how many products are in that basis. Complete market = every payoff over the 3 levels can be built.

(a) Independent? Step by step

  1. What we do Count: 5 products (columns), 3 levels (rows).

    Why Key fact: more unknowns than equations ($5 > 3$) means a non-zero combination giving zero must exist.

    What we have now Dependent, before any arithmetic. At least $5 - 3 = 2$ products are copies.

(b) Every dependency, step by step

  1. What we do Leader = the $1$ at the top of column 1 (PE). Under it: $0$ and $0$. Already clean.

  2. What we do Next leader: row 2, column 2 (bond) = $1$. Clear the $1$ under it in row 3.

    How $1 \div 1 = 1$ copy. Row 3 − row 2: $(0-0,\ 1-1,\ 1-0,\ 1-0,\ 1-0) = (0, 0, 1, 1, 1)$.

    In market words "Payoff at 20,100 minus payoff at 20,000" for each product.

  3. What we have now $U = \begin{bmatrix} \boxed{1} & 1 & 0 & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & \boxed{1} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \boxed{1} & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Leaders in columns 1, 2, 3 (PE, bond, CE). Free: column 4 (future) and column 5 (straddle).

  4. What we do First special solution: future $c_4 = 1$, straddle $c_5 = 0$.

    How Row 3: $c_3 + 1 + 0 = 0$, so $c_3 = -1$. Row 2: $c_2 = 0$. Row 1: $c_1 + 0 + 0 - 1 + 0 = 0$, so $c_1 = 1$.

    What we have now $s_1 = (1, 0, -1, 1, 0)$: buy 1 PE, sell 1 CE, buy 1 future.

    Check 19,900: $1 - 0 - 1 = 0$ ✓. 20,000: $0 - 0 + 0 = 0$ ✓. 20,100: $0 - 1 + 1 = 0$ ✓. This is put–call parity: CE − PE = future.

  5. What we do Second special solution: future $c_4 = 0$, straddle $c_5 = 1$.

    How Row 3: $c_3 + 0 + 1 = 0$, so $c_3 = -1$. Row 2: $c_2 = 0$. Row 1: $c_1 + 0 + 0 - 0 + 1 = 0$, so $c_1 = -1$.

    What we have now $s_2 = (-1, 0, -1, 0, 1)$: buy 1 straddle, sell 1 PE, sell 1 CE.

    Check 19,900: $1 - 1 - 0 = 0$ ✓. 20,000: $0$ ✓. 20,100: $1 - 0 - 1 = 0$ ✓. Of course: a straddle is a PE plus a CE.

(d) Are the prices consistent? Step by step

  1. What we do Price each zero-payoff trade (price today, buying costs +, selling gives −).

    Why A trade that pays nothing tomorrow in every case must cost nothing today. Otherwise someone gets free money.

  2. How, $s_1$ Buy PE $+40$, sell CE $-40$, buy future $+0$: total $40 - 40 + 0 = 0$ ✓. Parity holds.

  3. How, $s_2$ Buy straddle $+85$, sell PE $-40$, sell CE $-40$: total $85 - 80 = 5$ ✗. Not zero!

  4. What it means The straddle is 5 too expensive. Do the reverse of $s_2$: sell the straddle (receive 85), buy 1 PE and 1 CE (pay 80). Keep 5 now. Tomorrow what you owe on the straddle is exactly what your PE + CE pay you, at every level.

Five products, but only 3 are truly different (dimension 3; basis PE, bond, CE). The other two are copies, giving 2 price rules. Parity holds; the straddle is overpriced by 5, so there is an arbitrage.

Example 2: The same idea with two Nifty levels (finance, 2D)

Problem

Now Nifty ends at only 19,900 or 20,100. Products (points ÷ 100): 20,000 PE $(1, 0)$, 20,000 CE $(0, 1)$, future @ 20,000 $(-1, 1)$, bond $(1, 1)$. (a) Independent? (b) Is {bond, future} a basis? (c) Dimension?

Words used here

Vector = payoff at (19,900, 20,100). Basis for $\mathbb{R}^2$ = 2 independent vectors. Dimension of $\mathbb{R}^2$ = 2.

  1. (a) What we do Count: 4 vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$. More than 2, so dependent.

    Example bond = PE + CE: $(1,0) + (0,1) = (1,1)$ ✓. Also future = CE − PE: $(0,1) - (1,0) = (-1, 1)$ ✓.

  2. (b) What we do Test bond and future: columns $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

    How Leader $1$. Row 2 − row 1: $(1-1,\ 1-(-1)) = (0, 2)$. Leaders $1$ and $2$: two pivots, invertible.

    What we have now Independent, and 2 vectors in a space of dimension 2, so they also span. Basis ✓.

  3. Build the PE from them Want $a(1,1) + b(-1,1) = (1,0)$. First: $a - b = 1$. Second: $a + b = 0$. Add the two: $2a = 1$, $a = \tfrac12$; then $b = -\tfrac12$. So PE $= \tfrac12$ bond $- \tfrac12$ future. Check 19,900: $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$ ✓; 20,100: $\tfrac12 - \tfrac12 = 0$ ✓.

Dimension 2: any two non-parallel products are a basis. {PE, CE}, {bond, future}, {PE, bond} … all work. The other two products are always recipes of the chosen pair.

Example 3: Independent or dependent?

Problem

Decide for each set: (i) $(1, 2, 3), (2, 4, 6)$; (ii) $(1, 0, 1), (0, 1, 1), (1, 1, 0)$; (iii) $(1, 2), (3, 1), (0, 0)$.

  1. (i) Is one a multiple of the other? $2 \times (1, 2, 3) = (2, 4, 6)$ ✓. So $2v_1 - v_2 = 0$: dependent.

  2. (ii) What we do Put them in columns: $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Eliminate.

    How Row 3 − row 1: $(0, 1, -1)$. Then row 3 − row 2: $(0, 0, -2)$.

    What we have now Leaders $1, 1, -2$: three pivots, no free variables. Independent (and a basis for $\mathbb{R}^3$).

  3. (iii) Contains the zero vector: $0 \cdot (1,2) + 0 \cdot (3,1) + 1 \cdot (0,0) = 0$. Dependent. (Also: 3 vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$.)

Example 4: Bases and dimensions of C(A) and N(A)

Problem

$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 \\ 3 & 6 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Find a basis and the dimension of $C(A)$ and $N(A)$.

  1. What we do Leader $1$. Clear the $2$ and $3$ under it.

    How Row 2 − 2 × row 1: $(2-2,\ 4-4,\ 1-0) = (0, 0, 1)$. Row 3 − 3 × row 1: $(3-3,\ 6-6,\ 1-0) = (0, 0, 1)$.

  2. What we do Column 2 has no leader (both 0). Next leader: row 2, column 3 = $1$. Row 3 − row 2 $= (0, 0, 0)$.

    What we have now Leaders in columns 1 and 3. Rank 2. Free: $x_2$.

  3. Column space Basis: columns 1 and 3 of the original $A$: $(1, 2, 3)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$. Dimension 2.

  4. Nullspace $x_2 = 1$: row 2 says $x_3 = 0$; row 1 says $x_1 + 2 = 0$, so $x_1 = -2$. Basis: $(-2, 1, 0)$. Dimension $1 = 3 - 2$.

    Check $-2 \times$ col 1 + col 2 $= (-2 + 2,\ -4 + 4,\ -6 + 6) = 0$ ✓.

$\dim C(A) = 2$ with basis $(1,2,3), (0,1,1)$; $\dim N(A) = 1$ with basis $(-2, 1, 0)$.

Example 5: A basis for the plane x + y + z = 0

Problem

The plane of all $(x, y, z)$ with $x + y + z = 0$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$. Find a basis and its dimension.

  1. What we do See it as a nullspace: it's $N(A)$ for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

    What we have now One leader (column 1), rank 1. Free: $y$ and $z$. So $\dim = 3 - 1 = 2$.

  2. How $y = 1, z = 0$: $x + 1 = 0$, $x = -1$, giving $(-1, 1, 0)$. $y = 0, z = 1$: $x = -1$, giving $(-1, 0, 1)$.

Basis $(-1, 1, 0), (-1, 0, 1)$; dimension 2. (Market reading: with 3 Nifty levels and one bond, these are the payoffs with "total across levels = 0".)

1. Can 4 vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$ be independent? Can 2 vectors span $\mathbb{R}^3$?

No (more than 3 in dimension 3 are dependent). No (fewer than 3 can't span).

2. Are $(1, 1)$ and $(2, 3)$ a basis for $\mathbb{R}^2$?

$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$: row 2 − row 1 $= (0, 1)$. Two pivots, invertible: yes.

3. A $5 \times 8$ matrix has rank 4. What are $\dim C(A)$ and $\dim N(A)$?

$\dim C(A) = 4$, $\dim N(A) = 8 - 4 = 4$.

4. Why is any set containing the zero vector dependent?

Take coefficient 1 on the zero vector and 0 on everything else: a non-zero combination that gives 0.

5. In Example 1, what is the dimension of the span of {PE, CE, straddle}?

2: straddle = PE + CE, and PE, CE are independent.

6. Market: 4 Nifty levels, 6 products with rank 4. Complete? How many independent price rules?

Complete (rank = 4 levels). $6 - 4 = 2$ zero-payoff trades, so 2 price rules.
