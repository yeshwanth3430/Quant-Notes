1. The lecture in five questions

Words used here

$Ax = b$ = the whole system in one line: matrix $A$ (the coefficients) times the unknowns $x$ equals the right sides $b$. Elimination = subtract a multiple of one equation from another to remove an unknown. Back substitution = solve the bottom equation first, then climb. Pivot = the non-zero leader used to clear the numbers below it. Step matrix $E$ = a matrix that does one elimination step when multiplied on the left.

#QuestionShort answer
1How do we solve $Ax = b$?Elimination: remove unknowns one at a time. Every computer program does this.
2Then what?Back substitution: solve the last equation first, then go upwards.
3Can it go wrong?Yes, if a $0$ lands where we need a pivot. Sometimes a row swap fixes it, sometimes nothing can.
4How do matrices come in?Each elimination step is "multiply by a simple matrix $E$".
5Can a step be undone?Yes. If a step subtracts, its undo adds back.

2. Warm-up: two equations

Words used here

Equation = "left side = right side", like a balanced see-saw. Unknown = a hidden number ($x$, $y$). Coefficient = the number in front of an unknown (in $2x$ it is $2$). Eliminate = make an unknown disappear from an equation. Back substitution = put a known value into the equation above. Check = put the answer into the original equations.

$$ \begin{aligned} x + y &= 3 &&\text{(equation 1)} \\ 2x + 3y &= 8 &&\text{(equation 2)} \end{aligned} $$

Idea: make $x$ disappear from equation 2. Equation 2 has $2x$, equation 1 has $1x$. So take 2 times equation 1 away from equation 2.

  1. What we do Decide how many copies of equation 1 to take away from equation 2.

    Why this amount Equation 2 has $2x$. Equation 1 has $1x$. "How many $1x$ make $2x$?" $2 \div 1 = 2$. So 2 copies.

    How Two copies of equation 1: $2 \times x = 2x$, $\ 2 \times y = 2y$, $\ 2 \times 3 = 6$. So $2x + 2y = 6$.

    What we have now The amount to remove: $2x + 2y = 6$.

  2. What we do Take $2x + 2y = 6$ away from $2x + 3y = 8$, box by box.

    Why Taking the same amount off both sides of a balanced see-saw keeps it balanced, and the $x$ boxes cancel.

    How $x$: $2 - 2 = 0$. $\ y$: $3 - 2 = 1$. $\ $Right side: $8 - 6 = 2$.

    $x$$y$right side
    Equation 2$2$$3$$8$
    minus 2 × equation 1$-2$$-2$$-6$
    New equation 2$0$$1$$2$

    What we have now New equation 2: $0x + 1y = 2$, that is, $y = 2$.

  3. What we do Put $y = 2$ into equation 1 (back substitution).

    Why Equation 1 then has only one unknown left, $x$.

    How $x + 2 = 3$. Take $2$ from both sides: $x = 3 - 2 = 1$.

    What we have now $x = 1$, $y = 2$.

  4. Check, equation 1 $x + y = 1 + 2 = 3$. Right side $3$ ✓.

    Check, equation 2 $2x = 2 \times 1 = 2$. $\ 3y = 3 \times 2 = 6$. $\ 2 + 6 = 8$. Right side $8$ ✓.

Why subtracting equations is allowed

An equation is a balanced scale: the left side weighs the same as the right side. If you take the same amount off both sides, it stays balanced. "2 × equation 1" is the same amount on both sides ($2x + 2y$ and $6$), so removing it keeps equation 2 true.

That is the whole idea of elimination. The rest of the lecture does exactly this with three equations, then writes it with matrices.

3. Elimination on three equations

Words used here

Matrix $A$ = the rectangle of coefficients. Right side $b$ = the numbers after "=". Augmented matrix = $A$ with $b$ stuck on as a last column. Row = one equation. Pivot = the non-zero leader used to clear the numbers below it. Multiplier = (number to remove) ÷ (pivot). Upper triangular $U$ = staircase: only zeros below the diagonal. Diagonal = top-left to bottom-right. Determinant = one number from a square matrix (here, the pivots multiplied).

$$ \begin{aligned} x + 2y + z &= 2 \\ 3x + 8y + z &= 12 \\ 4y + z &= 2 \end{aligned} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 8 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 12 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}}_{b} $$

We only need the numbers, so from now on we write just the rows of $A$ with $b$ tacked on at the end. This is called the augmented matrix ("augmented" = something added):

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 8 & 1 & 12 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 & 2 \end{array}\right] $$
Definition: Pivot and multiplier

The pivot is the number we use to knock out the numbers below it. The first pivot is the top-left number, $1$.

The multiplier is how many times the pivot row we subtract: (number to remove) ÷ (pivot).

A pivot is never allowed to be 0, because you cannot divide by 0.

Step 1: clear the 3 below the first pivot

  1. What we do Choose the first pivot: the top-left number of row 1, which is $1$.

    Why We want zeros under it, so rows 2 and 3 lose their $x$.

    What we have now Under the pivot: $3$ in row 2, $0$ in row 3.

  2. What we do Find the multiplier for row 2.

    Why this amount "How many pivots make 3?" $3 \div 1 = 3$.

    How Three copies of row 1: $3 \times (1, 2, 1 \mid 2) = (3, 6, 3 \mid 6)$.

    What we have now Multiplier $3$; the amount to take away is $(3, 6, 3 \mid 6)$.

  3. What we do Row 2 minus 3 × row 1, box by box.

    How col 1: $3 - 3 = 0$. col 2: $8 - 6 = 2$. col 3: $1 - 3 = -2$. right side: $12 - 6 = 6$.

    Why It already has $0$ under the first pivot. Multiplier $0 \div 1 = 0$: take away nothing.

    What we have now $x$ is gone from rows 2 and 3:

  1. What we do Choose the second pivot: row 2, column 2, which is $2$.

    Why Row 1 is finished. Now we clear column 2 below row 2, so row 3 loses its $y$.

    What we have now Under the pivot $2$ sits a $4$ in row 3.

  2. What we do Find the multiplier.

    Why this amount "How many 2's make 4?" $4 \div 2 = 2$.

    How Two copies of row 2: $2 \times (0, 2, -2 \mid 6) = (0, 4, -4 \mid 12)$.

    What we have now Multiplier $2$; amount to take away $(0, 4, -4 \mid 12)$.

  3. What we do Row 3 minus 2 × row 2, box by box.

    How col 1: $0 - 0 = 0$. col 2: $4 - 4 = 0$. col 3: $1 - (-4) = 1 + 4 = 5$. right side: $2 - 12 = -10$.

    What we have now New row 3 $= (0, 0, 5 \mid -10)$.

The result: a staircase

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} \boxed{1} & 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & \boxed{2} & -2 & 6 \\ 0 & 0 & \boxed{5} & -10 \end{array}\right] $$
Definition: Upper triangular U

Everything below the diagonal is $0$. The matrix $A$ has become

$$ U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \text{and } b \text{ has become } c = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 6 \\ -10 \end{bmatrix}. $$

The pivots are $1$, $2$, $5$, sitting on the diagonal. The goal of elimination is always: turn $A$ into $U$.

A bonus fact for later: multiplying the pivots gives the determinant, $1 \times 2 = 2$, then $2 \times 5 = 10$.

4. Back substitution

Words used here

Staircase $U$ = zeros below the diagonal, so the last equation has only one unknown. Back substitution = solve that last equation, put its answer into the equation above, and keep climbing. Original equations = the ones we started with, before elimination. Check = put the answer into each original equation and compare both sides.

Write the staircase as equations again:

$$ \begin{aligned} x + 2y + z &= 2 \\ 2y - 2z &= 6 \\ 5z &= -10 \end{aligned} $$

The last equation has only one unknown, so start at the bottom and climb up:

  1. What we do Solve the bottom equation $5z = -10$.

    Why It has only one unknown, $z$.

    How "5 times what is $-10$?" Divide both sides by $5$: $z = -10 \div 5 = -2$.

    What we have now $z = -2$.

  2. What we do Put $z = -2$ into the middle equation $2y - 2z = 6$.

    Why Then only $y$ is unknown there.

    How $-2z = -2 \times (-2) = +4$. So the equation is $2y + 4 = 6$.

    How, next Take $4$ from both sides: $2y = 6 - 4 = 2$.

    How, last Divide by $2$: $y = 2 \div 2 = 1$.

    What we have now $y = 1$, $z = -2$.

    How $2y = 2 \times 1 = 2$. So $x + 2 + (-2) = 2$.

    How, next $2 + (-2) = 0$, so $x + 0 = 2$, so $x = 2$.

    What we have now $(x, y, z) = (2, 1, -2)$.

    Check, row 2 $3x + 8y + z = 3 \times 2 + 8 \times 1 + (-2) = 6 + 8 - 2 = 12$. Right side $12$ ✓.

    Check, row 3 $4y + z = 4 \times 1 + (-2) = 4 - 2 = 2$. Right side $2$ ✓.

The two-part recipe

1. Elimination (top to bottom): turn $A$ into the staircase $U$, doing the same to $b$.

2. Back substitution (bottom to top): solve the last equation, then work upwards.

5. When elimination gets stuck

Words used here

Pivot spot = the diagonal place where the next leader should sit. Pivot = a non-zero leader in that spot. Multiplier = (number to remove) ÷ (pivot), so a pivot of $0$ would mean dividing by $0$: impossible. Row swap = trade two rows (with their right sides). Singular = elimination runs out of pivots, so there is never exactly one answer: either none, or infinitely many.

Elimination needs a nonzero pivot at every step, because the multiplier is (number to remove) ÷ (pivot), and you cannot divide by $0$. So at each pivot spot there are three possibilities:

What is in the pivot spot?What to doResult
a nonzero numberuse it as the pivot✓ carry on
$0$, but a nonzero number somewhere below itswap that row up✓ carry on (temporary trouble)
$0$, and only zeros below itnothing can help✗ no pivot here: the matrix is singular
Why swapping rows is allowed

The rows are just equations in a list. Writing the same equations in a different order does not change their answer: "$x = 1$ and $y = 2$" is the same as "$y = 2$ and $x = 1$". So we may swap any two rows (with their right-hand sides).

Why Case 2 is stuck: row 3 was a copy in disguise

In Case 2, row 3 of the matrix is made from rows 1 and 2:

$$ 2 \times (3, 8, 1) - 6 \times (1, 2, 1) = (6 - 6,\ 16 - 12,\ 2 - 6) = (0, 4, -4) = \text{row 3}. $$

  1. What we do Make 2 copies of row 2.

    How $2 \times 3 = 6$, $\ 2 \times 8 = 16$, $\ 2 \times 1 = 2$: $(6, 16, 2)$.

  2. What we do Make 6 copies of row 1.

    How $6 \times 1 = 6$, $\ 6 \times 2 = 12$, $\ 6 \times 1 = 6$: $(6, 12, 6)$.

  3. What we do Take the second from the first, box by box.

    How $6 - 6 = 0$, $\ 16 - 12 = 4$, $\ 2 - 6 = -4$.

    What we have now $(0, 4, -4)$: exactly row 3 of the matrix.

So row 3 is not a new equation. Elimination "discovers" this by wiping it out completely. This is the same idea as Lecture 02: one row (or column) that is a mix of the others makes the matrix singular.

Summary of section 5
  • 0 in a pivot spot, nonzero below: swap rows. Temporary trouble, still solvable with one answer.
  • 0 in a pivot spot, nothing nonzero below: no pivot. The matrix is singular: no solution or infinitely many.

6. Each step is a matrix

Words used here

Matrix on the left = a list of recipes, one per row: row $i$ of the left matrix says how much of each row to take. Identity $I$ = 1s on the diagonal, 0s elsewhere: "keep every row as it is". Step matrix (elimination matrix) $E_{ij}$ = $I$ with minus the multiplier at (row $i$, column $j$); it does one elimination step. Associative law = brackets can move. Order matters = $AB$ and $BA$ are usually different.

Every elimination step ("row 2 minus 3 × row 1") can be written as multiplying by a matrix. That is how computers store and repeat the steps. Three facts make it work:

#FactIn one line
1Matrix on the left mixes rowsEach row of the answer = a mix of the rows, using the numbers in that row of the left matrix.
2The identity $I$ does nothingEach row says "take 1 of my own row, nothing else".
3Step matrix $E$Start with $I$ and put minus the multiplier in the spot being cleared.

6.1 Matrix on the left = mixing rows

How to read it

To get row $i$ of the answer, read row $i$ of the left matrix as a recipe: its numbers say how much of each row to take.

Example: if row 2 of the left matrix is $(-2,\ 1)$, then row 2 of the answer $= -2 \times$ (row 1) $+ 1 \times$ (row 2).

Example A: a 2 × 2 step, done with a matrix

The warm-up of section 2 did "equation 2 minus 2 × equation 1" on

$$ M = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{rows of } x + y \text{ and } 2x + 3y). $$

The matrix that does this step is

$$ E = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. $$

just like $2 \times (3 \times 4) = (2 \times 3) \times 4$. This is called the associative law.

Watch out

The answers differ in one spot ($6$ vs $0$), so $AB$ and $BA$ are usually different. You may move the brackets, but never swap the order. In elimination the order is fixed: step 1 happens first, so its matrix sits closest to $A$.

7. Undoing a step

Words used here

Step matrix $E_{21}$ = identity with $-3$ at (row 2, column 1); it does "row 2 minus 3 × row 1". Inverse $E_{21}^{-1}$ = the undo matrix: it does "row 2 plus 3 × row 1". Identity $I$ = the do-nothing matrix. $E^{-1}E = I$ means "do, then undo = nothing happened".

Step 1 subtracted 3 × row 1 from row 2. To undo it, add 3 × row 1 back:

$$ E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad E_{21}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ \color{#2e7d4f}{+3} & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
Example: undo on the right side

Step 1 turned $b = (2, 12, 2)$ into $(2, 6, 2)$, because $12 - 3 \times 2 = 6$. The undo adds it back: $6 + 3 \times 2 = 12$, giving $(2, 12, 2)$ again ✓.

  1. What we do Do the step: entry 2 minus 3 × entry 1.

    How $3 \times 2 = 6$. $\ 12 - 6 = 6$.

    What we have now $(2, 6, 2)$.

  2. What we do Undo it: entry 2 plus 3 × entry 1.

    Why Adding back exactly what was taken away returns the start.

    How $3 \times 2 = 6$. $\ 6 + 6 = 12$.

    What we have now $(2, 12, 2)$, the original $b$ ✓.

Doing the step and then the undo changes nothing: $E_{21}^{-1}E_{21} = I$. This is what inverse means: the undo button from Lecture 02.

  1. What we do Check row 2 of $E_{21}^{-1}E_{21}$ (rows 1 and 3 of $E_{21}^{-1}$ just copy rows 1 and 3 of $E_{21}$, which are already rows of $I$).

    How Recipe $(3, 1, 0)$ on the rows of $E_{21}$: $3 \times (1, 0, 0) = (3, 0, 0)$. Add row 2 of $E_{21}$, $(-3, 1, 0)$: $(3 - 3,\ 0 + 1,\ 0 + 0) = (0, 1, 0)$.

    What we have now $(0, 1, 0)$ = row 2 of $I$ ✓. So $E_{21}^{-1}E_{21} = I$.

8. Swapping rows

Words used here

Row swap = trade the places of two rows. Permutation matrix $P$ = the identity with its rows swapped; multiplying by it swaps things. Left = $P$ written before the matrix ($PM$): works on rows. Right = $P$ written after ($MP$): works on columns.

Section 5 sometimes needed a row swap. That is a matrix too, called a permutation matrix $P$.

Recipe: swap the same rows of the identity $I$.

$$ P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad\text{(rows of } I \text{ swapped)} $$
Put $P$…Result for $\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$Effect
on the left: $PM$$\begin{bmatrix} c & d \\ a & b \end{bmatrix}$swaps the rows
on the right: $MP$$\begin{bmatrix} b & a \\ d & c \end{bmatrix}$swaps the columns

  1. What we do Work out $PM$ row by row.

    How Row 1 of $P$ is $(0, 1)$: $0 \times (a, b) + 1 \times (c, d) = (c, d)$. Row 2 of $P$ is $(1, 0)$: $1 \times (a, b) + 0 \times (c, d) = (a, b)$.

    What we have now The rows traded places.

  2. What we do Work out $MP$ column by column.

    Why A matrix on the right mixes columns: column $j$ of $P$ is the recipe for column $j$ of the answer.

    How Column 1 of $P$ is $(0, 1)$: $0 \times (a, c) + 1 \times (b, d) = (b, d)$. Column 2 of $P$ is $(1, 0)$: $(a, c)$.

    What we have now The columns traded places.

Left vs right

Matrix on the left → works on rows. Matrix on the right → works on columns. Elimination always works on rows, so its matrices go on the left.

This also shows $PM \ne MP$: order matters.

In finance

Whenever a quant model needs to solve equations (portfolio weights, hedge ratios, calibrating a curve to several prices), the computer uses exactly this: elimination to a staircase, then back substitution. In Python, numpy.linalg.solve does it for you. Example 9 in the Examples tab solves a small portfolio this way.

1. The agenda: what question are we solving?

The question

A client comes to your desk and says: "I want a position that pays me these amounts depending on where Nifty expires." You can only trade what is on the screen: a few CEs, PEs, a future, a bond. The question is:

How many lots of each product should I buy or sell so that, at every Nifty level, my position pays exactly what the client wants?

And three follow-ups: Is it possible at all? Is there only one way, or many? And do the prices on the screen make sense, or is there free money (arbitrage)?

4. Walkthrough: the market the lab starts with

A = buy 20,100 PE, B = buy 19,900 CE, C = buy 20,000 PE. The client wants 3 / 2 / 2 (i.e. 300 / 200 / 200 points) at 19,900 / 20,000 / 20,100.

  1. What we do Work out what 1 lot of each product pays at each level.

    How A (20,100 PE) at 19,900: $\max(20{,}100 - 19{,}900, 0) = 200$ points → 2. At 20,000: 100 → 1. At 20,100: 0. So A = (2, 1, 0). Same way B (19,900 CE) = (0, 1, 2) and C (20,000 PE) = (1, 0, 0).

  2. What we do Write one equation per Nifty level.

    How At 19,900: $2x + 0y + 1z = 3$. At 20,000: $1x + 1y + 0z = 2$. At 20,100: $0x + 2y + 0z = 2$.

    $$ [A \mid b] = \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 2 & 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 & 2 \end{array}\right] $$

  3. Step 1 Pivot = 2 (row 1). Under it: 1. Multiplier $= 1 \div 2 = \tfrac12$. Row 2 − ½ × row 1: $(1-1,\ 1-0,\ 0-\tfrac12 \mid 2-\tfrac32) = (0, 1, -\tfrac12 \mid \tfrac12)$.

    As a matrix $E_{21}$ = identity with $-\tfrac12$ in row 2, column 1.

  4. Step 2 Row 3 already has 0 under the first pivot. Nothing to do ($E_{31}$ is just $I$).

  5. Step 3 Pivot = 1 (row 2, column 2). Under it: 2. Multiplier $= 2 \div 1 = 2$. Row 3 − 2 × row 2: $(0,\ 2-2,\ 0+1 \mid 2-1) = (0, 0, 1 \mid 1)$.

    As a matrix $E_{32}$ = identity with $-2$ in row 3, column 2.

  6. What we have now The staircase $U$ and the new right side $c$:

7. How this is used with real options

  • Replicating a client's payoff: structured products and custom payoffs are built exactly this way, by mixing listed CEs, PEs and futures.
  • Pricing it: once you have the lots, the fair price is just lots × premiums added up. That's what you quote.
  • Spotting copies and arbitrage: a missing pivot means two recipes give the same payoff. If their prices differ on the NSE option chain, buy the cheap one and sell the dear one.
  • Knowing what you can't hedge: a zero row with a non-zero right side tells you in advance that the payoff isn't buildable from these instruments.
  • Reusing $E$: $E$ depends only on the products, not on the client. A new client with wish $b'$ needs only $c' = Eb'$ and back substitution, with no new elimination. Example: $b' = (1, 0, 0)$ gives $c' = (1,\ -\tfrac12,\ 1 - 0 + 0) = (1, -\tfrac12, 1)$, so $z = 1$, $y = 0$, $x = 0$: just 1 lot of the 20,000 PE, which indeed pays (1, 0, 0).
  • Before expiry: with the days-left slider above 0, the same maths finds the lots that match the client's wish on that day, using option values (payoff + time value) instead of expiry payoffs. That match only holds for that day and IV, so real desks re-size (re-hedge) as time passes.
