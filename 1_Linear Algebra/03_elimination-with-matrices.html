1. The lecture in five questions

#QuestionShort answer
1How do we solve $Ax = b$?Elimination: remove unknowns one at a time. Every computer program does this.
2Then what?Back substitution: solve the last equation first, then go upwards.
3Can it go wrong?Yes, if a $0$ lands where we need a pivot. Sometimes a row swap fixes it, sometimes nothing can.
4How do matrices come in?Each elimination step is "multiply by a simple matrix $E$".
5Can a step be undone?Yes. If a step subtracts, its undo adds back.

2. Warm-up: two equations

$$ \begin{aligned} x + y &= 3 &&\text{(equation 1)} \\ 2x + 3y &= 8 &&\text{(equation 2)} \end{aligned} $$

Idea: make $x$ disappear from equation 2. Equation 2 has $2x$, equation 1 has $1x$. So take 2 times equation 1 away from equation 2:

$x$$y$right side
Equation 2$2$$3$$8$
minus 2 × equation 1$-2$$-2$$-6$
New equation 2$0$$1$$2$

The new equation 2 says $y = 2$. Put that into equation 1: $x + 2 = 3$, so $x = 1$.

Check: $1 + 2 = 3$ ✓ and $2(1) + 3(2) = 8$ ✓.

Why subtracting equations is allowed

An equation is a balanced scale: the left side weighs the same as the right side. If you take the same amount off both sides, it stays balanced. "2 × equation 1" is the same amount on both sides ($2x + 2y$ and $6$), so removing it keeps equation 2 true.

That is the whole idea of elimination. The rest of the lecture does exactly this with three equations, then writes it with matrices.

3. Elimination on three equations

$$ \begin{aligned} x + 2y + z &= 2 \\ 3x + 8y + z &= 12 \\ 4y + z &= 2 \end{aligned} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 8 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 12 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}}_{b} $$

We only need the numbers, so from now on we write just the rows of $A$ with $b$ tacked on at the end. This is called the augmented matrix ("augmented" = something added):

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 8 & 1 & 12 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 & 2 \end{array}\right] $$
Definition: Pivot and multiplier

The pivot is the number we use to knock out the numbers below it. The first pivot is the top-left number, $1$.

The multiplier is how many times the pivot row we subtract: (number to remove) ÷ (pivot).

A pivot is never allowed to be 0, because you cannot divide by 0.

Step 1: clear the 3 below the first pivot

Pivot $= 1$, number to remove $= 3$, so the multiplier is $3 \div 1 = 3$. Row 2 minus 3 × row 1:

col 1col 2col 3right side
Row 2$3$$8$$1$$12$
minus 3 × row 1$-3$$-6$$-3$$-6$
New row 2$0$$2$$-2$$6$

Row 3 already has $0$ under the first pivot, so its multiplier is $0$ and nothing changes.

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} \boxed{1} & 2 & 1 & 2 \\ \color{#b42318}{0} & 2 & -2 & 6 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 & 2 \end{array}\right] $$

$x$ is now gone from rows 2 and 3.

Step 2: clear the 4 below the second pivot

The second pivot is the $2$ in row 2. Number to remove $= 4$, so the multiplier is $4 \div 2 = 2$. Row 3 minus 2 × row 2:

col 1col 2col 3right side
Row 3$0$$4$$1$$2$
minus 2 × row 2$0$$-4$$+4$$-12$
New row 3$0$$0$$5$$-10$

(Careful: $-2 \times (-2) = +4$, so $1 + 4 = 5$.)

The result: a staircase

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} \boxed{1} & 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & \boxed{2} & -2 & 6 \\ 0 & 0 & \boxed{5} & -10 \end{array}\right] $$
Definition: Upper triangular U

Everything below the diagonal is $0$. The matrix $A$ has become

$$ U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \text{and } b \text{ has become } c = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 6 \\ -10 \end{bmatrix}. $$

The pivots are $1$, $2$, $5$, sitting on the diagonal. The goal of elimination is always: turn $A$ into $U$.

A bonus fact for later: multiplying the pivots gives the determinant, $1 \times 2 \times 5 = 10$.

4. Back substitution

Write the staircase as equations again:

$$ \begin{aligned} x + 2y + z &= 2 \\ 2y - 2z &= 6 \\ 5z &= -10 \end{aligned} $$

The last equation has only one unknown, so start at the bottom and climb up:

StepEquationWorkResult
1$5z = -10$divide by 5$z = -2$
2$2y - 2z = 6$$2y - 2(-2) = 6 \Rightarrow 2y + 4 = 6 \Rightarrow 2y = 2$$y = 1$
3$x + 2y + z = 2$$x + 2(1) + (-2) = 2 \Rightarrow x + 0 = 2$$x = 2$

Check in the original equations with $(x, y, z) = (2, 1, -2)$:

$x + 2y + z$$2 + 2 - 2 = 2$$2$ ✓
$3x + 8y + z$$6 + 8 - 2 = 12$$12$ ✓

The two-part recipe

1. Elimination (top to bottom): turn $A$ into the staircase $U$, doing the same to $b$.

2. Back substitution (bottom to top): solve the last equation, then work upwards.

5. When elimination gets stuck

Elimination needs a nonzero pivot at every step, because the multiplier is (number to remove) ÷ (pivot), and you cannot divide by $0$. So at each pivot spot there are three possibilities:

What is in the pivot spot?What to doResult
a nonzero numberuse it as the pivot✓ carry on
$0$, but a nonzero number somewhere below itswap that row up✓ carry on (temporary trouble)
$0$, and only zeros below itnothing can help✗ no pivot here: the matrix is singular
Why swapping rows is allowed

The rows are just equations in a list. Writing the same equations in a different order does not change their answer: "$x = 1$ and $y = 2$" is the same as "$y = 2$ and $x = 1$". So we may swap any two rows (with their right-hand sides).

First, three tiny examples (two equations each)

Example A: normal, nothing goes wrong $$ x + y = 3, \qquad 2x + 3y = 8 $$

Pivot spot 1 holds $1$ (not zero) ✓. Equation 2 minus 2 × equation 1:

$x$$y$right
Equation 2$2$$3$$8$
minus 2 × equation 1$-2$$-2$$-6$
New equation 2$0$$\boxed{1}$$2$

Pivot spot 2 holds $1$ (not zero) ✓. Two pivots, so exactly one answer: $y = 2$, then $x + 2 = 3$ gives $x = 1$.

Example B: zero pivot, fixed by a swap $$ 0x + 2y = 4, \qquad 3x + y = 5 $$

Pivot spot 1 holds $0$ ✗. Is there a nonzero number below it? Yes, the $3$. So swap the two equations:

$$ 3x + y = 5, \qquad 0x + 2y = 4 $$

Now pivot spot 1 holds $3$ ✓, and the number below it is already $0$. Pivot spot 2 holds $2$ ✓. Two pivots, so exactly one answer:

$2y = 4$ gives $y = 2$; then $3x + 2 = 5$ gives $3x = 3$, so $x = 1$.

Check: $0 + 2(2) = 4$ ✓, $\ 3(1) + 2 = 5$ ✓.

Example C: zero pivot, stuck for good $$ x + 2y = 3, \qquad 2x + 4y = 7 $$

Pivot spot 1 holds $1$ ✓. Equation 2 minus 2 × equation 1:

$x$$y$right
Equation 2$2$$4$$7$
minus 2 × equation 1$-2$$-4$$-6$
New equation 2$0$$\color{#b42318}{0}$$1$

Pivot spot 2 holds $0$ ✗, and there is no row below to swap with. Stuck. The new equation says $0x + 0y = 1$, that is, $0 = 1$: impossible. No solution.

Why? The left side of equation 2 ($2x + 4y$) is exactly 2 × the left side of equation 1 ($x + 2y$). So it isn't a new equation, and it contradicts equation 1 (which forces $2x + 4y = 6$, not $7$).

If the right side had been $6$ instead of $7$, the last line would say $0 = 0$: always true, so every point on the line $x + 2y = 3$ works. Infinitely many solutions.

Now the same two kinds of trouble on the lecture's three-equation example.

Case 1: a zero pivot fixed by a swap (change the 8 to a 6)

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 3 & \color{#b42318}{6} & 1 & 12 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 & 2 \end{array}\right] $$

Step 1. Pivot $1$, multiplier $3$. Row 2 minus 3 × row 1:

col 1col 2col 3right side
Row 2$3$$6$$1$$12$
minus 3 × row 1$-3$$-6$$-3$$-6$

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} \boxed{1} & 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & \color{#b42318}{\boxed{0}} & -2 & 6 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 & 2 \end{array}\right] $$

The problem. The second pivot spot (row 2, column 2) holds $0$. To clear the $4$ below it we would need $4 \div 0$, which is impossible.

The fix. Look below the bad spot: row 3 has a $4$ in that column. Swap rows 2 and 3:

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} \boxed{1} & 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & \boxed{4} & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & \boxed{-2} & 6 \end{array}\right] $$

Now $4$ is the second pivot, and the matrix is already a staircase: the number below the new pivot is already $0$. We got three pivots, $1$, $4$, $-2$, so elimination succeeded. Back substitution then finishes as usual.

The problem. The third pivot spot holds $0$. We look below it for a row to swap with, but row 3 is the last row. There is nothing below, so no swap can help. There is no third pivot.

What the last row says. Row 3 means $0x + 0y + 0z = -10$, that is,

$$ 0 = -10, $$

which is impossible. So this system has no solution.

Either way there is never exactly one solution. That is what singular means.

Why Case 2 is stuck: row 3 was a copy in disguise

In Case 2, row 3 of the matrix is made from rows 1 and 2:

$$ 2 \times (3, 8, 1) - 6 \times (1, 2, 1) = (6 - 6,\ 16 - 12,\ 2 - 6) = (0, 4, -4) = \text{row 3}. $$

So row 3 is not a new equation. Elimination "discovers" this by wiping it out completely. This is the same idea as Lecture 02: one row (or column) that is a mix of the others makes the matrix singular.

Summary of section 5
  • 0 in a pivot spot, nonzero below: swap rows. Temporary trouble, still solvable with one answer.
  • 0 in a pivot spot, nothing nonzero below: no pivot. The matrix is singular: no solution or infinitely many.

6. Each step is a matrix

Every elimination step ("row 2 minus 3 × row 1") can be written as multiplying by a matrix. That is how computers store and repeat the steps. Three facts make it work:

#FactIn one line
1Matrix on the left mixes rowsEach row of the answer = a mix of the rows, using the numbers in that row of the left matrix.
2The identity $I$ does nothingEach row says "take 1 of my own row, nothing else".
3Step matrix $E$Start with $I$ and put minus the multiplier in the spot being cleared.

6.1 Matrix on the left = mixing rows

How to read it

To get row $i$ of the answer, read row $i$ of the left matrix as a recipe: its numbers say how much of each row to take.

Example: if row 2 of the left matrix is $(-2,\ 1)$, then row 2 of the answer $= -2 \times$ (row 1) $+ 1 \times$ (row 2).

Example A: a 2 × 2 step, done with a matrix

The warm-up of section 2 did "equation 2 minus 2 × equation 1" on

$$ M = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{rows of } x + y \text{ and } 2x + 3y). $$

The matrix that does this step is

$$ E = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Read $E$ row by row:

Row of $E$RecipeWorkRow of $EM$
$(1,\ 0)$1 × row 1 + 0 × row 2$(1, 1) + (0, 0)$$(1, 1)$
$(-2,\ 1)$$-2$ × row 1 + 1 × row 2$(-2, -2) + (2, 3)$$(0, 1)$
$$ EM = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Row 1 is unchanged, and row 2 lost its $x$: exactly the warm-up step ✓.

Example B: a single row on the left

The row $[1\ \ 2\ \ 7]$ times the lecture's matrix $A$ means $1 \times$ row 1 $+ 2 \times$ row 2 $+ 7 \times$ row 3:

col 1col 2col 3
$1 \times (1, 2, 1)$$1$$2$$1$
$2 \times (3, 8, 1)$$6$$16$$2$
$7 \times (0, 4, 1)$$0$$28$$7$
Add$7$$46$$10$

So $[1\ \ 2\ \ 7]\,A = [7\ \ 46\ \ 10]$: a row goes in, a mix of rows comes out.

6.2 The identity matrix: do nothing

$$ I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \ (2 \times 2), \qquad I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \ (3 \times 3) $$
Example C: I times M
Row of $I$RecipeRow of $IM$
$(1,\ 0)$1 × row 1, nothing of row 2$(1, 1)$ = row 1 of $M$
$(0,\ 1)$nothing of row 1, 1 × row 2$(2, 3)$ = row 2 of $M$

$IM = M$. The identity is the "number 1" of matrices: multiplying by it changes nothing.

6.3 Building a step matrix

Recipe
  1. Start with the identity $I$.
  2. Find the spot you are clearing: (row being changed, column being cleared).
  3. Put minus the multiplier in that spot.

Why minus? Row $i$ of $E$ then reads "take $-$multiplier of the pivot row, plus 1 of my own row", which is exactly "my row minus multiplier × pivot row".

For the lecture's example:

StepIn wordsSpot clearedMultiplierMatrix
1row 2 minus 3 × row 1row 2, col 1$3$$E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ \color{#b42318}{-3} & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$
2row 3 minus 2 × row 2row 3, col 2$2$$E_{32} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \color{#b42318}{-2} & 1 \end{bmatrix}$

The small numbers name the spot: $E_{21}$ clears (row 2, column 1), and $E_{32}$ clears (row 3, column 2).

6.5 One matrix for all the steps

Instead of applying $E_{21}$ and then $E_{32}$, first multiply them together into one matrix $E = E_{32}E_{21}$. This is allowed because you may move the brackets:

$$ E_{32}\,(E_{21}\,A) = (E_{32}\,E_{21})\,A $$

just like $2 \times (3 \times 4) = (2 \times 3) \times 4$. This is called the associative law.

Work out $E = E_{32}E_{21}$ the same way: $E_{32}$ on the left mixes the rows of $E_{21}$:

Row of $E_{32}$Recipe (rows of $E_{21}$)WorkRow of $E$
$(1, 0, 0)$1 × row 1$(1, 0, 0)$$(1, 0, 0)$
$(0, 1, 0)$1 × row 2$(-3, 1, 0)$$(-3, 1, 0)$
$(0, -2, 1)$$-2$ × row 2 + 1 × row 3$(6, -2, 0) + (0, 0, 1)$$(6, -2, 1)$
$$ E = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 & 0 \\ 6 & -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check the hardest row: row 3 of $EA = 6 \times (1, 2, 1) - 2 \times (3, 8, 1) + 1 \times (0, 4, 1) = (6 - 6 + 0,\ 12 - 16 + 4,\ 6 - 2 + 1) = (0, 0, 5)$ ✓, the last row of $U$.

6.6 Order matters

Multiply the two step matrices the other way round, $E_{21}E_{32}$ ($E_{21}$ on the left now mixes the rows of $E_{32}$):

Row of $E_{21}$Recipe (rows of $E_{32}$)Row of $E_{21}E_{32}$
$(1, 0, 0)$1 × row 1$(1, 0, 0)$
$(-3, 1, 0)$$-3$ × row 1 + 1 × row 2 $= (-3, 0, 0) + (0, 1, 0)$$(-3, 1, 0)$
$(0, 0, 1)$1 × row 3$(0, -2, 1)$
$$ E_{32}E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 & 0 \\ \color{#b42318}{6} & -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\ne\qquad E_{21}E_{32} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 & 0 \\ \color{#b42318}{0} & -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
Watch out

The answers differ in one spot ($6$ vs $0$), so $AB$ and $BA$ are usually different. You may move the brackets, but never swap the order. In elimination the order is fixed: step 1 happens first, so its matrix sits closest to $A$.

7. Undoing a step

Step 1 subtracted 3 × row 1 from row 2. To undo it, add 3 × row 1 back:

$$ E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad E_{21}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ \color{#2e7d4f}{+3} & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
Example: undo on the right side

Step 1 turned $b = (2, 12, 2)$ into $(2, 6, 2)$, because $12 - 3 \times 2 = 6$. The undo adds it back: $6 + 3 \times 2 = 12$, giving $(2, 12, 2)$ again ✓.

Doing the step and then the undo changes nothing: $E_{21}^{-1}E_{21} = I$. This is what inverse means: the undo button from Lecture 02.

8. Swapping rows

Section 5 sometimes needed a row swap. That is a matrix too, called a permutation matrix $P$.

Recipe: swap the same rows of the identity $I$.

$$ P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad\text{(rows of } I \text{ swapped)} $$
Put $P$…Result for $\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$Effect
on the left: $PM$$\begin{bmatrix} c & d \\ a & b \end{bmatrix}$swaps the rows
on the right: $MP$$\begin{bmatrix} b & a \\ d & c \end{bmatrix}$swaps the columns
Left vs right

Matrix on the left → works on rows. Matrix on the right → works on columns. Elimination always works on rows, so its matrices go on the left.

This also shows $PM \ne MP$: order matters.

In finance

Whenever a quant model needs to solve equations (portfolio weights, hedge ratios, calibrating a curve to several prices), the computer uses exactly this: elimination to a staircase, then back substitution. In Python, numpy.linalg.solve does it for you. Example 8 in the Examples tab solves a small portfolio this way.

$3x - 3y - 2z + 4u$$3 - 6 - 6 + 16 = 7$$7$ ✓

Answer: $x = 1,\ y = 2,\ z = 3,\ u = 4$

What this example shows
  • Elimination works the same way for 4 equations as for 3: clear one column at a time.
  • A multiplier can be a fraction or negative ($-\tfrac12$ here). "Subtract a negative" = "add".
  • A zero in a pivot spot is fine if a lower row can be swapped up. Here it could, so there is still exactly one answer.

Example 4: A zero pivot fixed by a swap

Problem $$ 0x + 2y = 4, \qquad 3x + y = 5 $$

Problem. The first pivot position holds $0$. Below it is a $3$, so swap the two equations:

$$ 3x + y = 5, \qquad 2y = 4 $$

This is already a staircase. Back substitute: $2y = 4$ gives $y = 2$; then $3x + 2 = 5$ gives $3x = 3$, so $x = 1$.

Check. $0 + 4 = 4$ ✓, $3 + 2 = 5$ ✓.

Answer: $x = 1$, $y = 2$ (temporary trouble, fixed by a swap)

Example 6: Build a step matrix and its undo

Problem

For the matrix of Example 3, write the matrix $E_{21}$ that does "row 2 minus 2 × row 1", check it, and write its inverse.

Build. Start with $I$, put $-2$ in row 2, column 1:

$$ E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check row 2 of the result: $-2 \times (1, 1, 1) + 1 \times (2, 3, 1) = (-2, -2, -2) + (2, 3, 1) = (0, 1, -1)$ ✓ (rows 1 and 3 stay the same).

Undo: add 2 × row 1 back, so flip the sign:

$$ E_{21}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ +2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Undo check: $(0, 1, -1) + 2 \times (1, 1, 1) = (2, 3, 1)$, the original row 2 ✓

Example 7: Swap rows vs swap columns

Problem

With $P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and $M = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$, find $PM$ and $MP$.

$PM$ (left = rows). Row 1 of $P$ is $(0, 1)$: take row 2 of $M$. Row 2 of $P$ is $(1, 0)$: take row 1 of $M$.

$$ PM = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} $$

$MP$ (right = columns). Column 1 of $P$ is $(0, 1)$: take column 2 of $M$. Column 2 of $P$ is $(1, 0)$: take column 1 of $M$.

$$ MP = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 \end{bmatrix} $$

$PM$ swaps the rows, $MP$ swaps the columns, and $PM \ne MP$.

Example 8: Solving a small portfolio (finance)

Problem

Tomorrow the market is in one of three states: down, flat or up. Three funds are for sale today:

Story first

Like the weather tickets from Lecture 01: each fund is a ticket that pays depending on tomorrow. You want a certain payout in each case, so you need the right number of each ticket. With three tickets and three cases, that is three equations, and elimination solves them.

Step 1. One equation per state ($x$, $y$, $z$ = number of fund 1, 2, 3; use only what they pay tomorrow):

$$ \begin{aligned} x + y + z &= 6 &&\text{(down)} \\ 2x + 3y + z &= 11 &&\text{(flat)} \\ x + 3y + 4z &= 19 &&\text{(up)} \end{aligned} $$

1. Solve $x + y = 4$, $\ 2x + 5y = 11$ by elimination.

Equation 2 minus 2 × equation 1: $3y = 3$, so $y = 1$. Then $x + 1 = 4$, so $x = 3$. Check: $6 + 5 = 11$ ✓.

2. What is the multiplier to clear the 6 below a pivot of 2?

$6 \div 2 = 3$. Subtract 3 × the pivot row.

3. Back substitute: $x + y + z = 3$, $\ y + z = 2$, $\ 2z = 2$.

$z = 1$; $y + 1 = 2$ so $y = 1$; $x + 1 + 1 = 3$ so $x = 1$.

4. Write the 3 × 3 matrix that does "row 3 minus 4 × row 1", and its inverse.

$E_{31}$ = identity with $-4$ in row 3, column 1. Its inverse is the identity with $+4$ in row 3, column 1.

5. Does $x + 3y = 2$, $\ 2x + 6y = 4$ have a unique solution?

No. Equation 2 minus 2 × equation 1 gives $0 = 0$: no second pivot. The matrix is singular. Here every point on the line $x + 3y = 2$ works (infinitely many solutions).

6. Which 3 × 3 matrix swaps rows 1 and 3?

Swap rows 1 and 3 of the identity: $P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, used on the left.
