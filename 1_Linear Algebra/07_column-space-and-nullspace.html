1. The lecture in five questions

Words used here

Subspace = a collection of vectors (lists of numbers) that contains $0$ and stays inside when you add two members or multiply one by a number. Union = everything in either set. Intersection = only what is in both sets. Column space $C(A)$ = every output $Ax$ = all combinations of the columns. Nullspace $N(A)$ = all inputs $x$ with $Ax = 0$. Origin = the zero vector.

#QuestionShort answer
1Is the union of two subspaces a subspace?No (usually). The intersection is.
2What is the column space $C(A)$?All combinations of the columns: every output $Ax$ can produce.
3When does $Ax = b$ have a solution?Exactly when $b$ is in $C(A)$.
4What is the nullspace $N(A)$?All inputs $x$ with $Ax = 0$.
5Are the solutions of $Ax = b$ a subspace?Only if $b = 0$. Otherwise they miss the origin.

2. The two ideas in the simplest way

Words used here

Pack = a fixed bundle of fruit (in maths: one column). Buying $-1$ of a pack = returning one. Basket = the fruit you end up with (in maths: the output $b$). Column space = every basket you can end up with. Nullspace = every buy/return plan that leaves you with an empty basket. Copy (dependent) = a pack that is just other packs taped together.

Column space in one sentence

The column space is the list of everything you can end up with by buying (or returning) any amounts of the packs.

Nullspace in one sentence

The nullspace is the list of every way of buying/returning packs that leaves you with nothing.

Here: all $c \times (1, 1, -1)$, meaning buy $c$ A, buy $c$ B, return $c$ C.

Why does it exist? Only because pack C is a copy (A + B taped together). If every pack were truly different, the only way to end up with nothing would be to do nothing.

Why it is useful:

  • It spots copies. A nullspace trade tells you some pack is a copy of others.
  • It gives other ways to fill the same order (steps below).
  • It catches wrong prices (steps below).

3. Recap: union and intersection

Words used here

Subspace = a collection of vectors that contains $0$ and is closed under adding and scaling (adding two members, or multiplying one by a number, gives another member). Union $P \cup L$ = everything in $P$ or in $L$. Intersection $P \cap L$ = only what is in $P$ and in $L$. Plane / line through the origin = flat sheet / straight stick passing through $0$. $\mathbb{R}^3$ = all lists of 3 numbers.

A subspace is a set of vectors you cannot escape by taking combinations: any $c\,v + d\,w$ of its members is still inside. It always contains $0$.

Take two subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^3$: a plane $P$ and a line $L$, both through the origin, with $L$ not lying in $P$.

MeansSubspace?Why
Union $P \cup L$vectors in $P$ or in $L$Noadd one vector from $P$ and one from $L$: the sum is usually in neither
Intersection $P \cap L$vectors in $P$ and in $L$Yeshere it is just $\{0\}$, which is a subspace

With numbers: $P$ = the floor (all $(x, y, 0)$), $L$ = the z-axis (all $(0, 0, t)$).

  1. What we do Pick one vector from each: $(1, 0, 0)$ from the floor, $(0, 0, 1)$ from the z-axis.

    Why A subspace must keep the sum of any two members. If the union is a subspace, their sum must be in it.

    How Add entry by entry: $1 + 0 = 1$, $0 + 0 = 0$, $0 + 1 = 1$. Sum $= (1, 0, 1)$.

    What we have now Third entry is $1 \ne 0$: not on the floor. First entry is $1 \ne 0$: not on the z-axis. The sum escaped, so the union is not a subspace.

  2. What we do Find what is in both.

    How On the z-axis: $(0, 0, t)$. On the floor: third entry $= 0$. So $t = 0$.

    What we have now The intersection is only $(0, 0, 0)$, and $\{0\}$ is a subspace ($0 + 0 = 0$, $c \times 0 = 0$).

4. The column space C(A)

Words used here

Matrix $A$ = a table of numbers; here 4 rows and 3 columns ($4 \times 3$). Column = one vertical list of the table (one "pack"). Linear combination = scale each column by a number, then add. $Ax$ = $x_1 \times$ column 1 + $x_2 \times$ column 2 + $x_3 \times$ column 3. $\mathbb{R}^4$ = all lists of 4 numbers. Subspace = contains $0$, closed under adding and scaling. Dimension = number of independent directions.

The example for the whole lecture is a $4 \times 3$ matrix:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 1 & 4 \\ 4 & 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix} $$
Definition: Column space

The column space $C(A)$ is the set of all linear combinations of the columns of $A$. Equivalently, it is every output $Ax$ you can get from any input $x$.

  • Each column has 4 numbers, so $C(A)$ lives in $\mathbb{R}^4$. (Number of rows = where the column space lives.)
  • It is a subspace: a combination of combinations is still a combination.

Can 3 columns fill all of $\mathbb{R}^4$? No. Three vectors can make at most a 3-dimensional piece of a 4-dimensional space. Here it is even smaller (section 6).

5. When can Ax = b be solved?

Words used here

$Ax = b$ = "find amounts $x_1, x_2, x_3$ of the three columns that add up to the target $b$". Unknowns = $x_1, x_2, x_3$ (3 of them). Equations = one per row (4 of them). Solution = an $x$ that makes all 4 equations true. Solvable = at least one solution exists. Column space $C(A)$ = every $b$ you can reach. Step = the difference between one entry of $b$ and the next.

$Ax = b$ is 4 equations, 3 unknowns:

$$ x_1 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} + x_2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + x_3 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ b_3 \\ b_4 \end{bmatrix} $$

Usually there is no solution. But for some special $b$ there is. The easy way to find good $b$'s: pick an $x$ first and see what $b$ comes out.

    Why To see that "pick $x$, compute $Ax$" really gives a reachable $b$.

    How $2 \times$ column 1 $= (2, 4, 6, 8)$. $-1 \times$ column 2 $= (-1, -1, -1, -1)$. $0 \times$ column 3 $= (0, 0, 0, 0)$.

  2. What we do Add them row by row.

    How Row 1: $2 - 1 + 0 = 1$. Row 2: $4 - 1 + 0 = 3$. Row 3: $6 - 1 + 0 = 5$. Row 4: $8 - 1 + 0 = 7$.

    What we have now $b = (1, 3, 5, 7)$, and $x = (2, -1, 0)$ solves $Ax = b$.

The key fact $$ Ax = b \text{ has a solution} \iff b \text{ is in the column space } C(A). $$

If $b$ is a combination of the columns, the numbers in that combination are the solution $x$. If it is not, no $x$ can work.

What does C(A) look like here? "Equal steps"

  1. What we do Drop column 3 and write a general $b$ from columns 1 and 2 only.

    Why Column 3 adds nothing new (section 6), so every $b$ in $C(A)$ is $x_1(1, 2, 3, 4) + x_2(1, 1, 1, 1)$.

    How Row by row: $1 \cdot x_1 + x_2$, then $2x_1 + x_2$, then $3x_1 + x_2$, then $4x_1 + x_2$.

  2. What we do Subtract each entry from the next one.

    How $(2x_1 + x_2) - (x_1 + x_2) = x_1$. $(3x_1 + x_2) - (2x_1 + x_2) = x_1$. $(4x_1 + x_2) - (3x_1 + x_2) = x_1$.

    What we have now Every step is the same number $x_1$. So every $b$ in the column space goes up (or down) in equal steps.

6. A column that adds nothing

Words used here

Column = one vertical list of $A$. Combination = scale and add. Column space $C(A)$ = all combinations of the columns. Dependent (redundant) column = a column that is already a combination of others, so it adds no new direction. Pivot column = a column that adds a new direction (we keep it). Dimension = how many independent directions. Plane = a flat 2-direction piece.

Look at column 3:

$$ \underbrace{(2, 3, 4, 5)}_{\text{col } 3} = \underbrace{(1, 2, 3, 4)}_{\text{col } 1} + \underbrace{(1, 1, 1, 1)}_{\text{col } 2} $$

  1. What we do Check col 1 + col 2 = col 3, one entry at a time.

    Why If it is true, column 3 is a copy and adds nothing.

    How Row 1: $1 + 1 = 2$ ✓. Row 2: $2 + 1 = 3$ ✓. Row 3: $3 + 1 = 4$ ✓. Row 4: $4 + 1 = 5$ ✓.

    What we have now All four match: column 3 $=$ column 1 $+$ column 2.

  2. What we do Check that column 2 is not a multiple of column 1.

    How To turn $(1, 2, 3, 4)$ into $(1, 1, 1, 1)$, row 1 needs "times 1" but row 2 needs "times $\tfrac12$". Different amounts, so no single multiple works.

    What we have now Column 2 adds a new direction. Columns 1 and 2 are pivot columns; column 3 is not.

Column 3 is already a combination of columns 1 and 2. Throwing it away does not change the column space: anything you could build with it, you can build without it.

ColumnAdds a new direction?Name
1: $(1, 2, 3, 4)$yes (first one)pivot column
2: $(1, 1, 1, 1)$yes (not a multiple of column 1)pivot column
3: $(2, 3, 4, 5)$no (= col 1 + col 2)not a pivot column
Result

$C(A)$ is a 2-dimensional subspace (a "plane") inside $\mathbb{R}^4$, made by columns 1 and 2.

(We could have thrown away column 1 instead, since column 1 = column 3 − column 2. By convention we keep the earlier columns and drop the later dependent ones.)

7. The nullspace N(A)

Words used here

Nullspace $N(A)$ = all inputs $x$ with $Ax = 0$ ("null" = zero). Input $x$ = how much of each column we take. $Ax = 0$ = the columns, scaled by $x$, cancel out completely. $\mathbb{R}^m$ / $\mathbb{R}^n$ = lists of $m$ / $n$ numbers ($m$ = rows, $n$ = columns). Line through the origin = all multiples $c \times$ (one vector).

Definition: Nullspace

The nullspace $N(A)$ is the set of all inputs $x$ that give output zero:

$$ N(A) = \{\ x \ :\ Ax = 0\ \} $$

"Null" means zero. These are the combinations of the columns that cancel out completely.

8. Why N(A) is a subspace

Words used here

Subspace = contains $0$, and closed under adding and scaling. Closed under adding = if $v$ and $w$ are in, so is $v + w$. Closed under scaling = if $v$ is in, so is $c\,v$ for any number $c$. Distributive law = $A(v + w) = Av + Aw$. Nullspace = all $x$ with $Ax = 0$.

Two checks. Suppose $Av = 0$ and $Aw = 0$.

TestWorkResult
Adding$A(v + w) = Av + Aw = 0 + 0$$0$, so $v + w$ is in $N(A)$ ✓
Multiplying$A(12v) = 12\,Av = 12 \times 0$$0$, so $12v$ is in $N(A)$ ✓

  1. What we do Take two nullspace members: $v = (1, 1, -1)$ and $w = (2, 2, -2)$.

    Why Test "closed under adding" with real numbers.

    How Add entry by entry: $1 + 2 = 3$, $1 + 2 = 3$, $-1 + (-2) = -3$.

    What we have now $v + w = (3, 3, -3)$.

  2. Check Multiply each row of the original $A$ by $(3, 3, -3)$. Row 1: $3 + 3 - 6 = 0$ ✓. Row 2: $6 + 3 - 9 = 0$ ✓. Row 3: $9 + 3 - 12 = 0$ ✓. Row 4: $12 + 3 - 15 = 0$ ✓.

    What we have now The sum is still in $N(A)$.

  3. What we do Test scaling: $12v = (12, 12, -12)$.

    How Row 1: $12 + 12 - 24 = 0$. Row 2: $24 + 12 - 36 = 0$. Row 3: $36 + 12 - 48 = 0$. Row 4: $48 + 12 - 60 = 0$.

    What we have now Still in $N(A)$ ✓.

This uses the distributive law $A(v + w) = Av + Aw$ and the fact that a number can move outside: $A(cv) = c(Av)$.

9. Solutions of Ax = b are not a subspace

Words used here

Solution of $Ax = b$ = an $x$ that makes all rows equal the target $b$. Nullspace = all $x$ with $Ax = 0$. Subspace = contains $0$, closed under adding and scaling. Shifted = moved away from the origin without turning. Origin = the zero vector.

In finance

7. How this is used with real options

  • Can we quote it? A client's payoff is quotable from listed options only if it is in the column space; otherwise the desk carries leftover risk.
  • Parity and arbitrage. Zero-payoff trades (like CE − PE − future) are exactly the price relations that must hold. Arbitrage desks scan the option chain for zero trades that don't cost zero.
  • Cheapest recipe. When the nullspace is non-zero, many recipes pay the same; traders pick the cheapest (or the most liquid).
  • Redundant instruments. A free column means an instrument adds nothing new for hedging these scenarios.

Example 9: A zero-payoff portfolio and arbitrage (finance)

Problem

Same three products. Today the future-note trades at price $2$, the bond at $1$, and note C at $3.2$. Find a portfolio that pays nothing in every scenario, and say whether the prices allow free money.

Words used here

Portfolio = a list of how many of each product you hold (negative = sold). Zero-payoff portfolio = pays 0 at every Nifty level = a vector in the nullspace ($Ax = 0$). Cost today = price × quantity, added up (buying costs, selling pays you). Arbitrage = a trade that pays you today and can never cost you later. Fair price = the price that makes the zero-payoff portfolio cost 0.

Story first

If a mix of products pays zero in every outcome, it should cost zero today. That mix is a vector in the nullspace.

  1. What we do Pick the nullspace vector $x = (1, 1, -1)$: buy 1 future-note, buy 1 bond, sell 1 note C.

    Why Note C = future-note + bond, so selling C cancels buying the other two.

  2. Check Payoff at each level, one scenario at a time.
