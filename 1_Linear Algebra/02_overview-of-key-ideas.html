2. Mixing vectors

A vector is a list of numbers. The lecture uses these three:

$$ u = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad v = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad w = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
Definition: Linear combination (a mix)

Multiply each vector by a number, then add them up. The numbers are called scalars.

Example: mix 2 of u, 3 of v and 1 of w

Work position by position, top to bottom:

Position$2u$$3v$$1w$Add
top$2 \times 1 = 2$$3 \times 0 = 0$$1 \times 0 = 0$$2$
middle$2 \times (-1) = -2$$3 \times 1 = 3$$1 \times 0 = 0$$1$
bottom$2 \times 0 = 0$$3 \times (-1) = -3$$1 \times 1 = 1$$-2$

So $2u + 3v + 1w = (2, 1, -2)$.

What do all the mixes fill?

Imagine trying every possible number:

Mix of…FillsIn plain words
$u$ onlya lineOne arrow, stretched forwards and backwards.
$u$ and $v$a plane (flat sheet)Two arrows in different directions sweep out a flat sheet.
$u$, $v$ and $w$all of 3D space$w$ points off the sheet, so now you can reach every point.

3. A matrix is a machine

Put the three vectors side by side as the columns of a matrix:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \text{(column 1 is } u\text{, column 2 is } v\text{, column 3 is } w\text{)} $$

Feeding a vector $x = (x_1, x_2, x_3)$ into $A$ means mixing the columns with those amounts:

$$ Ax = x_1 u + x_2 v + x_3 w $$
Example: feed in x = (1, 4, 9)
Position$1u$$4v$$9w$Add
top$1$$0$$0$$1$
middle$-1$$4$$0$$3$
bottom$0$$-4$$9$$5$

In: $(1, 4, 9)$.   Out: $(1, 3, 5)$.

What is this machine doing?

Look closely at the output:

OutputWhere it comes from
$1$the first input, $1$
$3$second minus first: $4 - 1$
$5$third minus second: $9 - 4$

In general, $A$ turns $(x_1, x_2, x_3)$ into $(x_1,\ x_2 - x_1,\ x_3 - x_2)$. It takes differences, so it is called the difference matrix.

Story: a savings jar

You check your savings jar every evening. The totals are $1$, $4$, $9$. How much did you add each day?

DayTotal in jarAdded that day
1$1$$1 - 0 = 1$
2$4$$4 - 1 = 3$
3$9$$9 - 4 = 5$

That is exactly what $A$ does: totals in, daily amounts out.

4. Running the machine backwards

Now reverse the question: you know the daily amounts $(1, 3, 5)$. What were the totals? Just keep adding:

DayAdded that dayRunning total
1$1$$1$
2$3$$1 + 3 = 4$
3$5$$1 + 3 + 5 = 9$

We are back at $(1, 4, 9)$.

The same thing with letters

If the output is $b = (b_1, b_2, b_3)$, the three equations are:

$$ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= b_1 \\ x_2 - x_1 &= b_2 \\ x_3 - x_2 &= b_3 \end{aligned} $$

Solve from the top, one line at a time:

$$ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= b_1 &&\text{read it off the first line} \\ x_2 &= b_2 + x_1 = b_1 + b_2 &&\text{add } x_1 \text{ to both sides of line 2} \\ x_3 &= b_3 + x_2 = b_1 + b_2 + b_3 &&\text{add } x_2 \text{ to both sides of line 3} \end{aligned} $$

Each answer is a running total. The "adding up" machine is the matrix

$$ S = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad S \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ b_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_1 + b_2 \\ b_1 + b_2 + b_3 \end{bmatrix} $$
Definition: Inverse (the undo button)

The inverse of $A$, written $A^{-1}$, is the machine that undoes $A$. If $A$ turns $x$ into $b$, then $A^{-1}$ turns $b$ back into $x$.

Here $A^{-1} = S$: differences are undone by running totals.

Why A can be undone

At every step there was exactly one possible answer. Different inputs always give different outputs, so from any output you can tell exactly which input it came from.

In calculus, the same pair appears as the derivative (differences) and the integral (running totals), and one undoes the other.

5. A machine that cannot be undone

Change only the third column, from $w = (0, 0, 1)$ to $w^* = (-1, 0, 1)$:

$$ C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad C \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 - x_3 \\ x_2 - x_1 \\ x_3 - x_2 \end{bmatrix} $$

Every output is now a difference, going round in a circle: the first output compares $x_1$ with $x_3$.

Story: three friends at a round table

Friends 1, 2 and 3 sit in a circle with $x_1$, $x_2$, $x_3$ coins. Each friend only says how much more they have than the person before them (friend 1's "before" is friend 3, because it's a circle).

Say they have $5$, $7$ and $6$:

FriendCompares withSays
1 (has 5)friend 3 (has 6)$5 - 6 = -1$
2 (has 7)friend 1 (has 5)$7 - 5 = 2$
3 (has 6)friend 2 (has 7)$6 - 7 = -1$

That is $C(5, 7, 6) = (-1, 2, -1)$.

Problem 1: you cannot work out how much they have

Give everyone $10$ more, so they have $15, 17, 16$:

$$ C \begin{bmatrix} 15 \\ 17 \\ 16 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 15 - 16 \\ 17 - 15 \\ 16 - 17 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Exactly the same answers. Hearing $(-1, 2, -1)$, you cannot tell whether they have $(5, 7, 6)$ or $(15, 17, 16)$ or anything else shifted up or down. The same happens with everyone equal:

$$ C \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = C \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

Two different inputs give the same output, so no undo button can exist. $C$ has no inverse.

Problem 2: some outputs are impossible

Add the three answers: $-1 + 2 + (-1) = 0$. That is not luck. Add the three outputs in general:

$$ (x_1 - x_3) + (x_2 - x_1) + (x_3 - x_2) = 0 $$

Every amount appears once with $+$ and once with $-$, so everything cancels. The three answers always add up to $0$.

So if someone claims the friends said $(1, 1, 1)$, that is impossible, because $1 + 1 + 1 = 3$, not $0$. The equation $Cx = (1, 1, 1)$ has no solution.

Rule for the C machine

$Cx = b$ can be solved only if $b_1 + b_2 + b_3 = 0$. When it can, there are many answers (you can always add the same amount to everyone).

Why A worked and C does not

With $A$, friend 1 says their actual amount ($x_1$), not a difference. That one real number pins everything down. With $C$, everyone only gives differences, so the actual amounts are lost.

6. Why: one column is a mix of the others

The real cause is in the columns. Is $w^*$ a mix of $u$ and $v$? Try $-1$ of $u$ and $-1$ of $v$:

Position$-1 \times u$$-1 \times v$Add$w^*$
top$-1$$0$$-1$$-1$ ✓
middle$1$$-1$$0$$0$ ✓
bottom$0$$1$$1$$1$ ✓

So $w^* = -u - v$. The third column of $C$ is made from the first two, so it adds no new direction. All three columns lie on the same flat sheet, and mixing them can never leave that sheet. That is why most outputs are unreachable.

The original $w = (0, 0, 1)$ is not a mix of $u$ and $v$ (see Example 5 in the Examples tab). It points off the sheet, which is why $A$ reaches everything.

Definition: Independent and dependent

Vectors are independent if none of them is a mix of the others. Each one brings a new direction. ($u, v, w$)

Vectors are dependent if one of them is a mix of the others. ($u, v, w^*$, because $w^* = -u - v$)

Definition: Basis

A basis of 3D space is 3 independent vectors. Mixing them reaches every point. $u, v, w$ is a basis; $u, v, w^*$ is not.

The big rule

For a square matrix, these always go together:

Columns independent⇔Mixes reach every point⇔Matrix has an undo button (inverse)

$A$ has all three. $C$ has none.

7. Subspaces

Definition: Subspace

A subspace is a collection of vectors that you cannot escape by mixing. Multiply any of them by any number, or add any two, and you are still inside. It must contain the zero vector.

In plain words: a subspace is a flat line, sheet or space through the origin.

Subspaces of 3D spaceExample
just the origin $(0, 0, 0)$the point $0$ alone
a line through the originall mixes of $u$
a plane through the originall mixes of $u$ and $v$
all of 3D spaceall mixes of $u$, $v$, $w$
Not subspaces
  • A line or plane that misses the origin. $0 \times$ any vector $= 0$ would be outside.
  • A piece of a plane, like a triangle. Doubling a vector jumps outside it.
In finance
  • $A$ turns an account balance each day into daily profit or loss.
  • $S$ turns daily profit or loss into the running total.
  • Like $C$: daily price changes alone never tell you the price level. Prices $(100, 103, 101)$ and $(50, 53, 51)$ have the same changes. You need one real starting price.

Worked examples

Throughout, $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ (differences), $S = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ (running sums) and $C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ (cyclic differences).

Example 1: Compute $Ax$ both ways

Problem

Find $A x$ for $x = (2, 5, 7)$.

By columns.

$$ 2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + 5 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} + 7 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 + 0 + 0 \\ -2 + 5 + 0 \\ 0 - 5 + 7 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} $$

By differences (what $A$ does): $(x_1,\ x_2 - x_1,\ x_3 - x_2) = (2,\ 5 - 2,\ 7 - 5) = (2, 3, 2)$ ✓.

Answer: $Ax = (2, 3, 2)$

Example 2: Solve $Ax = b$ top-down

Problem

Solve $Ax = b$ for $b = (3, 1, 2)$.

The equations. $x_1 = 3$,   $x_2 - x_1 = 1$,   $x_3 - x_2 = 2$.

$$ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= 3 \\ x_2 &= 1 + x_1 = 1 + 3 = 4 &&\text{add } x_1 \text{ to both sides} \\ x_3 &= 2 + x_2 = 2 + 4 = 6 &&\text{add } x_2 \text{ to both sides} \end{aligned} $$

Check. $A(3, 4, 6) = (3,\ 4 - 3,\ 6 - 4) = (3, 1, 2)$ ✓.

Answer: $x = (3, 4, 6)$, the running sums of $(3, 1, 2)$.

Example 3: Use the inverse directly

Problem

Use $S = A^{-1}$ to solve $Ax = (2, 2, 2)$ in one step.

Multiply by $S$ (running sums):

$$ x = S \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 + 2 \\ 2 + 2 + 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check. $A(2, 4, 6) = (2,\ 4 - 2,\ 6 - 4) = (2, 2, 2)$ ✓.

Answer: $x = (2, 4, 6)$

Example 4: When can $Cx = b$ be solved?

Problem

Solve $Cx = b$ for (a) $b = (1, 2, -3)$ and (b) $b = (1, 1, 1)$, or show it is impossible.

The test. $Cx = b$ has a solution only if $b_1 + b_2 + b_3 = 0$.

$b$$b_1 + b_2 + b_3$Solvable?
(a) $(1, 2, -3)$$1 + 2 - 3 = 0$yes
(b) $(1, 1, 1)$$1 + 1 + 1 = 3$no

(a) Find the solutions

The equations are $x_1 - x_3 = 1$, $\ x_2 - x_1 = 2$, $\ x_3 - x_2 = -3$. Remember the friends at the round table: giving everyone the same extra amount changes none of the differences. So we are free to pick any starting value for friend 1. Choose the easiest, $x_1 = 0$:

$$ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= 0 &&\text{our choice} \\ x_2 &= 2 + x_1 = 2 &&\text{from } x_2 - x_1 = 2 \\ x_3 &= -3 + x_2 = -1 &&\text{from } x_3 - x_2 = -3 \\ x_1 - x_3 &= 0 - (-1) = 1 \;✓ &&\text{first equation checks} \end{aligned} $$

So $x = (0, 2, -1)$ works, and so does $(0, 2, -1) + c(1, 1, 1)$ for any $c$. For example $c = 1$ gives $(1, 3, 0)$: $1 - 0 = 1$, $3 - 1 = 2$, $0 - 3 = -3$ ✓.

(a) Infinitely many solutions: $x = (0, 2, -1) + c(1, 1, 1)$.

(b) Show it is impossible

Add the three equations: the left side is always $(x_1 - x_3) + (x_2 - x_1) + (x_3 - x_2) = 0$, but the right side is $1 + 1 + 1 = 3$. That would need $0 = 3$.

(b) No solution.

Example 5: Independent or dependent?

Problem

With $u = (1, -1, 0)$ and $v = (0, 1, -1)$: is (a) $w = (0, 0, 1)$ and (b) $w^* = (-1, 0, 1)$ a combination of $u$ and $v$?

General combination. $x_1 u + x_2 v = (x_1,\ -x_1 + x_2,\ -x_2)$. Match it with the target, position by position.

(a) Target $w = (0, 0, 1)$

$$ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= 0 &&\text{top} \\ -x_1 + x_2 &= 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x_2 = 0 &&\text{middle} \\ -x_2 &= 1 \;\Rightarrow\; 0 = 1 \;✗ &&\text{bottom fails} \end{aligned} $$

(a) No, so $u, v, w$ are independent (a basis for $\mathbb{R}^3$).

(b) Target $w^* = (-1, 0, 1)$

$$ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= -1 &&\text{top} \\ -x_1 + x_2 &= 0 \;\Rightarrow\; 1 + x_2 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x_2 = -1 &&\text{middle} \\ -x_2 &= 1 \;✓ &&\text{bottom checks} \end{aligned} $$

(b) Yes: $w^* = -u - v$, so $u, v, w^*$ are dependent.

Shortcut. $u$ and $v$ both have components that add to $0$, so every combination does too. $w$ adds to $1$ (not in the plane); $w^*$ adds to $0$ (in the plane).

Example 6: Subspace or not?

Problem

Which of these are subspaces?

  1. The line $y = 2x$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
  2. The line $y = 2x + 1$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
  3. The plane $b_1 + b_2 + b_3 = 0$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
  4. All vectors $(x, y)$ with $x \ge 0$ and $y \ge 0$.

Two tests. (1) Does it contain the origin? (2) Do multiplying by any number and adding keep you inside?

Contains $0$?Stays inside?Subspace?
(a) $y = 2x$yes: $0 = 2 \cdot 0$yes: multiples and sums of points with $y = 2x$ still have $y = 2x$yes (a line through $0$)
(b) $y = 2x + 1$no: $0 \ne 2 \cdot 0 + 1$—no
(c) $b_1 + b_2 + b_3 = 0$yes: $0 + 0 + 0 = 0$yes: scaling or adding keeps the sum $0$yes (a plane through $0$)
(d) $x \ge 0,\ y \ge 0$yesno: $(1, 1)$ is inside, but $-1 \times (1, 1) = (-1, -1)$ is notno

Answer: (a) and (c) are subspaces; (b) and (d) are not.

Example 7: Daily P&L and running totals (finance)

Problem

(a) A trader makes $+2$, $-1$, $+3$ on three days. What is the running total after each day?

(b) Another account starts at $0$ and ends the three days at $5$, $8$, $6$. What was each day's profit or loss?

Story first

Think of a piggy bank. Each day you put money in (or take some out). The daily amounts are the changes; the amount in the bank each evening is the running total. Running totals are $S$; daily changes are $A$.

(a) Running total = $S \times$ daily P&L.

$$ S \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 - 1 \\ 2 - 1 + 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} $$

(a) Running totals: $2, 1, 4$.

(b) Daily P&L = $A \times$ end-of-day balances.

$$ A \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 8 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 8 - 5 \\ 6 - 8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 3 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} $$

(b) Daily P&L: $+5, +3, -2$.

Check (b) with $S$: $S(5, 3, -2) = (5,\ 8,\ 6)$ ✓. $S$ undoes $A$.

Example 8: Building a put from a bond, a stock and a call (options)

Problem

Tomorrow the stock ends at 1 (low), 2 (middle) or 3 (high). Three assets are for sale today:

AssetPrice todayPays if low ($S=1$)Pays if middle ($S=2$)Pays if high ($S=3$)
Bond$1$$1$$1$$1$
Stock$2$$1$$2$$3$
Call, strike 2$0.25$$0$$0$$1$

A put option with strike 2 gives the right to sell the stock for $2$. What should it cost today?

Story first

Same idea as the tickets in Lecture 01, now with three kinds of weather instead of two. Each asset is a ticket that pays depending on tomorrow's "weather" (low, middle or high). We want a new ticket (the put), so we build it from the three tickets we can already buy, then add up their prices.

Step 1. What the put pays. You sell for $2$ only if the stock is worth less than $2$: payoff $= \max(2 - S,\ 0)$.

StateStock $S$$2 - S$Put pays
Low$1$$1$$1$
Middle$2$$0$$0$
High$3$$-1$$0$ (don't use it)

Step 2. Column picture. Find $x$ bonds, $y$ stocks and $z$ calls with

$$ x \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{bond}} + y \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{stock}} + z \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{call}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{put}} \begin{matrix} \leftarrow \text{low} \\ \leftarrow \text{middle} \\ \leftarrow \text{high} \end{matrix} $$

Step 3. One equation per state.

$$ \begin{aligned} x + y &= 1 &&\text{(low)} \\ x + 2y &= 0 &&\text{(middle)} \\ x + 3y + z &= 0 &&\text{(high)} \end{aligned} $$

Step 4. Solve.

$$ \begin{aligned} (x + 2y) - (x + y) &= 0 - 1 &&\text{(middle)} - \text{(low)} \\ y &= -1 \\ x &= 1 - y = 1 - (-1) = 2 &&\text{from (low)} \\ z &= -x - 3y = -2 + 3 = 1 &&\text{from (high)} \end{aligned} $$

Step 5. Check.

State2 bonds$-1$ stock1 callTotalPut pays
Low$2$$-1$$0$$1$$1$ ✓
Middle$2$$-2$$0$$0$$0$ ✓
High$2$$-3$$1$$0$$0$ ✓

Recipe: buy 2 bonds, sell 1 stock ($y = -1$), buy 1 call.

Step 6. Price the put.

$$ \underbrace{2 \times 1}_{\text{bonds}} + \underbrace{(-1) \times 2}_{\text{stock}} + \underbrace{1 \times 0.25}_{\text{call}} = 2 - 2 + 0.25 = 0.25 $$

The put is worth $0.25$ today.

The link to this lecture

The payoffs of bond, stock and call are three independent vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$, so they form a basis: any payoff over the three states can be built from them, and the payoff matrix is invertible. If the third asset were just "2 stocks" with payoff $(2, 4, 6)$, the columns would be dependent and the put could not be built.

The recipe "put $=$ 2 bonds $-$ stock $+$ call" is called put–call parity.

Practice problems

Answers are hidden. Click a problem to check your work.

1. Compute $A(1, 1, 1)$ and $C(1, 1, 1)$. What does each result tell you?

$A(1, 1, 1) = (1,\ 0,\ 0)$, which is not zero, as it must be for an invertible matrix. $C(1, 1, 1) = (0, 0, 0)$: a nonzero input gives output $0$, so $C$ is not invertible.

2. Solve $Ax = (1, 1, 1)$.

Running sums: $x = (1,\ 1 + 1,\ 1 + 1 + 1) = (1, 2, 3)$. Check: $A(1, 2, 3) = (1,\ 2 - 1,\ 3 - 2) = (1, 1, 1)$ ✓.

3. Is $Cx = (2, -5, 3)$ solvable? Is $Cx = (1, 0, 0)$?

$2 - 5 + 3 = 0$, so the first is solvable. $1 + 0 + 0 = 1 \ne 0$, so the second is not.

4. Are $(1, 2, 3)$, $(2, 4, 6)$, $(0, 1, 0)$ independent?

No. $(2, 4, 6) = 2 \times (1, 2, 3)$, so the second is a combination of the first. They are dependent and fill only a plane.

5. Is the set of all vectors $(x, y, z)$ with $z = 1$ a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$?

No. It does not contain the origin $(0, 0, 0)$, since $0 \ne 1$. (It is a flat plane, but it misses the origin.)

6. Using $S$, find the running totals of daily P&L $(4, -2, -1)$.

$S(4, -2, -1) = (4,\ 4 - 2,\ 4 - 2 - 1) = (4, 2, 1)$.
