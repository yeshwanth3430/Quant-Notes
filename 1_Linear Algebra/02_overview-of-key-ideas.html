1. The lecture in four questions

Words used here

Vector = a list of numbers. Mix (linear combination) = multiply each vector by a number, then add. Matrix = a table of numbers; it acts like a machine: a vector goes in, a vector comes out. Inverse = the machine that undoes it (the undo button). Column = a stack of numbers going down inside a matrix.

#QuestionShort answer
1What can we do with vectors?Mix them: multiply by numbers and add.
2What does a matrix do?It is a machine: put numbers in, get numbers out.
3Can we run the machine backwards?For matrix $A$, yes. The "undo" machine is called the inverse.
4When can we not run it backwards?For matrix $C$. One of its columns is just a mix of the others.

2. Mixing vectors

Words used here

Vector = a list of numbers, top to bottom. Position = one slot in that list (top, middle, bottom). Number (scalar) = an ordinary number that stretches a vector. Multiply a vector by a number = multiply every position by it. Add vectors = add matching positions. Mix (linear combination) = do both. Line = all stretches of one arrow. Plane = a flat sheet. 3D space = every list of 3 numbers.

A vector is a list of numbers. The lecture uses these three:

$$ u = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad v = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad w = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
Definition: Linear combination (a mix)

Multiply each vector by a number, then add them up. The numbers are called scalars.

Example: mix 2 of u, 3 of v and 1 of w

  1. What we do Make 2 copies of $u = (1, -1, 0)$.

    Why The recipe says "2 of $u$". Multiplying a vector by 2 means multiplying every position by 2.

    How Top: $2 \times 1 = 2$. Middle: $2 \times (-1) = -2$. Bottom: $2 \times 0 = 0$.

    What we have now $2u = (2, -2, 0)$.

  2. What we do Make 3 copies of $v = (0, 1, -1)$.

    Why The recipe says "3 of $v$".

    How Top: $3 \times 0 = 0$. Middle: $3 \times 1 = 3$. Bottom: $3 \times (-1) = -3$.

    What we have now $3v = (0, 3, -3)$.

  3. What we do Make 1 copy of $w = (0, 0, 1)$.

    Why The recipe says "1 of $w$". One copy is just $w$ itself.

    How Top: $1 \times 0 = 0$. Middle: $1 \times 0 = 0$. Bottom: $1 \times 1 = 1$.

    What we have now $1w = (0, 0, 1)$.

  4. What we do Add the three pieces, one position at a time.

    Why Adding vectors means adding matching positions: tops with tops, middles with middles, bottoms with bottoms.

    How Top: $2 + 0 = 2$, then $2 + 0 = 2$. Middle: $-2 + 3 = 1$, then $1 + 0 = 1$. Bottom: $0 + (-3) = -3$, then $-3 + 1 = -2$.

    Position$2u$$3v$$1w$Add
    top$2 \times 1 = 2$$3 \times 0 = 0$$1 \times 0 = 0$$2$
    middle$2 \times (-1) = -2$$3 \times 1 = 3$$1 \times 0 = 0$$1$
    bottom$2 \times 0 = 0$$3 \times (-1) = -3$$1 \times 1 = 1$$-2$

    What we have now $2u + 3v + 1w = (2, 1, -2)$.

  5. Check Read across each row of the three vectors placed side by side, multiply by the amounts $(2, 3, 1)$ and add. Top row $(1, 0, 0)$: $1 \times 2 + 0 \times 3 + 0 \times 1 = 2$ ✓. Middle row $(-1, 1, 0)$: $-1 \times 2 + 1 \times 3 + 0 = -2 + 3 = 1$ ✓. Bottom row $(0, -1, 1)$: $0 + (-1) \times 3 + 1 \times 1 = -3 + 1 = -2$ ✓.

What do all the mixes fill?

Imagine trying every possible number:

3. A matrix is a machine

Words used here

Matrix = a table of numbers. Column = a stack going down; row = a line going across. Matrix times vector ($Ax$) = mix the columns of $A$ using the numbers in $x$ as the amounts. Input = what goes in ($x$); output = what comes out ($Ax$). Difference = "this one minus the one before". Difference matrix = the machine that outputs differences.

Put the three vectors side by side as the columns of a matrix:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \text{(column 1 is } u\text{, column 2 is } v\text{, column 3 is } w\text{)} $$

Feeding a vector $x = (x_1, x_2, x_3)$ into $A$ means mixing the columns with those amounts:

$$ Ax = x_1 u + x_2 v + x_3 w $$
Example: feed in x = (1, 4, 9)

  1. What we do Take 1 copy of column 1, $u = (1, -1, 0)$.

    Why The first number of $x$ is $1$, so it says how much of column 1 to use.

    How $1 \times 1 = 1$, $1 \times (-1) = -1$, $1 \times 0 = 0$.

    What we have now $1u = (1, -1, 0)$.

  2. What we do Take 4 copies of column 2, $v = (0, 1, -1)$.

    Why The second number of $x$ is $4$.

    How $4 \times 0 = 0$, $4 \times 1 = 4$, $4 \times (-1) = -4$.

    What we have now $4v = (0, 4, -4)$.

  3. What we do Take 9 copies of column 3, $w = (0, 0, 1)$.

    Why The third number of $x$ is $9$.

    How $9 \times 0 = 0$, $9 \times 0 = 0$, $9 \times 1 = 9$.

    What we have now $9w = (0, 0, 9)$.

  4. What we do Add the three pieces, position by position.

    How Top: $1 + 0 + 0 = 1$. Middle: $-1 + 4 = 3$, then $3 + 0 = 3$. Bottom: $0 + (-4) = -4$, then $-4 + 9 = 5$.

    Position$1u$$4v$$9w$Add
    top$1$$0$$0$$1$
    middle$-1$$4$$0$$3$
    bottom$0$$-4$$9$$5$

    What we have now In: $(1, 4, 9)$.   Out: $(1, 3, 5)$.

  5. Check, row by row of A Row 1 $(1, 0, 0)$: $1 \times 1 + 0 \times 4 + 0 \times 9 = 1$ ✓. Row 2 $(-1, 1, 0)$: $-1 \times 1 + 1 \times 4 + 0 = -1 + 4 = 3$ ✓. Row 3 $(0, -1, 1)$: $0 + (-1) \times 4 + 1 \times 9 = -4 + 9 = 5$ ✓.

What is this machine doing?

Look closely at the output:

OutputWhere it comes from
$1$the first input, $1$
$3$second minus first: $4 - 1$
$5$third minus second: $9 - 4$

In general, $A$ turns $(x_1, x_2, x_3)$ into $(x_1,\ x_2 - x_1,\ x_3 - x_2)$. It takes differences, so it is called the difference matrix.

Story: a savings jar

You check your savings jar every evening. The totals are $1$, $4$, $9$. How much did you add each day?

DayTotal in jarAdded that day
1$1$$1 - 0 = 1$
2$4$$4 - 1 = 3$
3$9$$9 - 4 = 5$

That is exactly what $A$ does: totals in, daily amounts out.

4. Running the machine backwards

Words used here

Output $b$ = what came out of the machine. Solve $Ax = b$ = find the input $x$ that gives that output. Running total = keep adding as you go. Sum matrix $S$ = the machine that makes running totals. Inverse $A^{-1}$ = the machine that undoes $A$. Derivative / integral = the calculus versions of differences / running totals.

Now reverse the question: you know the daily amounts $(1, 3, 5)$. What were the totals? Just keep adding:

  1. What we do Find the total after day 1.

    Why The jar started empty, so the day-1 total is just what was added on day 1.

    How $0 + 1 = 1$.

    What we have now Total after day 1 $= 1$.

  2. What we do Find the total after day 2.

    Why Yesterday's total plus what was added today.

    How $1 + 3 = 4$.

    What we have now Total after day 2 $= 4$.

  3. What we do Find the total after day 3.

    Why Again: yesterday's total plus today's amount.

    How $4 + 5 = 9$.

    What we have now Totals $(1, 4, 9)$.

    DayAdded that dayRunning total
    1$1$$1$
    2$3$$1 + 3 = 4$
    3$5$$1 + 3 + 5 = 9$

  4. Check in A $A$ takes differences: first entry $1$; $4 - 1 = 3$; $9 - 4 = 5$. Out comes $(1, 3, 5)$ ✓. We are back at $(1, 4, 9)$ from $(1, 3, 5)$.

The same thing with letters

If the output is $b = (b_1, b_2, b_3)$, the three equations are:

$$ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= b_1 \\ x_2 - x_1 &= b_2 \\ x_3 - x_2 &= b_3 \end{aligned} $$

Solve from the top, one line at a time:

  1. What we do Read $x_1$ off line 1.

    Why Line 1 already says $x_1 = b_1$. Nothing else is in it.

    What we have now $x_1 = b_1$.

  2. What we do Get $x_2$ alone in line 2: $x_2 - x_1 = b_2$.

    Why The $-x_1$ is in the way. Adding $x_1$ cancels it, since $-x_1 + x_1 = 0$. Whatever we add on the left we must add on the right, so the "$=$" stays true.

    How Add $x_1$ to both sides: $x_2 = b_2 + x_1$. Now put in $x_1 = b_1$ from step 1: $x_2 = b_2 + b_1$.

    What we have now $x_2 = b_1 + b_2$.

  3. What we do Get $x_3$ alone in line 3: $x_3 - x_2 = b_3$.

    Why Same trick: add $x_2$ to both sides to cancel the $-x_2$.

    How $x_3 = b_3 + x_2$. Put in $x_2 = b_1 + b_2$ from step 2: $x_3 = b_3 + b_1 + b_2$.

    What we have now $x_3 = b_1 + b_2 + b_3$.

  4. Check, line by line Line 1: $x_1 = b_1$ ✓. Line 2: $x_2 - x_1 = (b_1 + b_2) - b_1 = b_2$ ✓. Line 3: $x_3 - x_2 = (b_1 + b_2 + b_3) - (b_1 + b_2) = b_3$ ✓.

Each answer is a running total. The "adding up" machine is the matrix

$$ S = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad S \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ b_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_1 + b_2 \\ b_1 + b_2 + b_3 \end{bmatrix} $$
Definition: Inverse (the undo button)

The inverse of $A$, written $A^{-1}$, is the machine that undoes $A$. If $A$ turns $x$ into $b$, then $A^{-1}$ turns $b$ back into $x$.

Here $A^{-1} = S$: differences are undone by running totals.

Why A can be undone

At every step there was exactly one possible answer. Different inputs always give different outputs, so from any output you can tell exactly which input it came from.

In calculus, the same pair appears as the derivative (differences) and the integral (running totals), and one undoes the other.

5. A machine that cannot be undone

Words used here

Column = a stack of numbers going down in a matrix. Cyclic difference matrix $C$ = differences taken round a circle (the first one compares with the last). Inverse = undo button. No solution = no input gives that output. Many solutions = lots of inputs give the same output. Zero vector = all entries 0.

Change only the third column, from $w = (0, 0, 1)$ to $w^* = (-1, 0, 1)$:

$$ C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad C \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 - x_3 \\ x_2 - x_1 \\ x_3 - x_2 \end{bmatrix} $$

Every output is now a difference, going round in a circle: the first output compares $x_1$ with $x_3$.

Story: three friends at a round table

Friends 1, 2 and 3 sit in a circle with $x_1$, $x_2$, $x_3$ coins. Each friend only says how much more they have than the person before them (friend 1's "before" is friend 3, because it's a circle).

Say they have $5$, $7$ and $6$:

FriendCompares withSays
1 (has 5)friend 3 (has 6)$5 - 6 = -1$
2 (has 7)friend 1 (has 5)$7 - 5 = 2$
3 (has 6)friend 2 (has 7)$6 - 7 = -1$

That is $C(5, 7, 6) = (-1, 2, -1)$.

Problem 1: you cannot work out how much they have

Give everyone $10$ more, so they have $15, 17, 16$:

  1. What we do Friend 1 compares with friend 3.

    Why Row 1 of $C$ is $(1, 0, -1)$: "$x_1$ minus $x_3$".

    How $15 - 16 = -1$.

    What we have now First answer $-1$, same as before.

  2. What we do Friend 2 compares with friend 1.

    Why Row 2 of $C$ is $(-1, 1, 0)$: "$x_2$ minus $x_1$".

    How $17 - 15 = 2$.

    What we have now Second answer $2$, same as before.

  3. What we do Friend 3 compares with friend 2.

    Why Row 3 of $C$ is $(0, -1, 1)$: "$x_3$ minus $x_2$".

    How $16 - 17 = -1$.

    What we have now $C(15, 17, 16) = (-1, 2, -1)$, exactly what $C(5, 7, 6)$ gave.

$$ C \begin{bmatrix} 15 \\ 17 \\ 16 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 15 - 16 \\ 17 - 15 \\ 16 - 17 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Exactly the same answers. Hearing $(-1, 2, -1)$, you cannot tell whether they have $(5, 7, 6)$ or $(15, 17, 16)$ or anything else shifted up or down. The same happens with everyone equal: for $(1, 1, 1)$ the three differences are $1 - 1 = 0$, $1 - 1 = 0$, $1 - 1 = 0$.

$$ C \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = C \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

Two different inputs give the same output, so no undo button can exist. $C$ has no inverse.

Problem 2: some outputs are impossible

Add the three answers: $-1 + 2 = 1$, then $1 + (-1) = 0$. That is not luck. Add the three outputs in general:

  1. What we do Write the sum of the three outputs: $(x_1 - x_3) + (x_2 - x_1) + (x_3 - x_2)$.

    Why We want to know what the three answers always add up to, whatever the coins are.

    How Collect the $x_1$'s: $+x_1$ (first bracket) and $-x_1$ (second bracket) give $x_1 - x_1 = 0$.

    What we have now $x_1$ is gone.

  2. What we do Collect the $x_2$'s.

    How $+x_2$ (second bracket) and $-x_2$ (third bracket): $x_2 - x_2 = 0$.

    What we have now $x_2$ is gone too.

  3. What we do Collect the $x_3$'s.

    How $-x_3$ (first bracket) and $+x_3$ (third bracket): $-x_3 + x_3 = 0$.

    What we have now Everything cancels: the sum is $0 + 0 + 0 = 0$.

$$ (x_1 - x_3) + (x_2 - x_1) + (x_3 - x_2) = 0 $$

Every amount appears once with $+$ and once with $-$, so everything cancels. The three answers always add up to $0$.

So if someone claims the friends said $(1, 1, 1)$, that is impossible, because $1 + 1 + 1 = 3$, not $0$. The equation $Cx = (1, 1, 1)$ has no solution.

Rule for the C machine

$Cx = b$ can be solved only if $b_1 + b_2 + b_3 = 0$. When it can, there are many answers (you can always add the same amount to everyone).

Why A worked and C does not

With $A$, friend 1 says their actual amount ($x_1$), not a difference. That one real number pins everything down. With $C$, everyone only gives differences, so the actual amounts are lost.

6. Why: one column is a mix of the others

Words used here

Mix = multiply vectors by numbers, then add. Plane (flat sheet) = all mixes of two arrows pointing different ways. Independent = no vector is a mix of the others (each brings a new direction). Dependent = one vector is a mix of the others. Basis of 3D space = 3 independent vectors whose mixes reach every point. Inverse = undo button.

The real cause is in the columns. Is $w^*$ a mix of $u$ and $v$? Look for amounts $a$ and $b$ with $a\,u + b\,v = w^* = (-1, 0, 1)$:

  1. What we do Look at the top position.

    Why $u$ has a $1$ on top and $v$ has a $0$ on top, so only $a$ matters there. One unknown: easy.

    How Top: $a \times 1 + b \times 0 = -1$, so $a + 0 = -1$, so $a = -1$.

    What we have now $a = -1$.

  2. What we do Look at the bottom position.

    Why $u$ has a $0$ at the bottom and $v$ has $-1$, so only $b$ matters there.

    How Bottom: $a \times 0 + b \times (-1) = 1$, so $-b = 1$, so $b = -1$.

    What we have now $b = -1$.

  3. What we do Test the middle position with $a = -1$, $b = -1$.

    Why We used top and bottom to find $a$ and $b$. The middle is the real test: it must also come out right.

    How Middle: $a \times (-1) + b \times 1 = (-1)(-1) + (-1)(1) = 1 - 1 = 0$. And $w^*$ has $0$ in the middle ✓.

    What we have now $w^* = -u - v$.

  4. Check, every position $-1 \times u = (-1, 1, 0)$ and $-1 \times v = (0, -1, 1)$. Add:

    Position$-1 \times u$$-1 \times v$Add$w^*$
    top$-1$$0$$-1$$-1$ ✓
    middle$1$$-1$$0$$0$ ✓
    bottom$0$$1$$1$$1$ ✓

So $w^* = -u - v$. The third column of $C$ is made from the first two, so it adds no new direction. All three columns lie on the same flat sheet, and mixing them can never leave that sheet. That is why most outputs are unreachable.

The original $w = (0, 0, 1)$ is not a mix of $u$ and $v$ (see Example 5 in the Examples tab). It points off the sheet, which is why $A$ reaches everything.

Definition: Independent and dependent

Vectors are independent if none of them is a mix of the others. Each one brings a new direction. ($u, v, w$)

Vectors are dependent if one of them is a mix of the others. ($u, v, w^*$, because $w^* = -u - v$)

Definition: Basis

A basis of 3D space is 3 independent vectors. Mixing them reaches every point. $u, v, w$ is a basis; $u, v, w^*$ is not.

The big rule

For a square matrix, these always go together:

Columns independent⇔Mixes reach every point⇔Matrix has an undo button (inverse)

$A$ has all three. $C$ has none.

7. Subspaces

Words used here

Origin = the point $(0, 0, 0)$ where all axes meet; also called the zero vector. Mix = multiply by numbers and add. Line / plane = a straight stick / a flat sheet, going on forever. "Through the origin" = it passes through $(0, 0, 0)$. Subspace = a collection you can't escape by mixing.

Definition: Subspace

A subspace is a collection of vectors that you cannot escape by mixing. Multiply any of them by any number, or add any two, and you are still inside. It must contain the zero vector.

In plain words: a subspace is a flat line, sheet or space through the origin.

Subspaces of 3D spaceExample

Not subspaces
  • A line or plane that misses the origin. $0 \times$ any vector $= 0$ would be outside.
  • A piece of a plane, like a triangle. Doubling a vector jumps outside it.
In finance
  • $A$ turns an account balance each day into daily profit or loss.
  • $S$ turns daily profit or loss into the running total.
  • Like $C$: daily price changes alone never tell you the price level. Prices $(100, 103, 101)$ and $(50, 53, 51)$ have the same changes. You need one real starting price.

Worked examples

Throughout, $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ (differences), $S = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ (running sums) and $C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ (cyclic differences).

Example 1: Compute $Ax$ both ways

Problem

Find $A x$ for $x = (2, 5, 7)$.

Words used here

$A$ = the difference matrix; its columns are $u = (1, -1, 0)$, $v = (0, 1, -1)$, $w = (0, 0, 1)$. $Ax$ = mix the columns of $A$ using the numbers of $x$ as amounts. Difference = "this one minus the one before". Row = a line of numbers going across.

By columns.

  1. What we do Take 2 of column 1: $2 \times (1, -1, 0)$.

    Why The first number of $x$ is $2$.

    How $2 \times 1 = 2$, $2 \times (-1) = -2$, $2 \times 0 = 0$.

    What we have now $(2, -2, 0)$.

  2. What we do Take 5 of column 2: $5 \times (0, 1, -1)$.

    Why The second number of $x$ is $5$.

    How $5 \times 0 = 0$, $5 \times 1 = 5$, $5 \times (-1) = -5$.

    What we have now $(0, 5, -5)$.

  3. What we do Take 7 of column 3: $7 \times (0, 0, 1)$.

    Why The third number of $x$ is $7$.

    How $7 \times 0 = 0$, $7 \times 0 = 0$, $7 \times 1 = 7$.

    What we have now $(0, 0, 7)$.

  4. What we do Add the three pieces, position by position.

    How Top: $2 + 0 + 0 = 2$. Middle: $-2 + 5 = 3$, then $3 + 0 = 3$. Bottom: $0 + (-5) = -5$, then $-5 + 7 = 2$.

    $$ 2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + 5 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} + 7 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 + 0 + 0 \\ -2 + 5 + 0 \\ 0 - 5 + 7 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} $$

    What we have now $Ax = (2, 3, 2)$.

By differences (what $A$ does), as a check:

  1. What we do Use the rule $Ax = (x_1,\ x_2 - x_1,\ x_3 - x_2)$.

    Why This is what each row of $A$ does: row 1 $(1, 0, 0)$ keeps $x_1$; row 2 $(-1, 1, 0)$ gives $x_2 - x_1$; row 3 $(0, -1, 1)$ gives $x_3 - x_2$.

    How Row 1: $2$. Row 2: $5 - 2 = 3$. Row 3: $7 - 5 = 2$.

    What we have now $(2, 3, 2)$, the same as by columns ✓.

Answer: $Ax = (2, 3, 2)$

Example 2: Solve $Ax = b$ top-down

Problem

Solve $Ax = b$ for $b = (3, 1, 2)$.

Words used here

Solve $Ax = b$ = find the input $x$ that the difference machine $A$ turns into $b$. Equation = a sentence "left side $=$ right side". Running sum (running total) = keep adding as you go. Check = put the answer back into the original equations.

The equations. $x_1 = 3$,   $x_2 - x_1 = 1$,   $x_3 - x_2 = 2$.

  1. What we do Read $x_1$ off equation 1.

    Why Equation 1 already says $x_1 = 3$.

    What we have now $x_1 = 3$.

  2. What we do Solve equation 2, $x_2 - x_1 = 1$, for $x_2$.

    Why Adding $x_1$ to both sides cancels the $-x_1$ and keeps the "$=$" true.

    How $x_2 = 1 + x_1$. Put in $x_1 = 3$: $x_2 = 1 + 3 = 4$.

    What we have now $x_2 = 4$.

  3. What we do Solve equation 3, $x_3 - x_2 = 2$, for $x_3$.

    Why Same trick: add $x_2$ to both sides.

    How $x_3 = 2 + x_2$. Put in $x_2 = 4$: $x_3 = 2 + 4 = 6$.

    What we have now $x = (3, 4, 6)$.

  4. Check, row by row Equation 1: $x_1 = 3$ ✓. Equation 2: $x_2 - x_1 = 4 - 3 = 1$ ✓. Equation 3: $x_3 - x_2 = 6 - 4 = 2$ ✓. So $A(3, 4, 6) = (3, 1, 2)$ ✓.

$$ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= 3 \\ x_2 &= 1 + x_1 = 1 + 3 = 4 &&\text{add } x_1 \text{ to both sides} \\ x_3 &= 2 + x_2 = 2 + 4 = 6 &&\text{add } x_2 \text{ to both sides} \end{aligned} $$

Answer: $x = (3, 4, 6)$, the running sums of $(3, 1, 2)$.

Example 3: Use the inverse directly

Problem

Use $S = A^{-1}$ to solve $Ax = (2, 2, 2)$ in one step.

Words used here

Inverse $A^{-1}$ = the undo machine for $A$. Here it is $S$, the running-sum matrix: row 1 $(1, 0, 0)$ keeps the first number, row 2 $(1, 1, 0)$ adds the first two, row 3 $(1, 1, 1)$ adds all three. If $Ax = b$, then $x = Sb$.

Multiply by $S$ (running sums):

  1. What we do Row 1 of $S$ times $(2, 2, 2)$.

    Why Row 1 of $S$ is $(1, 0, 0)$: it keeps only the first number.

    How $1 \times 2 + 0 \times 2 + 0 \times 2 = 2$.

    What we have now $x_1 = 2$.

  2. What we do Row 2 of $S$ times $(2, 2, 2)$.

    Why Row 2 is $(1, 1, 0)$: add the first two numbers.

    How $2 + 2 = 4$.

    What we have now $x_2 = 4$.

  3. What we do Row 3 of $S$ times $(2, 2, 2)$.

    Why Row 3 is $(1, 1, 1)$: add all three.

    How $2 + 2 = 4$, then $4 + 2 = 6$.

    What we have now $x = (2, 4, 6)$.

$$ x = S \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 + 2 \\ 2 + 2 + 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check in $A$, row by row. Row 1: $2$ ✓. Row 2: $4 - 2 = 2$ ✓. Row 3: $6 - 4 = 2$ ✓. So $A(2, 4, 6) = (2, 2, 2)$ ✓.

Answer: $x = (2, 4, 6)$

Example 4: When can $Cx = b$ be solved?

Problem

Solve $Cx = b$ for (a) $b = (1, 2, -3)$ and (b) $b = (1, 1, 1)$, or show it is impossible.

Words used here

$C$ = cyclic difference matrix: $Cx = (x_1 - x_3,\ x_2 - x_1,\ x_3 - x_2)$, differences round a circle. Solvable = at least one $x$ works. Infinitely many solutions = endless $x$'s work. $c$ = any number you like. No solution = no $x$ can work.

The test. $Cx = b$ has a solution only if $b_1 + b_2 + b_3 = 0$ (the three outputs of $C$ always add to $0$, section 5).

  1. What we do Add the three numbers of (a) $b = (1, 2, -3)$.

    Why If they don't add to $0$, $C$ can never produce them.

    How $1 + 2 = 3$, then $3 + (-3) = 0$.

    What we have now Sum $0$: (a) passes the test.

  2. What we do Add the three numbers of (b) $b = (1, 1, 1)$.

    How $1 + 1 = 2$, then $2 + 1 = 3$.

    What we have now Sum $3 \ne 0$: (b) fails.

$b$$b_1 + b_2 + b_3$Solvable?
(a) $(1, 2, -3)$$1 + 2 - 3 = 0$yes
(b) $(1, 1, 1)$$1 + 1 + 1 = 3$no

(a) Find the solutions

The equations are $x_1 - x_3 = 1$, $\ x_2 - x_1 = 2$, $\ x_3 - x_2 = -3$. Remember the friends at the round table: giving everyone the same extra amount changes none of the differences.

  1. What we do Choose $x_1 = 0$.

    Why Only differences are known, so we are free to pick any starting value for friend 1. $0$ is the easiest.

    What we have now $x_1 = 0$.

  2. What we do Use equation 2, $x_2 - x_1 = 2$, to get $x_2$.

    Why It links $x_2$ to $x_1$, which we now know.

    How Add $x_1$ to both sides: $x_2 = 2 + x_1 = 2 + 0 = 2$.

    What we have now $x_2 = 2$.

  3. What we do Use equation 3, $x_3 - x_2 = -3$, to get $x_3$.

    How Add $x_2$ to both sides: $x_3 = -3 + x_2 = -3 + 2 = -1$.

    What we have now $x = (0, 2, -1)$.

  4. Check, all three equations Equation 1: $x_1 - x_3 = 0 - (-1) = 0 + 1 = 1$ ✓. Equation 2: $x_2 - x_1 = 2 - 0 = 2$ ✓. Equation 3: $x_3 - x_2 = -1 - 2 = -3$ ✓.

  5. What we do Add the same amount $c$ to everyone: $x = (0, 2, -1) + c(1, 1, 1)$.

    Why Adding $c$ to both numbers in a difference leaves the difference the same.

    How Try $c = 1$: $(0 + 1,\ 2 + 1,\ -1 + 1) = (1, 3, 0)$. Equation 1: $1 - 0 = 1$ ✓. Equation 2: $3 - 1 = 2$ ✓. Equation 3: $0 - 3 = -3$ ✓.

    What we have now Endless solutions, one for every $c$.

$$ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= 0 &&\text{our choice} \\ x_2 &= 2 + x_1 = 2 &&\text{from } x_2 - x_1 = 2 \\ x_3 &= -3 + x_2 = -1 &&\text{from } x_3 - x_2 = -3 \\ x_1 - x_3 &= 0 - (-1) = 1 \;✓ &&\text{first equation checks} \end{aligned} $$

(a) Infinitely many solutions: $x = (0, 2, -1) + c(1, 1, 1)$.

(b) Show it is impossible

  1. What we do Add the three left sides: $(x_1 - x_3) + (x_2 - x_1) + (x_3 - x_2)$.

    Why If all three equations were true, their sums would have to be equal too.

    How $x_1 - x_1 = 0$, $x_2 - x_2 = 0$, $-x_3 + x_3 = 0$.

    What we have now Left sides add to $0$.

  2. What we do Add the three right sides.

    How $1 + 1 = 2$, then $2 + 1 = 3$.

    What we have now That would need $0 = 3$, which is false for every $x$. No solution.

(b) No solution.

Example 5: Independent or dependent?

Problem

With $u = (1, -1, 0)$ and $v = (0, 1, -1)$: is (a) $w = (0, 0, 1)$ and (b) $w^* = (-1, 0, 1)$ a combination of $u$ and $v$?

Words used here

Combination (mix) = $x_1 u + x_2 v$: multiply by numbers $x_1, x_2$ and add. Independent = no vector is a mix of the others. Dependent = one vector is a mix of the others. Basis for $\mathbb{R}^3$ = 3 independent vectors reaching every point of 3D space. Plane = flat sheet.

General combination. $x_1 u + x_2 v = (x_1,\ -x_1 + x_2,\ -x_2)$. Match it with the target, position by position.

(a) Target $w = (0, 0, 1)$

  1. What we do Match the top: $x_1 = 0$.

    Why The top of the mix is $x_1$, the top of $w$ is $0$.

    What we have now $x_1 = 0$.

  2. What we do Match the middle: $-x_1 + x_2 = 0$.

    How Put in $x_1 = 0$: $-0 + x_2 = 0$, so $x_2 = 0$.

    What we have now $x_2 = 0$.

  3. What we do Match the bottom: $-x_2 = 1$.

    How Put in $x_2 = 0$: $-0 = 1$, i.e. $0 = 1$ ✗.

    What we have now False. The only amounts the top and middle allow break the bottom, so no mix of $u$ and $v$ equals $w$.

$$ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= 0 &&\text{top} \\ -x_1 + x_2 &= 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x_2 = 0 &&\text{middle} \\ -x_2 &= 1 \;\Rightarrow\; 0 = 1 \;✗ &&\text{bottom fails} \end{aligned} $$

(a) No, so $u, v, w$ are independent (a basis for $\mathbb{R}^3$).

(b) Target $w^* = (-1, 0, 1)$

  1. What we do Match the top: $x_1 = -1$.

    What we have now $x_1 = -1$.

  2. What we do Match the middle: $-x_1 + x_2 = 0$.

    How $-x_1 = -(-1) = 1$, so $1 + x_2 = 0$. Take 1 from both sides: $x_2 = -1$.

    What we have now $x_2 = -1$.

  3. What we do Match the bottom: $-x_2 = 1$.

    How $-x_2 = -(-1) = 1$. And the target bottom is $1$ ✓.

    What we have now All three positions work: $w^* = -u - v$.

  4. Check, every position $-u - v = (-1 - 0,\ 1 - 1,\ 0 - (-1)) = (-1, 0, 1) = w^*$ ✓.

$$ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= -1 &&\text{top} \\ -x_1 + x_2 &= 0 \;\Rightarrow\; 1 + x_2 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x_2 = -1 &&\text{middle} \\ -x_2 &= 1 \;✓ &&\text{bottom checks} \end{aligned} $$

(b) Yes: $w^* = -u - v$, so $u, v, w^*$ are dependent.

Shortcut. Add up the numbers in each vector. $u$: $1 + (-1) + 0 = 0$. $v$: $0 + 1 + (-1) = 0$. So every combination of $u$ and $v$ also adds to $0$. $w$: $0 + 0 + 1 = 1$ (not in the plane). $w^*$: $-1 + 0 + 1 = 0$ (in the plane).

Example 6: Subspace or not?

Problem

Which of these are subspaces?

  1. The line $y = 2x$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
  2. The line $y = 2x + 1$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
  3. The plane $b_1 + b_2 + b_3 = 0$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
  4. All vectors $(x, y)$ with $x \ge 0$ and $y \ge 0$.
Words used here

Subspace = a collection of vectors you cannot escape by multiplying by numbers or adding; it must hold the origin. Origin = the zero vector, $(0, 0)$ or $(0, 0, 0)$. $\mathbb{R}^2$ = all pairs of numbers (a flat floor). $\mathbb{R}^3$ = all triples (the whole room). $\ge$ = "greater than or equal to".

Two tests. (1) Does it contain the origin? (2) Do multiplying by any number and adding keep you inside?

  1. (a) Origin? Put $x = 0$: $y = 2 \times 0 = 0$. So $(0, 0)$ is on the line ✓.

    Stays inside? Take $(1, 2)$ and $(3, 6)$ (both have $y = 2x$). Add: $(4, 8)$, and $2 \times 4 = 8$ ✓. Times $-5$: $(-5, -10)$, and $2 \times (-5) = -10$ ✓. Always: if $y = 2x$ then $cy = 2(cx)$.

    What we have now Subspace (a line through $0$).

  2. (b) Origin? Put $x = 0$: $y = 2 \times 0 + 1 = 0 + 1 = 1$, not $0$. So $(0, 0)$ is not on it ✗.

    What we have now Not a subspace. No need for test 2.

  3. (c) Origin? $0 + 0 + 0 = 0$ ✓.

    Stays inside? Take $(1, 2, -3)$ (sum $0$). Times $2$: $(2, 4, -6)$, sum $2 + 4 - 6 = 0$ ✓. Add $(1, -1, 0)$ (sum $0$): $(2, 1, -3)$, sum $2 + 1 - 3 = 0$ ✓. Scaling multiplies the sum by the same number, and $c \times 0 = 0$; adding adds the sums, and $0 + 0 = 0$.

    What we have now Subspace (a plane through $0$).

  4. (d) Origin? $0 \ge 0$ and $0 \ge 0$ ✓.

    Stays inside? $(1, 1)$ is inside. Multiply by $-1$: $(-1, -1)$. But $-1 \ge 0$ is false ✗.

    What we have now Not a subspace: multiplying by a negative number escapes.

Contains $0$?Stays inside?Subspace?
(a) $y = 2x$yes: $0 = 2 \cdot 0$yes: multiples and sums of points with $y = 2x$ still have $y = 2x$yes (a line through $0$)
(b) $y = 2x + 1$no: $0 \ne 2 \cdot 0 + 1$—no
(c) $b_1 + b_2 + b_3 = 0$yes: $0 + 0 + 0 = 0$yes: scaling or adding keeps the sum $0$yes (a plane through $0$)

Example 7: Daily P&L and running totals (finance)

Problem

(a) A trader makes $+2$, $-1$, $+3$ on three days. What is the running total after each day?

(b) Another account starts at $0$ and ends the three days at $5$, $8$, $6$. What was each day's profit or loss?

Words used here

P&L = profit and loss: money made ($+$) or lost ($-$) on a day. Running total = the account balance after adding each day's P&L. Balance = how much is in the account at the end of a day. $S$ = sum matrix (daily P&L in, balances out). $A$ = difference matrix (balances in, daily P&L out).

Story first

Think of a piggy bank. Each day you put money in (or take some out). The daily amounts are the changes; the amount in the bank each evening is the running total. Running totals are $S$; daily changes are $A$.

(a) Running total = $S \times$ daily P&L.

  1. What we do Balance after day 1.

    Why Starting from $0$, day 1 adds $+2$.

    How $0 + 2 = 2$.

    What we have now $2$.

  2. What we do Balance after day 2.

    Why Yesterday's balance plus today's P&L of $-1$.

    How $2 + (-1) = 1$.

    What we have now $1$.

  3. What we do Balance after day 3.

    How $1 + 3 = 4$.

    What we have now Running totals $(2, 1, 4)$.

  4. Check with A Differences of $(2, 1, 4)$: first $2$ ✓; $1 - 2 = -1$ ✓; $4 - 1 = 3$ ✓. Back to the daily P&L $(2, -1, 3)$.

$$ S \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 - 1 \\ 2 - 1 + 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} $$

(a) Running totals: $2, 1, 4$.

(b) Daily P&L = $A \times$ end-of-day balances.

  1. What we do Day 1 P&L.

    Why The account started at $0$, so day 1's P&L is day 1's balance minus $0$.

    How $5 - 0 = 5$.

    What we have now $+5$.

  2. What we do Day 2 P&L.

    Why Today's balance minus yesterday's.

    How $8 - 5 = 3$.

    What we have now $+3$.

  3. What we do Day 3 P&L.

    How $6 - 8 = -2$.

    What we have now Daily P&L $(5, 3, -2)$: a loss of 2 on day 3.

  4. Check with S Running totals of $(5, 3, -2)$: $5$; $5 + 3 = 8$; $8 + (-2) = 6$. That is $(5, 8, 6)$, the original balances ✓. $S$ undoes $A$.

$$ A \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 8 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 8 - 5 \\ 6 - 8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 3 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} $$

(b) Daily P&L: $+5, +3, -2$.

Example 8: Building a put from a bond, a stock and a call (options)

Problem

Tomorrow the stock ends at 1 (low), 2 (middle) or 3 (high). Three assets are for sale today:

AssetPrice todayPays if low ($S=1$)Pays if middle ($S=2$)Pays if high ($S=3$)
Bond$1$$1$$1$$1$
Stock$2$$1$$2$$3$
Call, strike 2$0.25$$0$$0$$1$

A put option with strike 2 gives the right to sell the stock for $2$. What should it cost today?

Words used here

Payoff = what an asset pays tomorrow, in each state (low, middle, high). Bond = pays $1$ whatever happens. Stock = pays whatever the share price $S$ is. Call (like a Nifty CE) = the right to buy at the strike; pays $\max(S - 2, 0)$. Put (like a Nifty PE) = the right to sell at the strike; pays $\max(2 - S, 0)$. Strike = the fixed price on the option, here $2$. $\max(a, 0)$ = "$a$ if it is positive, otherwise $0$". Negative amount ($y = -1$) = sell instead of buy. Here $S$ means the share price, not the sum matrix.

Story first

Same idea as the tickets in Lecture 01, now with three kinds of weather instead of two. Each asset is a ticket that pays depending on tomorrow's "weather" (low, middle or high). We want a new ticket (the put), so we build it from the three tickets we can already buy, then add up their prices.

Step 1. What the put pays. You sell for $2$ only if the stock is worth less than $2$: payoff $= \max(2 - S,\ 0)$.

  1. What we do Put payoff in the low state, $S = 1$.

    Why You may sell a thing worth $1$ for $2$: you gain the gap.

    How $2 - 1 = 1$, and $\max(1, 0) = 1$.

    What we have now Low: put pays $1$.

  2. What we do Middle state, $S = 2$.

    How $2 - 2 = 0$, and $\max(0, 0) = 0$.

    What we have now Middle: put pays $0$.

  3. What we do High state, $S = 3$.

    Why Selling a thing worth $3$ for only $2$ would lose money, so you simply don't use the put.

    How $2 - 3 = -1$, and $\max(-1, 0) = 0$.

    What we have now Put payoff vector $(1, 0, 0)$.

    StateStock $S$$2 - S$Put pays
    Low$1$$1$$1$
    Middle$2$$0$$0$
    High$3$$-1$$0$ (don't use it)

Step 2. Column picture. Find $x$ bonds, $y$ stocks and $z$ calls with

$$ x \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{bond}} + y \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{stock}} + z \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{call}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{put}} \begin{matrix} \leftarrow \text{low} \\ \leftarrow \text{middle} \\ \leftarrow \text{high} \end{matrix} $$

Step 3. One equation per state. Read across each row: (amount of bonds × bond pays) + (stocks × stock pays) + (calls × call pays) = put pays.

$$ \begin{aligned} x + y &= 1 &&\text{(low)} \\ x + 2y &= 0 &&\text{(middle)} \\ x + 3y + z &= 0 &&\text{(high)} \end{aligned} $$

Step 4. Solve.

  1. What we do Take the low equation away from the middle equation.

    Why Both have exactly one $x$. Subtracting makes $x$ disappear, leaving only $y$.

    How Left sides: $(x + 2y) - (x + y) = x - x + 2y - y = 0 + y = y$. Right sides: $0 - 1 = -1$.

    What we have now $y = -1$: sell 1 stock.

  2. What we do Use the low equation, $x + y = 1$, to get $x$.

    Why It has only $x$ and $y$, and we now know $y$.

    How Take $y$ from both sides: $x = 1 - y$. Put in $y = -1$: $x = 1 - (-1) = 1 + 1 = 2$.

    What we have now $x = 2$: buy 2 bonds.

  3. What we do Use the high equation, $x + 3y + z = 0$, to get $z$.

    How Put in $x = 2$ and $y = -1$: $3y = 3 \times (-1) = -3$, so $2 + (-3) + z = 0$. $2 - 3 = -1$, so $-1 + z = 0$. Add $1$ to both sides: $z = 1$.

    What we have now $z = 1$: buy 1 call.

$$ \begin{aligned} (x + 2y) - (x + y) &= 0 - 1 &&\text{(middle)} - \text{(low)} \\ y &= -1 \\ x &= 1 - y = 1 - (-1) = 2 &&\text{from (low)} \\ z &= -x - 3y = -2 + 3 = 1 &&\text{from (high)} \end{aligned} $$

Step 5. Check in the original three equations, state by state.

  1. Low $x + y = 2 + (-1) = 1$ ✓ (put pays $1$).

  2. Middle $x + 2y = 2 + 2 \times (-1) = 2 - 2 = 0$ ✓ (put pays $0$).

  3. High $x + 3y + z = 2 + 3 \times (-1) + 1 = 2 - 3 + 1 = 0$ ✓ (put pays $0$).

State2 bonds$-1$ stock1 callTotalPut pays
Low$2$$-1$$0$$1$$1$ ✓
Middle$2$$-2$$0$$0$$0$ ✓
High$2$$-3$$1$$0$$0$ ✓

Recipe: buy 2 bonds, sell 1 stock ($y = -1$), buy 1 call.

Step 6. Price the put.

  1. What we do Add up today's cost of the recipe.

    Why The recipe pays exactly what the put pays in every state, so it must cost the same today. Otherwise one could buy the cheap one, sell the dear one, and pocket free money.

    How Bonds: $2 \times 1 = 2$. Stock: $(-1) \times 2 = -2$ (selling brings money in). Call: $1 \times 0.25 = 0.25$. Total: $2 - 2 = 0$, then $0 + 0.25 = 0.25$.

    What we have now Put price $= 0.25$.

    In market words Same as a Nifty PE priced from a CE, the index and cash: PE = cash − index + CE.

$$ \underbrace{2 \times 1}_{\text{bonds}} + \underbrace{(-1) \times 2}_{\text{stock}} + \underbrace{1 \times 0.25}_{\text{call}} = 2 - 2 + 0.25 = 0.25 $$

The put is worth $0.25$ today.

The link to this lecture

The payoffs of bond, stock and call are three independent vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$, so they form a basis: any payoff over the three states can be built from them, and the payoff matrix is invertible. If the third asset were just "2 stocks" with payoff $(2, 4, 6)$, the columns would be dependent and the put could not be built.

The recipe "put $=$ 2 bonds $-$ stock $+$ call" is called put–call parity.

  1. What we do Compare the first two. $2 \times (1, 2, 3) = (2 \times 1, 2 \times 2, 2 \times 3) = (2, 4, 6)$.

    What we have now The second is a combination of the first. They are dependent and fill only a plane.

5. Is the set of all vectors $(x, y, z)$ with $z = 1$ a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$?

No. It does not contain the origin $(0, 0, 0)$, since that has $z = 0$ and $0 \ne 1$. (It is a flat plane, but it misses the origin.)

6. Using $S$, find the running totals of daily P&L $(4, -2, -1)$.

  1. How Day 1: $4$. Day 2: $4 + (-2) = 2$. Day 3: $2 + (-1) = 1$.

    What we have now $S(4, -2, -1) = (4, 2, 1)$.

    Check with A $4$ ✓; $2 - 4 = -2$ ✓; $1 - 2 = -1$ ✓.
