1. The lecture in five questions

Words used here

$Ax = b$ = "mix the columns of $A$ with amounts $x$ to get exactly $b$". Solution = amounts that work. Particular solution = one working $x$. Nullspace = all $x$ with $Ax = 0$. Rank = number of pivots.

Lecture 08 solved $Ax = 0$ (target = nothing). This lecture solves $Ax = b$ for any target $b$, completely.

#QuestionShort answer
1Is there a solution at all?Only if $b$ passes the conditions found by elimination (zero rows must say $0 = 0$).
2How do I find one solution?Set all free variables to 0, solve for the pivot variables: the particular solution $x_p$.
3How do I find all solutions?$x = x_p + x_n$: one particular solution plus anything from the nullspace.
4What do the solutions look like?A flat piece (line, plane, …) shifted away from the origin. Not a subspace.
5How does the rank decide everything?Comparing $r$ with $m$ (rows) and $n$ (columns) tells you whether there are 0, 1 or infinitely many solutions.
The trading question behind this lecture

Columns = products (CE, PE, bond, future…), rows = Nifty scenarios, $b$ = the payoff a client wants. The lecture answers: Can I build the client's payoff? If yes, give me one recipe. Then give me every recipe. The rank tells you in advance whether the answer will be "impossible", "exactly one way" or "infinitely many ways".

2. The system and the augmented matrix

Words used here

Right side $b = (b_1, b_2, b_3)$ = the numbers each equation must equal. Augmented matrix $[A \mid b]$ = the matrix $A$ with $b$ stuck on as an extra column, so that whatever we do to the rows of $A$ we also do to $b$.

Same matrix as Lecture 08, but now with a general right side:

$$ \begin{aligned} x_1 + 2x_2 + 2x_3 + 2x_4 &= b_1 \\ 2x_1 + 4x_2 + 6x_3 + 8x_4 &= b_2 \\ 3x_1 + 6x_2 + 8x_3 + 10x_4 &= b_3 \end{aligned} \qquad [A \mid b] = \left[\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 2 & 2 & 2 & b_1 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 & 8 & b_2 \\ 3 & 6 & 8 & 10 & b_3 \end{array}\right] $$

Something to notice first: row 3 of $A$ is row 1 + row 2: $(1 + 2,\ 2 + 4,\ 2 + 6,\ 2 + 8) = (3, 6, 8, 10)$ ✓. So the left side of equation 3 is always the left side of equation 1 plus the left side of equation 2.

So we can predict the condition

If left 1 + left 2 = left 3, then the right sides must also match: $b_1 + b_2 = b_3$. Otherwise equation 3 contradicts equations 1 and 2.

$b$$b_1 + b_2$$b_3$Can we solve?
$(1, 5, 6)$$6$$6$yes (maybe)
$(1, 5, 17)$$6$$17$no: equations contradict each other

Why the augmented matrix? Every elimination step changes a whole equation, both sides. Writing $b$ as an extra column makes sure the right side gets exactly the same treatment. (In MATLAB: [A b].)

3. Elimination with the right side carried along

Words used here

Elimination = subtract a multiple of one row from a row below to make zeros. Pivot = the first non-zero number of a row, used to clear below it. Multiplier = (number to remove) ÷ (pivot). The last column ($b$) goes along for the ride: it gets the same subtraction.

We solve it like a child would: one small move at a time. Each step says what we do, why, how (the actual numbers), and what we have now.

  1. What we do Look at column 1. Pick the top number, the $1$ in row 1, as our leader (pivot).

    Why We want zeros under the leader. Then rows 2 and 3 no longer contain $x_1$, and a row with fewer unknowns is easier to solve.

    What we have now Leader $= 1$. Under it sit a $2$ (row 2) and a $3$ (row 3). Both must become $0$.

  2. What we do Turn the $2$ in row 2 into $0$.

    Why this amount Ask: "how many leaders make 2?" $2 \div 1 = 2$. So we take away 2 copies of row 1 from row 2.

    How First make 2 copies of row 1: $2 \times (1, 2, 2, 2 \mid b_1) = (2, 4, 4, 4 \mid 2b_1)$. Then take them away, box by box:

    col 1col 2col 3col 4right side
    Row 2$2$$4$$6$$8$$b_2$
    take away 2 × row 1$-2$$-4$$-4$$-4$$-2b_1$
    New row 2$2-2=\mathbf{0}$$4-4=\mathbf{0}$$6-4=\mathbf{2}$$8-4=\mathbf{4}$$\mathbf{b_2 - 2b_1}$

    Why b changes too Row 2 is a whole equation: left side $=$ right side. If we take $2$ copies of equation 1 away from the left side, we must take the same away from the right side, or the "$=$" stops being true. Equation 1's right side is $b_1$, so we take away $2b_1$.

    What we have now Row 2 $= (0, 0, 2, 4 \mid b_2 - 2b_1)$. The $x_1$ is gone from it. (Bonus: $x_2$ vanished too.)

  3. What we do Turn the $3$ in row 3 into $0$.

    Why this amount "How many leaders make 3?" $3 \div 1 = 3$. Take away 3 copies of row 1.

    How 3 copies of row 1: $3 \times (1, 2, 2, 2 \mid b_1) = (3, 6, 6, 6 \mid 3b_1)$. Take them away:

    col 1col 2col 3col 4right side
    Row 3$3$$6$$8$$10$$b_3$

    What we have now Column 1 is cleaned: $(1, 0, 0)$ going down.

  4. What we do Look for the next leader in row 2, column 2.

    What we see Row 2 has a $0$ there, and row 3 under it also has a $0$. There is no non-zero number to use.

    Why we skip A leader can't be $0$: we would have to divide by it. There's no row below with a non-zero number to swap in. So column 2 gets no leader, and $x_2$ will be a free variable.

    What we have now Move right to column 3. Row 2 has a $2$ there: that is leader number 2.

  5. What we do Turn the $2$ under it (row 3, column 3) into $0$.

    Why this amount "How many leaders make 2?" $2 \div 2 = 1$. Take away 1 copy of row 2.

    How

    col 1col 2col 3col 4right side
    Row 3$0$$0$$2$$4$$b_3 - 3b_1$
    take away 1 × row 2$0$$0$$-2$$-4$$-(b_2 - 2b_1)$
    New row 3$\mathbf{0}$$\mathbf{0}$$2-2=\mathbf{0}$$4-4=\mathbf{0}$see below

    Right side, slowly $b_3 - 3b_1 - (b_2 - 2b_1)$. Open the bracket: taking away "$-2b_1$" means adding $2b_1$, so it becomes $b_3 - 3b_1 - b_2 + 2b_1$. Put the $b_1$'s together: $-3b_1 + 2b_1 = -b_1$. Result: $\mathbf{b_3 - b_2 - b_1}$.

    What we have now The whole left side of row 3 is zeros.

  6. Final picture Leaders are boxed. The left part is the staircase $U$ from Lecture 08. The right column is now called $c$ ($b$ after the same moves).

    $$ [U \mid c] = \left[\begin{array}{cccc|c} \boxed{1} & 2 & 2 & 2 & b_1 \\ 0 & 0 & \boxed{2} & 4 & b_2 - 2b_1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & b_3 - b_2 - b_1 \end{array}\right] $$

4. When is there a solution?

Words used here

Zero row = a row of the left part that became all zeros. Solvable = at least one solution exists. Condition on $b$ = a rule $b$ must satisfy. Column space = every $b$ that can be written as a combination of the columns.

Look at the last row: $0x_1 + 0x_2 + 0x_3 + 0x_4 = b_3 - b_2 - b_1$, i.e.

$$ 0 = b_3 - b_2 - b_1 $$
IfThe last row saysResult
$b_3 - b_2 - b_1 = 0$$0 = 0$: always trueno contradiction: solvable
$b_3 - b_2 - b_1 \ne 0$$0 = $ something non-zero: impossibleno solution

Condition for solvability: $b_1 + b_2 = b_3$, exactly as predicted.

With numbers, step by step:

  1. What we do Try $b = (1, 5, 6)$: put $b_1 = 1$, $b_2 = 5$, $b_3 = 6$ into the right column $c$.

    How Top: $c_1 = b_1 = 1$. Middle: $c_2 = b_2 - 2b_1 = 5 - 2 \times 1 = 5 - 2 = 3$. Bottom: $c_3 = b_3 - b_2 - b_1 = 6 - 5 - 1 = 0$.

    What we have now The last row reads $0 = 0$. True. No fight between the equations: solvable ✓.

  2. What we do Try $b = (1, 5, 17)$.

    How Bottom: $c_3 = 17 - 5 - 1 = 11$.

    What we have now The last row reads $0x_1 + 0x_2 + 0x_3 + 0x_4 = 11$, i.e. $0 = 11$. Zero times anything is zero, so no choice of $x$ can make this true. No solution ✗.

Two ways to say the same condition

LanguageConditionWhy
Columns$b$ is in the column space $C(A)$$Ax$ is a combination of the columns; a solution exists exactly when $b$ is such a combination
Rowsif a combination of the rows of $A$ gives the zero row, the same combination of the entries of $b$ must give $0$row 1 + row 2 − row 3 = zero row, so $b_1 + b_2 - b_3$ must be $0$
Market meaning

If one scenario's payoffs are always the sum of two others' (for every product), then every buildable payoff has that property too. A client order that breaks the pattern (e.g. wants $b = (1, 5, 17)$) simply cannot be built from these products.

5. One particular solution

Words used here

Pivot variables = unknowns of pivot columns (here $x_1, x_3$), forced by the equations. Free variables = unknowns of columns without pivots (here $x_2, x_4$), which you may choose. Particular solution $x_p$ = the one solution you get by setting all free variables to 0. Back substitution = solve the bottom equation first, then go up.

Take the solvable right side $b = (1, 5, 6)$. After elimination, $c = (1, 3, 0)$, so the equations are

$$ \begin{aligned} x_1 + 2x_2 + 2x_3 + 2x_4 &= 1 \\ 2x_3 + 4x_4 &= 3 \\ 0 &= 0 \quad (\text{fine}) \end{aligned} $$
Recipe for a particular solution
  1. Check every zero row says $0 = 0$ (otherwise: no solution, stop).
  2. Set all free variables to 0.
  3. Solve for the pivot variables by back substitution.

  1. What we do Look at the zero row first.

    Why If it said $0 = 11$, there would be no solution and we would stop right here.

    What we have now It says $0 = 0$. Safe to go on.

  2. What we do Sort the unknowns into "leaders" and "free".

    How Leaders sit in columns 1 and 3, so $x_1$ and $x_3$ are pivot variables. Columns 2 and 4 have no leader, so $x_2$ and $x_4$ are free.

    Why Pivot variables are forced by the equations. Free variables are ours to choose.

  3. What we do Choose the free ones: $x_2 = 0$ and $x_4 = 0$.

    Why 0 It's the easiest number: anything times 0 is 0, so those terms simply disappear.

    How In row 1: $2x_2 = 2 \times 0 = 0$ and $2x_4 = 2 \times 0 = 0$ vanish, leaving $x_1 + 2x_3 = 1$. In row 2: $4x_4 = 4 \times 0 = 0$ vanishes, leaving $2x_3 = 3$.

    What we have now Two small equations: $x_1 + 2x_3 = 1$ and $2x_3 = 3$.

  4. What we do Solve the bottom one first: $2x_3 = 3$.

    Why bottom first It has only one unknown, so we can answer it straight away.

    How "2 times what is 3?" Split both sides into 2 equal halves: $x_3 = 3 \div 2 = \tfrac32 = 1.5$. Check: $2 \times 1.5 = 3$ ✓.

    What we have now $x_3 = \tfrac32$.

  5. What we do Go up to row 1 and use what we just found: $x_1 + 2x_3 = 1$.

    How Put $x_3 = \tfrac32$ in: $2 \times \tfrac32 = 3$, so $x_1 + 3 = 1$. "What plus 3 gives 1?" Take 3 away from both sides: $x_1 = 1 - 3 = -2$.

    What we have now $x_1 = -2$.

  6. What we do Put the four numbers together in order $x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4$.

    $$ x_p = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \\ \tfrac32 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad \begin{matrix} \leftarrow \text{found in step 5} \\ \leftarrow \text{chosen in step 3} \\ \leftarrow \text{found in step 4} \\ \leftarrow \text{chosen in step 3} \end{matrix} $$

Check in the original $A$ (always do this):

Row of $A$Row · $x_p$Should be
$(1, 2, 2, 2)$$1(-2) + 0 + 2(\tfrac32) + 0 = -2 + 3 = 1$$b_1 = 1$ ✓
$(2, 4, 6, 8)$$2(-2) + 0 + 6(\tfrac32) + 0 = -4 + 9 = 5$$b_2 = 5$ ✓
$(3, 6, 8, 10)$$3(-2) + 0 + 8(\tfrac32) + 0 = -6 + 12 = 6$$b_3 = 6$ ✓
Why set the free variables to 0?

Any values would work, but 0 is the easiest: the free columns disappear and only the pivot columns remain, which always have exactly one answer by back substitution. In trading terms: "use only the original products, none of the copies".

Shortcut with $R$: going on to reduced form, $[R \mid d] = \left[\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 2 & 0 & -2 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 & \tfrac32 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array}\right]$. With the free variables 0, the pivot variables are simply the right-side numbers: $x_1 = -2$, $x_3 = \tfrac32$ ✓.

6. The complete solution

Words used here

$x_p$ = one particular solution ($Ax_p = b$). $x_n$ = any vector in the nullspace ($Ax_n = 0$). Special solutions = the basic nullspace vectors (one free variable 1, the others 0). Complete solution = the collection of every solution.

The complete solution $$ x = x_p + x_n $$

Why it works: $A(x_p + x_n) = Ax_p + Ax_n = b + 0 = b$ ✓. Adding anything that produces zero doesn't change the result.

Why it catches every solution: if $x$ and $x_p$ both solve $Ax = b$, then $A(x - x_p) = b - b = 0$, so $x - x_p$ is in the nullspace. So $x = x_p + (\text{something in the nullspace})$.

  1. What we do Keep the one answer we found: $x_p = (-2, 0, \tfrac32, 0)$.

    Why It hits the target $b$ exactly. Everything else will be built on top of it.

  2. What we do Find the "makes-nothing" vectors (special solutions of $Ax = 0$), the same way as Lecture 08.

    How, first one Set $x_2 = 1$, $x_4 = 0$, right side $0$. Row 2: $2x_3 + 4 \times 0 = 0$, so $2x_3 = 0$, so $x_3 = 0$. Row 1: $x_1 + 2 \times 1 + 2 \times 0 + 2 \times 0 = 0$, so $x_1 + 2 = 0$, so $x_1 = -2$. Result $s_1 = (-2, 1, 0, 0)$.

    How, second one Set $x_2 = 0$, $x_4 = 1$. Row 2: $2x_3 + 4 \times 1 = 0$, so $2x_3 = -4$, so $x_3 = -2$. Row 1: $x_1 + 0 + 2 \times (-2) + 2 \times 1 = 0$, so $x_1 - 4 + 2 = 0$, so $x_1 - 2 = 0$, so $x_1 = 2$. Result $s_2 = (2, 0, -2, 1)$.

    What we have now Every "makes-nothing" vector is $c_1 s_1 + c_2 s_2$ for any numbers $c_1, c_2$.

  3. What we do Add them to $x_p$.

    Why Adding something that makes 0 to something that makes $b$ still makes $b + 0 = b$.

    $$ x = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \\ \tfrac32 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{x_p} + \underbrace{c_1 \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + c_2 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{x_n} $$

  4. What we do Test it: pick $c_1 = 1$, $c_2 = 0$ and build that solution.

    How Add box by box: $(-2 + (-2),\ 0 + 1,\ \tfrac32 + 0,\ 0 + 0) = (-4, 1, \tfrac32, 0)$.

    Check every row of $A$

    Row of $A$Multiply and addShould be
    $(1, 2, 2, 2)$$1(-4) + 2(1) + 2(\tfrac32) + 2(0) = -4 + 2 + 3 + 0 = 1$$1$ ✓
    $(2, 4, 6, 8)$$2(-4) + 4(1) + 6(\tfrac32) + 8(0) = -8 + 4 + 9 + 0 = 5$$5$ ✓
    $(3, 6, 8, 10)$$3(-4) + 6(1) + 8(\tfrac32) + 10(0) = -12 + 6 + 12 + 0 = 6$$6$ ✓

    What we have now A second, different answer that also works. Any $c_1, c_2$ gives another.

PickSolutionCheck row 1: $x_1 + 2x_2 + 2x_3 + 2x_4$ (should be 1)
$c_1 = 0, c_2 = 0$$(-2, 0, \tfrac32, 0)$$-2 + 0 + 3 + 0 = 1$ ✓
$c_1 = 1, c_2 = 0$$(-4, 1, \tfrac32, 0)$$-4 + 2 + 3 + 0 = 1$ ✓
$c_1 = 0, c_2 = 1$$(0, 0, -\tfrac12, 1)$$0 + 0 - 1 + 2 = 1$ ✓
Watch out: no constant in front of x_p

You may multiply $x_n$ by anything ($3x_n$ still gives 0). You may not multiply $x_p$: $A(3x_p) = 3b \ne b$. So it's exactly one $x_p$, plus any nullspace vector.

Market meaning

$x_p$ = one recipe that builds the client's payoff. $x_n$ = any "no profit, no loss" add-on. Every recipe = that one recipe + some zero-payoff trade. All recipes pay the same, so if they cost different amounts today, you can sell the dear one and buy the cheap one: arbitrage.

7. The picture: a shifted flat piece

Words used here

Subspace = a flat collection through the origin that you can't leave by adding or scaling. Origin = the zero vector. "Shifted" = moved away from the origin without tilting.

8. Rank limits: r ≤ m and r ≤ n

Words used here

$m$ = number of rows (equations). $n$ = number of columns (unknowns). Rank $r$ = number of pivots. Each pivot lives in its own row and its own column.

FactWhy
$r \le m$each row can hold at most one pivot, and there are only $m$ rows
$r \le n$each column can hold at most one pivot, and there are only $n$ columns

So the rank is at most the smaller of $m$ and $n$. Full rank means the rank is as big as it can be. There are three kinds of "full", plus "not full":

9. The four cases

Words used here

Full column rank: $r = n$ (a pivot in every column, no free variables). Full row rank: $r = m$ (a pivot in every row, no zero rows). Unique = at most one solution. $R$ = reduced row echelon form. $I$ = identity. $F$ = free part of $R$.

Case 1: full column rank, r = n (tall and thin)

ConsequenceWhy
no free variablesevery column has a pivot
nullspace $= \{0\}$ onlyno free variable to set to 1, so no special solutions
$x = x_p$: 0 or 1 solutionnothing to add from the nullspace; but zero rows may block some $b$
$R = \begin{bmatrix} I \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$identity on top, zero rows below

Example: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 6 & 1 \\ 5 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ ($4 \times 2$, rank 2). The two columns point in different directions, so both get pivots. Four equations, two unknowns: most $b$ fail. But $b = (4, 3, 7, 6) = $ column 1 + column 2 works, with the only solution $x = (1, 1)$.

Case 2: full row rank, r = m (short and wide)

ConsequenceWhy
no zero rowsevery row has a pivot
solvable for every $b$no zero row means no condition on $b$
$n - r = n - m$ free variablesthe columns left over after the $m$ pivots
infinitely many solutions (if $n > m$)$x_p$ + a whole nullspace
$R = \begin{bmatrix} I & F \end{bmatrix}$ (columns possibly mixed)identity in the pivot columns, free part elsewhere

Example: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 6 & 5 \\ 3 & 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ ($2 \times 4$, rank 2). Two pivots (columns 1, 2), two free variables. Every $b$ can be reached, in infinitely many ways.

Case 3: full rank, r = m = n (square, invertible)

ConsequenceWhy
$R = I$square with a pivot in every row and column
nullspace $= \{0\}$$r = n$
solvable for every $b$$r = m$
exactly one solution, for every $b$both at once: the matrix is invertible (Lecture 04)

Example: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$: row 2 − 3 × row 1 $= (0, -5)$, two pivots, $R = I$.

Case 4: r < m and r < n (not full in either direction)

ConsequenceWhy
zero rows exist$r < m$: some $b$ fail the condition
free variables exist$r < n$: a nullspace beyond 0
0 or infinitely many solutionseither $b$ fails, or $x_p$ + a whole nullspace
$R = \begin{bmatrix} I & F \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$identity, free part, zero rows

The lecture's main $3 \times 4$ matrix (rank 2) is this case: $b = (1, 5, 17)$ gives no solution; $b = (1, 5, 6)$ gives infinitely many.

$r = n < m$$\begin{bmatrix} I \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$0 or 1no redundant products, but not every payoff is buildable
$r = m < n$$\begin{bmatrix} I & F \end{bmatrix}$∞ (always at least 1)complete market: every payoff buildable, many recipes
$r < m, r < n$$\begin{bmatrix} I & F \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$0 or ∞some payoffs impossible; possible ones have many recipes

The rank tells you everything about the number of solutions. Only the actual numbers in the solutions need the full calculation.

In finance
  • Solvability = can this payoff be replicated? It needs $b$ in the column space: the client's order must obey the same patterns all products obey.
  • Particular solution = one hedge recipe; complete solution = every hedge recipe (one recipe + any zero-payoff trade). Different recipes costing different amounts = arbitrage.
  • Full row rank ($r = m$) = a complete market: every payoff over these scenarios can be built. Full column rank ($r = n$) = no redundant products: each buildable payoff has one recipe. Examples 1 and 7 work through both with Nifty options.

Example 1: Can the client's Nifty payoff be built, and in how many ways? (finance)

Problem

At expiry Nifty ends at 19,900, 20,000 or 20,100. Four products (payoffs in points ÷ 100, same market as Lecture 08):

Product19,90020,00020,100Price today
1. 19,800 CE$1$$2$$3$$205$
2. 20,200 PE$3$$2$$1$$195$
3. Bond$1$$1$$1$$99$
4. Future @ 20,000$-1$$0$$1$$0$

(a) Which client payoffs $b = (b_1, b_2, b_3)$ can be built? (b) Client A wants $(2, 3, 4)$ and client B wants $(0, 0, 1)$ (like a 20,000 CE). Which can you build? (c) For the buildable one, find one recipe, then every recipe, and compare costs.

Words used here

Column = one product's payoffs. Row = one scenario. $b$ = client's wanted payoff. Solvable = buildable. Particular solution = one recipe. Nullspace = zero-payoff trades. Complete solution = every recipe.

    How Top: $b_1 = 2$. Middle: $b_2 - 2b_1 = 3 - 4 = -1$. Bottom: $2 - 6 + 4 = 0$ ✓.

    Why "First try using only the options, no bonds, no futures." The zero terms vanish: $x_1 + 3x_2 = 2$ and $-4x_2 = -1$.

    What we have now CE lots $= \tfrac54 = 1.25$. So $x_p = (\tfrac54, \tfrac14, 0, 0)$: buy 1.25 CE + 0.25 PE.

Example 2: The lecture's system, complete solution

Problem

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 & 8 \\ 3 & 6 & 8 & 10 \end{bmatrix}$, solve $Ax = (2, 4, 6)$ completely, or show it's impossible.

  1. What we do Test the condition $b_1 + b_2 = b_3$ first.

    How $2 + 4 = 6$, and $b_3 = 6$. Equal ✓. There is a solution.

  2. What we do Carry $b$ through the same elimination steps as the notes.

    How Top: $2$. Middle: $b_2 - 2b_1 = 4 - 2 \times 2 = 0$. Bottom: $b_3 - b_2 - b_1 = 6 - 4 - 2 = 0$.

    What we have now $x_1 + 2x_2 + 2x_3 + 2x_4 = 2$ and $2x_3 + 4x_4 = 0$.

  3. What we do Free ones to 0: $x_2 = x_4 = 0$. Bottom: $2x_3 = 0$, so $x_3 = 0 \div 2 = 0$.

  4. What we do Go up: $x_1 + 2 \times 0 = 2$, so $x_1 = 2$. So $x_p = (2, 0, 0, 0)$.

    Check $2 \times$ column 1 $= 2 \times (1, 2, 3) = (2, 4, 6)$ ✓. It makes sense: $b$ is exactly twice column 1.

  5. What we do Add the nullspace (same $A$, so same special solutions as the notes).

$x = (2, 0, 0, 0) + c_1(-2, 1, 0, 0) + c_2(2, 0, -2, 1)$.

Example 3: Full column rank, 0 or 1 solution

Problem

$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 6 & 1 \\ 5 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Solve $Ax = (4, 3, 7, 6)$ and $Ax = (1, 0, 0, 0)$.

  1. What we do Leader $1$ at the top of column 1. Clear 2, 6, 5 under it.

    How Row 2 − 2 × row 1: $(2 - 2,\ 1 - 6) = (0, -5)$. Row 3 − 6 × row 1: $(6 - 6,\ 1 - 18) = (0, -17)$. Row 4 − 5 × row 1: $(5 - 5,\ 1 - 15) = (0, -14)$.

  2. What we do Leader $-5$ in row 2, column 2. Clear $-17$ and $-14$ under it.

    How $-17 \div -5 = \tfrac{17}{5}$ copies: $-17 - \tfrac{17}{5}(-5) = -17 + 17 = 0$. $-14 \div -5 = \tfrac{14}{5}$ copies: $-14 + 14 = 0$.

    What we have now 2 leaders, 2 columns: every column has a leader. Rank $2 = n$: full column rank, no free variables, nullspace only $\{0\}$. Rows 3 and 4 are zero rows, so some $b$ will fail.

  3. What we do Try $b = (4, 3, 7, 6)$. Guess: is it column 1 + column 2?

    How Box by box: $(1 + 3,\ 2 + 1,\ 6 + 1,\ 5 + 1) = (4, 3, 7, 6)$ ✓. So $x = (1, 1)$.

    Why only one No free variables, nothing to add: that's the only answer.

  4. What we do Try $b = (1, 0, 0, 0)$.

    How Rows 2 and 3 say $2x_1 + x_2 = 0$ and $6x_1 + x_2 = 0$. Take the first from the second: $4x_1 = 0$, so $x_1 = 0$. Then $2 \times 0 + x_2 = 0$, so $x_2 = 0$. Row 1 wants $x_1 + 3x_2 = 1$, but $0 + 0 = 0 \ne 1$ ✗.

$(4, 3, 7, 6)$: exactly one solution $(1, 1)$. $(1, 0, 0, 0)$: no solution.

Example 4: Full row rank, always solvable, infinitely many

Problem

$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 6 & 5 \\ 3 & 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Solve $Ax = (3, 4)$ completely.

  1. What we do Leader $1$ in row 1. Clear the $3$ under it: $3 \div 1 = 3$ copies of row 1 $= (3, 6, 18, 15 \mid 9)$.

    How Row 2 − that: $(3-3,\ 1-6,\ 1-18,\ 1-15 \mid 4-9) = (0, -5, -17, -14 \mid -5)$.

    What we have now Leaders in columns 1 and 2. Both rows have a leader, so no zero row and no condition on $b$. Columns 3, 4 are free.

  2. What we do Free ones to 0: $x_3 = x_4 = 0$. Bottom: $-5x_2 = -5$, so $x_2 = 1$.

  3. What we do Go up: $x_1 + 2 \times 1 = 3$, so $x_1 = 1$. $x_p = (1, 1, 0, 0)$.

    Check Row 1: $1 + 2 = 3$ ✓. Row 2: $3 + 1 = 4$ ✓.

  4. What we do Tidy to $R$. Make leader 2 into $1$: divide row 2 by $-5$: $(0, 1, \tfrac{17}{5}, \tfrac{14}{5})$.

    How Clear above it: row 1 − 2 × row 2 $= (1,\ 2 - 2,\ 6 - \tfrac{34}{5},\ 5 - \tfrac{28}{5}) = (1, 0, -\tfrac45, -\tfrac35)$. So $F = \begin{bmatrix} -\tfrac45 & -\tfrac35 \\ \tfrac{17}{5} & \tfrac{14}{5} \end{bmatrix}$.

  5. What we do Special solutions = $-F$ on top, $I$ below: $(\tfrac45, -\tfrac{17}{5}, 1, 0)$ and $(\tfrac35, -\tfrac{14}{5}, 0, 1)$.

    Check the first Row 1: $\tfrac45 + 2(-\tfrac{17}{5}) + 6 = \tfrac45 - \tfrac{34}{5} + \tfrac{30}{5} = 0$ ✓. Row 2: $\tfrac{12}{5} - \tfrac{17}{5} + 1 = -\tfrac55 + 1 = 0$ ✓.

$x = (1, 1, 0, 0) + c_1(\tfrac45, -\tfrac{17}{5}, 1, 0) + c_2(\tfrac35, -\tfrac{14}{5}, 0, 1)$.

Example 5: Square and invertible, exactly one

Problem

Solve $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} x = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$.

  1. What we do Leader $1$. Clear the $3$: take away 3 copies of row 1, $(3, 6 \mid 15)$.

    How $(3 - 3,\ 1 - 6 \mid 5 - 15) = (0, -5 \mid -10)$.

    What we have now A leader in every row and every column: $r = m = n = 2$. Exactly one answer is coming.

  2. What we do Bottom: $-5x_2 = -10$, so $x_2 = -10 \div -5 = 2$.

  3. What we do Go up: $x_1 + 2 \times 2 = 5$, so $x_1 + 4 = 5$, so $x_1 = 1$.

    Check Row 1: $1 + 4 = 5$ ✓. Row 2: $3 \times 1 + 2 = 5$ ✓.

$x = (1, 2)$: the only solution ($r = m = n = 2$, $R = I$).

Example 7: The four cases as four Nifty markets (finance)

Problem

Scenarios: Nifty at 19,900 / 20,000 / 20,100 (rows). For each set of products (columns), say what kind of market it is.

Words used here

Complete market = every payoff can be built ($r = m$). No redundant products = no product is a copy of others ($r = n$). Rank = number of truly different payoff directions.

Products$m \times n$, rankCaseMarket meaning

1. For the lecture's $A$, is $b = (2, 3, 5)$ solvable? Is $(1, 1, 1)$?

$(2, 3, 5)$: $2 + 3 = 5$ ✓ yes. $(1, 1, 1)$: $1 + 1 = 2 \ne 1$ ✗ no.

2. For the lecture's $A$ and $b = (2, 3, 5)$, find $x_p$.

$c = (2,\ 3 - 4,\ 0) = (2, -1, 0)$. $2x_3 = -1 \Rightarrow x_3 = -\tfrac12$; $x_1 + 2(-\tfrac12) = 2 \Rightarrow x_1 = 3$. $x_p = (3, 0, -\tfrac12, 0)$. Check row 2: $6 - 3 = 3$ ✓.

3. Why can't you write the complete solution as $c\,x_p + x_n$?

$A(c\,x_p) = c\,b$, which equals $b$ only when $c = 1$.

4. A $6 \times 4$ matrix has rank 4. Can $Ax = b$ have two different solutions?

No: full column rank means nullspace $\{0\}$, so at most one solution.

5. A $3 \times 7$ matrix has rank 3. Is $Ax = b$ always solvable? How many free variables?

Yes (full row rank). Free variables $7 - 3 = 4$, so infinitely many solutions.

6. Market: 3 scenarios, 3 products with rank 2. Can every client payoff be built?

No: $r < m$ means a zero row and a condition on $b$. Some payoffs are impossible; possible ones have infinitely many recipes (since $r < n$).
