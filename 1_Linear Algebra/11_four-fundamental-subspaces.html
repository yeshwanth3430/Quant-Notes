The Four Fundamental Subspaces

Every matrix carries four spaces: the column space, the nullspace, the row space and the left nullspace. Where they live, their dimensions ($r$, $n - r$, $r$, $m - r$), and how one elimination gives a basis for each.

1. Fixing the error from last lecture

Words used here

Independent = only the zero combination gives 0. Basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$ = 3 independent vectors. Invertible = square with a pivot in every row and column. Row space = all combinations of the rows.

In the lecture before, Strang wanted a third vector to join $(1,1,2)$ and $(2,2,5)$ as a basis for $\mathbb{R}^3$, and picked $(3,3,8)$. (Our Lecture 10 notes already caught this.) A student saw the mistake instantly, by looking at the rows:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 5 & 8 \end{bmatrix} \quad \begin{matrix} \leftarrow \text{row 1} \\ \leftarrow \text{row 2 = row 1!} \\ \phantom{x} \end{matrix} $$

  1. What we see Row 1 and row 2 are the same: $(1, 2, 3)$.

    So Row 1 − row 2 = zero row. Elimination produces a zero row, so there are at most 2 pivots. Rank $\le 2$.

  2. Why it matters for columns This lecture proves: number of independent rows = number of independent columns = $r$. Rank 2 means only 2 of the 3 columns are independent.

    What we have now The columns $(1,1,2), (2,2,5), (3,3,8)$ are dependent. Not a basis.

The lesson

The rows tell you about the columns. Sometimes dependence is hard to see in the columns and obvious in the rows (or the other way round).

2. The four subspaces

Words used here

Transpose $A^T$ = rows become columns. Column space = combinations of columns. Nullspace = solutions of "matrix times vector = 0".

#NameSymbolWhat is in itNew?
1Column space$C(A)$all combinations of the columns of $A$seen (Lecture 07)
2Nullspace$N(A)$all $x$ with $Ax = 0$seen (Lecture 07–08)
3Row space$C(A^T)$all combinations of the rows of $A$new
4Left nullspace$N(A^T)$all $y$ with $A^Ty = 0$new

Why write the row space as $C(A^T)$? We like vectors standing up as columns. Transposing turns the rows of $A$ into the columns of $A^T$. So "combinations of the rows of $A$" = "combinations of the columns of $A^T$" = $C(A^T)$. No new maths, just the rows standing up.

Why "left" nullspace? Transpose both sides of $A^Ty = 0$:

  1. Start $A^Ty = 0$ (a column of zeros).

  2. Transpose The transpose of a product flips the order: $(A^Ty)^T = y^T(A^T)^T$. And flipping twice gives back the original: $(A^T)^T = A$.

    What we have now $y^TA = 0^T$ (a row of zeros).

  3. In words The row $y^T$ multiplies $A$ from the left. $y^TA$ = $y_1$(row 1) + $y_2$(row 2) + … So $y$ is a recipe of rows that gives the zero row.

Mirror image

Nullspace: combinations of columns giving the zero column. Left nullspace: combinations of rows giving the zero row.

3. Where they live and their dimensions

Words used here

$\mathbb{R}^n$ = lists of $n$ numbers. $m$ = number of rows. $n$ = number of columns. Dimension = number of vectors in a basis. Rank $r$ = number of pivots.

Where each one lives: count how many numbers its vectors have.

SpaceIts vectors are…LengthLives in
$C(A)$columns of $A$ and their combinationsa column has $m$ entries (one per row)$\mathbb{R}^m$
$N(A)$$x$ in $Ax$: one number per column$n$$\mathbb{R}^n$
$C(A^T)$rows of $A$: one number per column$n$$\mathbb{R}^n$
$N(A^T)$$y$: one number per row$m$$\mathbb{R}^m$
The dimensions (the short answer)
SpaceDimensionCounts
row space $C(A^T)$$r$pivot rows
nullspace $N(A)$$n - r$free columns
column space $C(A)$$r$pivot columns
left nullspace $N(A^T)$$m - r$zero rows of $R$

In $\mathbb{R}^n$: $r + (n - r) = n$. In $\mathbb{R}^m$: $r + (m - r) = m$.

The wonderful fact: the row space and the column space have the same dimension $r$.

Why $m - r$ for the left nullspace? $A^T$ is just another matrix, of size $n \times m$. It has $m$ columns and the same rank $r$ (row rank = column rank). Its nullspace has dimension "columns − rank" $= m - r$, exactly like $N(A)$ has $n - r$.

4. Column space: basis

Words used here

Pivot columns = columns that get a leader during elimination. "Original $A$" = the matrix before any row moves.

We use one matrix for the whole lecture:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad m = 3,\ n = 4 $$

  1. What we do Leader $1$ in row 1. Clear column 1.

    How Row 2 − row 1: $(1-1,\ 1-2,\ 2-3,\ 1-1) = (0, -1, -1, 0)$. Row 3 − row 1: $(1-1,\ 2-2,\ 3-3,\ 1-1) = (0, 0, 0, 0)$.

    What we have now Leaders in columns 1 and 2 (the $-1$). Rank $r = 2$.

  2. Basis Columns 1 and 2 of the original $A$: $(1,1,1)$ and $(2,1,2)$.

    Dimension $2 = r$.

Row operations change the column space

After elimination the third entry of every column is 0. But $(1,1,1)$ is in $C(A)$ and has third entry 1. So $C(R) \ne C(A)$. Use the original columns; elimination only tells you which ones.

5. Row space: basis from R

Words used here

Row space = all combinations of the rows. $R$ = the tidiest form (pivots 1, zeros above and below). Row operation = subtract a multiple of a row, or multiply a row by a non-zero number.

Keep going from section 4 all the way to $R$.

  1. What we do Make the second leader a $1$: multiply row 2 by $-1$.

    How $-1 \times (0, -1, -1, 0) = (0, 1, 1, 0)$.

  2. What we do Clear the $2$ above it (row 1, column 2): row 1 − 2 × row 2.

    How $2 \times (0,1,1,0) = (0,2,2,0)$. Then $(1-0,\ 2-2,\ 3-2,\ 1-0) = (1, 0, 1, 1)$.

    What we have now

    $$ R = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} I & F \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

  3. Key claim $A$ and $R$ have the same row space.

    Why Every row move makes a new row out of old rows (like "row 2 − row 1"). So every row of $R$ is a combination of rows of $A$. And every move can be undone, so every row of $A$ is a combination of rows of $R$. Each set builds the other: same space.

  4. Basis The first $r$ rows of $R$: $(1, 0, 1, 1)$ and $(0, 1, 1, 0)$.

    Why independent Look at the first two entries: $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$. That identity part means $a(1,0,\dots) + b(0,1,\dots)$ starts with $(a, b)$, which is zero only when $a = b = 0$.

  5. Check: rebuild A's rows Row 1 of $A$: $1(1,0,1,1) + 2(0,1,1,0) = (1,\ 2,\ 1+2,\ 1) = (1,2,3,1)$ ✓. Row 2: $1(1,0,1,1) + 1(0,1,1,0) = (1,1,2,1)$ ✓. Row 3 = row 1 ✓.

    Trick The coefficients are just the first two entries of each row of $A$, because of the identity part.

Row space

Basis = the first $r$ rows of $R$ (not of $A$). Dimension $= r$. This is the cleanest basis: it starts with the identity.

6. Nullspace: basis

Words used here

Free variable = unknown of a column with no pivot. Special solution = one free variable 1, the others 0, solve for the pivots.

From $R$: pivots in columns 1, 2; free: $x_3, x_4$. $Rx = 0$ says $x_1 + x_3 + x_4 = 0$ and $x_2 + x_3 = 0$.

  1. First $x_3 = 1, x_4 = 0$: $x_2 + 1 = 0$, so $x_2 = -1$. $x_1 + 1 + 0 = 0$, so $x_1 = -1$. $s_1 = (-1, -1, 1, 0)$.

  2. Second $x_3 = 0, x_4 = 1$: $x_2 + 0 = 0$, so $x_2 = 0$. $x_1 + 0 + 1 = 0$, so $x_1 = -1$. $s_2 = (-1, 0, 0, 1)$.

  3. Check in A $s_1$: $-$col 1 $-$ col 2 $+$ col 3 $= (-1-2+3,\ -1-1+2,\ -1-2+3) = (0,0,0)$ ✓. $s_2$: $-$col 1 + col 4 $= (-1+1, -1+1, -1+1) = (0,0,0)$ ✓.

    What we have now Basis $s_1, s_2$. Dimension $2 = n - r = 4 - 2$.

7. Left nullspace: basis from E

Words used here

Left nullspace = recipes $y$ of rows that give the zero row. Gauss–Jordan = do every row move to $A$ and to $I$ side by side. $E$ = what $I$ turns into; it records the moves, so $EA = R$.

$R$ shows the row space and nullspace directly, but not the left nullspace. For that we need the record of the moves. Put $I$ next to $A$ and do every move to both:

$$ [\,A \mid I\,] \longrightarrow [\,R \mid E\,] \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad EA = R $$

  1. Start $I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

  2. Move 1 Row 2 − row 1: $(0-1,\ 1-0,\ 0-0) = (-1, 1, 0)$.

    Move 2 Row 3 − row 1: $(0-1,\ 0-0,\ 1-0) = (-1, 0, 1)$.

  3. Move 3 Row 2 × $(-1)$: $(1, -1, 0)$.

  4. Move 4 Row 1 − 2 × row 2: $(1 - 2,\ 0 - (-2),\ 0 - 0) = (-1, 2, 0)$.

    What we have now

    $$ E = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 & 0 \\ 1 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

  5. Check EA = R Each row of $E$ is a recipe of rows of $A$.

    Row of $E$RecipeResultRow of $R$
    $(-1, 2, 0)$$-(1,2,3,1) + 2(1,1,2,1)$$(-1+2,\ -2+2,\ -3+4,\ -1+2) = (1,0,1,1)$✓
    $(1, -1, 0)$$(1,2,3,1) - (1,1,2,1)$$(0, 1, 1, 0)$✓
    $(-1, 0, 1)$$-(1,2,3,1) + (1,2,3,1)$$(0,0,0,0)$✓ zero row

  6. Read the answer The last row of $E$ produced the zero row. So $y = (-1, 0, 1)$ is in the left nullspace: $-$row 1 + row 3 = 0.

    Check $A^Ty = 0$ $y$ dotted with each column of $A$: col 1 $(1,1,1)$: $-1 + 0 + 1 = 0$. col 2 $(2,1,2)$: $-2 + 0 + 2 = 0$. col 3 $(3,2,3)$: $-3+0+3 = 0$. col 4 $(1,1,1)$: $0$ ✓.

    Dimension $m - r = 3 - 2 = 1$. One vector is a basis.

Left nullspace

Basis = the last $m - r$ rows of $E$ (the rows of $E$ that produce the zero rows of $R$). Dimension $= m - r$.

Square invertible case: $R = I$, so $EA = I$ and $E = A^{-1}$. That was Gauss–Jordan in Lecture 04. No zero rows, so the left nullspace is $\{0\}$.

9. A new vector space: matrices

Words used here

Vector space = anything you can add and multiply by numbers, with the usual rules (there is a zero, adding zero changes nothing, etc.). Upper triangular = zeros below the diagonal. Symmetric = $A^T = A$. Diagonal = zeros off the diagonal. Intersection = what two spaces share.

Let $M$ = all $3 \times 3$ matrices. Treat each matrix as a "vector": we can add two matrices and multiply one by 7. The zero matrix plays the zero vector. (We ignore that matrices can also be multiplied together; a vector space doesn't need that.)

Subspace of $M$ExampleClosed? (add two, scale one: still the same kind?)
upper triangular $U$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 4 & 5 \\ 0 & 0 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$yes: $0 + 0 = 0$ and $7 \times 0 = 0$ below the diagonal
symmetric $S$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 5 \\ 3 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$yes: mirror pairs stay equal after adding / scaling
diagonal $D = U \cap S$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$yes

Why $U \cap S = D$: symmetric and zeros below the diagonal ⇒ the mirror entries above must be zero too ⇒ only the diagonal is left.

  1. What we do Find a basis for $D$ (diagonal matrices).

    How Take $E_{11}$ = a 1 in position (1,1), zeros elsewhere; $E_{22}$ and $E_{33}$ the same for positions (2,2) and (3,3).

  2. Span Any diagonal matrix with diagonal $a, b, c$ equals $aE_{11} + bE_{22} + cE_{33}$.

    Independent $aE_{11} + bE_{22} + cE_{33}$ has diagonal $(a, b, c)$; it is the zero matrix only if $a = b = c = 0$.

    What we have now Basis of 3 matrices: $\dim D = 3$.

Preview of next lecture: $\dim M = 9$ (one free entry per position), $\dim U = 6$ (the 6 entries on or above the diagonal are free), $\dim S = 6$ (choose the diagonal and the 3 entries above it; the ones below are copies).

10. The four subspaces in a market

Words used here

$A$: rows = $m$ Nifty scenarios, columns = $n$ products, entries = payoffs. $x$ = lots of each product. $b$ = a target payoff. State price $q_i$ = today's price of "1 unit paid only in scenario $i$". Price vector $p$ = today's price of each product.

SpaceLives inMarket meaning
column space $C(A)$scenario space $\mathbb{R}^m$every payoff you can build
nullspace $N(A)$lots space $\mathbb{R}^n$every zero-payoff position (redundant products); each must cost 0
row space $C(A^T)$lots space $\mathbb{R}^n$where fair price vectors live: $p = A^Tq$, i.e. price of product $j$ = $\sum_i q_i \times$ (payoff of $j$ in scenario $i$)
left nullspace $N(A^T)$scenario space $\mathbb{R}^m$the tests a payoff must pass to be buildable ($b$ buildable ⇔ $y \cdot b = 0$ for every $y$ here), and the freedom in state prices when the market is incomplete
Connections to earlier lectures
  • Lecture 09's solvability condition $b_1 + b_2 - b_3 = 0$ is exactly $y \cdot b = 0$ with $y = (1, 1, -1)$ in the left nullspace of that matrix (row 1 + row 2 − row 3 = zero row).
  • Lecture 08's arbitrage check ("a zero-payoff trade must cost 0") is exactly "the price vector must be in the row space". Example 1 shows both at once.
  • Complete market ($r = m$): left nullspace $= \{0\}$, every payoff buildable, state prices unique. Incomplete ($r < m$): some payoffs impossible, state prices not unique.

Worked examples

Example 1: All four subspaces of a Nifty market (finance, 3D)

Story

Same market as Lectures 08 and 09. Nifty expires at 19,900, 20,000 or 20,100. A desk has four products and wants to know: which payoffs can we build, which trades are copies, which prices are fair, and what test must a client's payoff pass?

4. Future @ 20,000$-1$$0$$1$$0$

(Payoffs in points ÷ 100.) Find a basis and the dimension of all four subspaces of $A$, and say what each means for the desk. Then test whether the prices are fair.

Which part of the maths means what

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 1 & -1 \\ 2 & 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{matrix} \leftarrow 19{,}900 \\ \leftarrow 20{,}000 \\ \leftarrow 20{,}100 \end{matrix} $$

Step A: Gauss–Jordan on [A | I]

  1. What we do Leader $1$ (row 1, column 1). Row 2 − 2 × row 1 and row 3 − 3 × row 1, on $A$ and on $I$.

    How, on A Row 2: $(2-2,\ 2-6,\ 1-2,\ 0+2) = (0, -4, -1, 2)$. Row 3: $(3-3,\ 1-9,\ 1-3,\ 1+3) = (0, -8, -2, 4)$.

    How, on I Row 2: $(0,1,0) - 2(1,0,0) = (-2, 1, 0)$. Row 3: $(0,0,1) - 3(1,0,0) = (-3, 0, 1)$.

  2. What we do Leader $-4$ (row 2, column 2). Clear the $-8$: $-8 \div -4 = 2$, so row 3 − 2 × row 2.

    How, on A $(0,\ -8+8,\ -2+2,\ 4-4) = (0,0,0,0)$.

    How, on I $(-3, 0, 1) - 2(-2, 1, 0) = (-3+4,\ 0-2,\ 1-0) = (1, -2, 1)$.

    What we have now Rank $r = 2$ (leaders in columns 1, 2). Row 3 of $A$ is gone: a zero row.

  3. What we do Tidy to $R$. Row 2 ÷ $(-4)$: $(0, 1, \tfrac14, -\tfrac12)$. Then row 1 − 3 × row 2: $(1,\ 3-3,\ 1-\tfrac34,\ -1+\tfrac32) = (1, 0, \tfrac14, \tfrac12)$.

    $$ R = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & \tfrac14 & \tfrac12 \\ 0 & 1 & \tfrac14 & -\tfrac12 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step B: the four subspaces, one by one

  1. Column space Pivot columns of $A$: CE $(1,2,3)$ and PE $(3,2,1)$. Dimension $r = 2$, a plane inside $\mathbb{R}^3$.

    Market Only 2 truly different payoff shapes. The desk can build only payoffs on this plane (straight lines in Nifty).

  2. Nullspace Free: bond, future. Special solutions from $R$: bond $= 1$ gives $(-\tfrac14, -\tfrac14, 1, 0)$; future $= 1$ gives $(-\tfrac12, \tfrac12, 0, 1)$. Dimension $n - r = 2$.

    Whole lots ×(−4): $(1, 1, -4, 0)$ = "CE + PE − 4 bonds". ×(−2): $(1, -1, 0, -2)$ = "CE − PE − 2 futures".

    Check CE + PE $= (1+3, 2+2, 3+1) = (4,4,4) = 4 \times$ bond ✓. CE − PE $= (-2, 0, 2) = 2 \times$ future ✓.

  3. Row space First 2 rows of $R$: $(1, 0, \tfrac14, \tfrac12)$ and $(0, 1, \tfrac14, -\tfrac12)$. Dimension $r = 2$, a plane inside $\mathbb{R}^4$.

  4. Left nullspace Last row of $E$: $y = (1, -2, 1)$. Dimension $m - r = 3 - 2 = 1$.

    Check $y^TA = 0$ CE: $1 - 4 + 3 = 0$. PE: $3 - 4 + 1 = 0$. Bond: $1 - 2 + 1 = 0$. Future: $-1 - 0 + 1 = 0$ ✓.

    Market "Payoff at 19,900 − 2 × payoff at 20,000 + payoff at 20,100" is the curvature (a butterfly's view). Every product here has zero curvature. So a payoff $b$ is buildable only if $b_1 - 2b_2 + b_3 = 0$: exactly Lecture 09's test.

  5. Dimensions add up In $\mathbb{R}^4$: $2 + 2 = 4$ ✓. In $\mathbb{R}^3$: $2 + 1 = 3$ ✓.

Step C: are today's prices fair? (row space test)

  1. What we do Try to write $p = (205, 195, 99, 0)$ as $a \times$ (row 1 of $R$) $+ b \times$ (row 2 of $R$).

    Why Fair prices come from state prices: $p = A^Tq$, a combination of the rows of $A$. So $p$ must be in the row space.

  2. How The identity part forces $a = 205$ (entry 1) and $b = 195$ (entry 2).

    Entry 3 (bond) Should be $\tfrac14(205) + \tfrac14(195) = \tfrac{400}{4} = 100$. Market says 99 ✗.

    Entry 4 (future) Should be $\tfrac12(205) - \tfrac12(195) = \tfrac{10}{2} = 5$. Market says 0 ✗.

  3. What it means $p$ is not in the row space, so no state prices explain it. Two leaks, one per nullspace direction: CE + PE − 4 bonds costs $205 + 195 - 396 = 4$ (should be 0); CE − PE − 2 futures costs $205 - 195 - 0 = 10$ (should be 0). Both are arbitrages.

  4. A fair market Pick state prices $q = (30, 40, 30)$. Then $p = A^Tq$: CE $= 1(30) + 2(40) + 3(30) = 30 + 80 + 90 = 200$; PE $= 90 + 80 + 30 = 200$; bond $= 30 + 40 + 30 = 100$; future $= -30 + 0 + 30 = 0$. Check: CE + PE − 4 bonds $= 400 - 400 = 0$ ✓; CE − PE − 2 futures $= 0$ ✓.

  5. State prices not unique Add any multiple of $y = (1,-2,1)$: $q = (31, 38, 31)$ gives CE $= 31 + 76 + 93 = 200$, PE $= 93 + 76 + 31 = 200$, bond $= 100$, future $= 0$. Same prices. The left nullspace is exactly this freedom: the market can't tell $q$ and $q + ty$ apart, because it is incomplete.

$\dim C(A) = 2$, $\dim N(A) = 2$, $\dim C(A^T) = 2$, $\dim N(A^T) = 1$. Buildable payoffs = zero-curvature ones; two copy trades; the quoted prices fail the row-space test (arbitrage of 4 and 10); state prices, when they exist, are not unique.

Example 2: A complete two-level market (finance, 2D)

Problem

Nifty ends at 19,900 or 20,100. Products: 20,000 PE $(1, 0)$ price 45, 20,000 CE $(0, 1)$ price 55, bond $(1, 1)$ price 99. Find the four subspaces and test the prices.

Words used here

$m = 2$ scenarios, $n = 3$ products. Complete market = rank equals the number of scenarios. State prices $q = (q_1, q_2)$ = price today of "1 unit only at 19,900" and "1 unit only at 20,100".

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{matrix} \leftarrow 19{,}900 \\ \leftarrow 20{,}100 \end{matrix} $$

  1. What we see $A$ is already $R$: leaders $1, 1$ in columns 1, 2. Rank $r = 2 = m$.

  2. Column space PE, CE: dimension 2 = all of $\mathbb{R}^2$. Every payoff can be built: complete.

    Left nullspace $m - r = 0$: only $\{0\}$. No test on $b$.

  3. Nullspace Free: bond. Bond $= 1$: $x_1 + 1 = 0$, $x_2 + 1 = 0$, so $(-1, -1, 1)$: bond − PE − CE pays 0. Dimension $3 - 2 = 1$.

    Row space $(1, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$. Dimension 2.

  4. State prices PE pays only at 19,900, so $q_1 = 45$. CE pays only at 20,100, so $q_2 = 55$. Unique (no left nullspace to add).

    Fair bond price $q_1 + q_2 = 45 + 55 = 100$. Market: 99 ✗.

    Trade Buy the bond (pay 99), sell PE + CE (receive 100). Keep 1 now; tomorrow the bond's 1 unit pays whichever option finishes in the money.

Complete market: $C(A) = \mathbb{R}^2$, $N(A^T) = \{0\}$, state prices unique $(45, 55)$, bond should be 100.

Problem

$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 5 & 8 \end{bmatrix}$ (columns $(1,1,2), (2,2,5), (3,3,8)$). Find all four subspaces.

  1. Clear column 1 Row 2 − row 1 $= (0,0,0)$. Row 3 − 2 × row 1 $= (2-2,\ 5-4,\ 8-6) = (0, 1, 2)$. On $I$: row 2 becomes $(-1, 1, 0)$, row 3 becomes $(-2, 0, 1)$.

  2. Swap Row 2 has 0 in the leader spot, row 3 has 1: swap rows 2 and 3 (on $I$'s side too).

    What we have now Rows $(1,2,3), (0,1,2), (0,0,0)$. $E$ so far: $(1,0,0), (-2,0,1), (-1,1,0)$.

Example 4: A rank-1 matrix

Problem

$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$. Find all four subspaces.

  1. Eliminate Row 2 − 3 × row 1 $= (3-3,\ 6-6) = (0, 0)$. On $I$: $(0,1) - 3(1,0) = (-3, 1)$. Rank 1.

All four are lines (dimension 1): $r = 1$, $n - r = 1$, $m - r = 1$.

Example 6: Is it a subspace of the 2×2 matrices?

Problem

In the space of all $2 \times 2$ matrices: (a) matrices with trace (sum of diagonal) 0; (b) invertible matrices.

  1. (a) Add two trace-0 matrices: traces add, $0 + 0 = 0$ ✓. Scale by 7: $7 \times 0 = 0$ ✓. Zero matrix has trace 0 ✓. Subspace. Basis $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$: dimension 3.

  2. (b) The zero matrix is not invertible, so it's missing. Not a subspace. (Also $I + (-I) = 0$.)

Practice problems

Answers are hidden. Click a problem to check your work.

1. $A$ is $7 \times 3$ with rank 3. Dimensions of the four subspaces?

Row space 3, nullspace 0, column space 3, left nullspace 4.

2. Why do row operations not change the row space?

Each new row is a combination of old rows, and each step can be undone, so each set of rows builds the other.

3. Find the left nullspace of $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \\ 3 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

Rank 1, so dimension $3 - 1 = 2$. Row 2 − 2 row 1 = 0 gives $(-2, 1, 0)$; row 3 − 3 row 1 = 0 gives $(-3, 0, 1)$.

4. Can the row space and column space be the same space?

Only if $m = n$ (they must live in the same $\mathbb{R}^n$). Example: any symmetric matrix, since then $A^T = A$.

5. Market: 4 scenarios, 3 products, rank 3. Complete? Are state prices unique?

Not complete ($r = 3 < 4$). Left nullspace has dimension 1, so state prices (if any) are not unique.

6. What is the dimension of all symmetric $2 \times 2$ matrices?

3: $\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{bmatrix}$ has three free numbers $a, b, c$.
